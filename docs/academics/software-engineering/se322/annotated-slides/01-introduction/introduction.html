Overview

Introduction to Software Design and Architecture

Chapter 1 sets up the vocabulary for the whole course. It starts from what software engineering is, shows that design is a kind of problem solving (and how designers get stuck), defines design as both a process and a product, lists the five challenges that make design hard, splits the design process into major and supporting activities, and ends with the eight design principles, two strategies and two considerations used to judge every design later on. Slides labelled “Extra” are the instructor’s own explanations and examples, and they are examinable like the rest.

Design is problem solving

Problems move through an initial, operational and goal state. Functional fixedness (seeing things only in their usual role) blocks solutions in the operational state.

Process and product

Design means the activity of building models (a project-management concern) and the models it produces (a technical concern).

Architecture vs. detailed design

Architecture is the black-box, macro view focused on quality; detailed design is the white-box view of each component’s internals, focused on function.

Eight principles

Modularization, abstraction, encapsulation, coupling, cohesion, separation of interface and implementation, completeness and sufficiency.

Slide 4 / 73

Software Engineering (revised)

Notes

  1. 1

    Software is the set of instructions that lets a user make the hardware do useful work: operating systems, word processors and browsers are all software.

  2. 2

    Engineering is a systematic process: designers generate and evaluate designs whose functions meet the clients’ objectives and the users’ needs while respecting constraints (cost, time, laws, hardware). The bridge example makes the point: you plan for weight and weather before you build, and software needs the same planning.

  3. 3

    Keep user and client apart. The user is whoever actually works with the software; the client is whoever pays for it. A hospital buys a system (client) that nurses use every day (users), and the two may want different things.

  4. 4

    The IEEE definition is the one to memorise word for word: software engineering is the application of a systematic, disciplined, and quantifiable approach to the development, operation, and maintenance of software, in short, applying engineering principles to software. IEEE is the professional body that publishes such standards.

Slide 5 / 73

Software Engineering (revised)

Notes

  1. 1

    Systematic means the work is organised as a coherent body of ideas and is methodical: there is a plan and a procedure, not improvisation.

  2. 2

    Disciplined means a set of rules governs the work, and the team actually follows them (coding standards, review steps, approval rules).

  3. 3

    Quantifiable means you can measure the deliverables: how many requirements are done, how many defects were found, how long a response takes. If you can’t measure it, you can’t tell whether it is improving.

  4. 4

    The Extra example: agile methods run in short iterations with regular checks, which is one practical way to be systematic, disciplined and measurable at the same time.

Slide 6 / 73

Software Engineering (revised)

Notes

  1. 1

    Requirements: find out what is needed, then analyse, specify and validate it. Everything later depends on this being right.

  2. 2

    Design turns the requirements specification into a software design, which has two levels: the architecture (major parts and how they connect) and the detailed design (the inside of each part). This course lives in this phase.

  3. 3

    Implementation writes the code, using the requirements, the architecture and the detailed design together.

  4. 4

    Testing checks that the software behaves correctly and meets the specified requirements.

  5. 5

    Maintenance changes the software after delivery: fixing faults, improving performance, or adapting it to a new environment. It is usually the longest and most expensive phase, which is why designs are judged by how easy they are to change.

  1. 1

    An expanded life cycle with an example for each phase. Requirements: identify and document needs, check feasibility and completeness (example: interviewing users). Testing (top right): unit, integration and system tests, often automated.

Slide 9 / 73

Engineering Problem-solving

Notes

  1. 1

    Problem solving in design passes through three states. Knowing which state you are in tells you what activity is appropriate.

  2. 2

    Initial state: the problem is formulated and interpreted. Often the hardest part is understanding the problem itself (what exactly does the client need?).

  3. 3

    Operational state: once the problem is understood, you think about it and possible solutions appear. This is where creativity and alternatives matter, and where functional fixedness (slide 11) does the most damage.

  4. 4

    Goal state: a solution has been identified, evaluated and validated. Problem solving ends here.

Slide 11 / 73

Engineering Problem-solving

Notes

  1. 1

    Most students assumed each line must begin and end on a dot. The requirements never said so; it was an assumption they brought with them.

  2. 2

    Functional fixedness: you only see an object (or a component, or a technique) in the role you are used to, so solutions that use it differently never occur to you. It is a limit of the designer’s thinking, not a property of the software.

  3. 3

    The remedy is deliberate: attempt the problem several times, from different viewpoints and unusual angles (“think outside the box”). The possible solution on the right only works because its lines leave the square.

  4. 4

    It matters most in the operational state, the state where alternative solutions are generated (slide 9).

Slide 13 / 73

Slide 13

Notes

  1. 1

    Interpreting the problem (initial state): understand what is being asked and classify it, for example as a known kind of problem with known solutions.

  2. 2

    Evaluating constraints (initial state): find the external limits (budget, deadlines, platforms, regulations) that set the bounds of the “solution landscape”. Note the link to slide 10: only real constraints belong here, not assumed ones.

  3. 3

    Collaborative brainstorming (operational state): the team proposes as many different solutions as it can before judging them.

  4. 4

    Synthesizing the possibilities (operational state): narrow the ideas down and choose one acceptable solution.

  5. 5

    Evaluating the solution (operational state): test the chosen idea. A flaw found here sends you back to brainstorming, so the framework is a loop, not a straight line.

  6. 6

    Implementing the solution happens in the goal state, once a suitable solution has been found.

Software Engineering Design

Notes

  1. 1

    The formal definition has two halves. (1) Design is a process: identifying and evaluating the architectural and detailed models needed to build software that meets its functional and non-functional requirements.

  2. 2

    (2) Design is also the product of that process: the design diagrams and documents it produces. When someone says “show me the design”, they mean the product; when they say “we are in design”, they mean the process.

Slide 15 / 73

Software Engineering Design

Notes

  1. 1

    Because the word covers both meanings, industry uses “design” for the process and the product interchangeably. Exam questions often ask which perspective a statement belongs to.

  2. 2

    Process perspective: the activities and tasks needed to model the software’s structure and behaviour before construction. This is mostly a project-management concern: the management team plans, organises and assigns the design tasks and lines up schedule and resources.

  3. 3

    Product perspective: the artifacts that design produces, such as class diagrams and sequence diagrams. This is mostly a technical concern: the technical team creates the actual models that define structure and behaviour.

Slide 17 / 73

Possible reasons

Notes

  1. 1

    First reason set: the project-management (process) perspective.

  2. 2

    Good design minimises the effect of requirements volatility. In a modular design a new feature, such as multi-language support, is added to one module without disturbing the others.

  3. 3

    It makes human resource allocation efficient: once components are defined and documented, the manager can give each one to a developer with the right skills.

  4. 4

    It protects the project from depending on “one guy” who owns the whole system in his head. If the design is written down, the project survives that person leaving.

  5. 5

    Overall, design improves the four management functions: planning, organisation, staffing and tracking.

Possible reasons

Notes

    Second reason set: the technical (product-development) perspective. The online-store example runs through all six points.

  2. 2

    Requirements are mapped to conceptual models: “product listing, cart, checkout” become user-interface diagrams and flowcharts the team can reason about.

  7. 7

    Design is the foundation for construction, testing and maintenance: the database schema and API design guide coders, testers and maintainers alike.

Slide 19 / 73

Major Software Design Challenges

Notes

  1. 1

    Requirements volatility: the requirements change while you build. A banking app that started simple must suddenly add advanced login security late in development.

  2. 2

    Inconsistent development processes: teams don’t follow the same design process. One team uses Agile, another Waterfall, and integration gets delayed.

  3. 3

    Fast, ever-changing technology: platforms and libraries change constantly; new iOS and Android versions force quick updates just to keep the app working.

  4. 4

    Managing design influences: stakeholders pull in different directions; executives want profit features while the security team wants stronger protection.

  5. 5

    Ethical and professional practices: protecting customer data and privacy even under deadline pressure.

Slide 20

Notes

  1. 1

    Requirements volatility is growth or change in the requirements during the project’s development life cycle. Some change is normal; the challenge is designing so it does not wreck the project.

  2. 2

Slide 21 / 73

Software Design Challenge #2 Inconsistent development processes

Notes

  1. 1

    In the design phase, the process is the set of activities that bridges the gap between requirements and construction.

  2. 2

    Examples of those activities: architectural and detailed design, design reviews, setting quality evaluation criteria, design change management and version control, and adopting design tools.

  3. 3

    The challenge is that in many companies this process is not well established, is poorly understood, focuses on one activity (say the user interface) while ignoring others, or is not done at all.

  4. 4

    The house analogy: one team follows a plan, another works without one, one builds doors before the walls exist. In software, Team A uses Agile, Team B uses Waterfall, nobody agreed on design rules, and the pieces don’t fit at integration time.

Slide 22 / 73

Software Design Challenge #3 The Technology

Notes

  1. 1

    The technology used to design and build software keeps evolving to offer new capabilities. AI and machine learning are today’s obvious example.

  2. 2

    The chatbot example shows the cost: before integrating an AI chatbot into customer service, developers must learn ML algorithms, data preparation and model training, and still make the bot work with existing customer databases.

  3. 3

    Designers are expected to understand and use new technologies all at the same time, which creates demand for designers who learn quickly.

  4. 4

    Why it is hard: learning takes time while the project deadline doesn’t move, and the new technology must interoperate with old legacy systems that can’t simply be replaced.

Slide 23 / 73

Software Design Challenge #4 Managing Design influences

Notes

  1. 1

    A project has many stakeholders (users, clients, managers, security, operations), and each has needs that pull the design their way: one wants performance, another usability, another security.

  2. 2

    Some of those needs conflict, and each stakeholder is convinced they are right. Stronger security can make the system slower or harder to use, for instance.

  3. 3

    The designer has to make trade-offs that satisfy each stakeholder as far as possible, without the decision harming the project as a whole.

  4. 4

    This is especially difficult in large-scale systems, where there are more stakeholders and every decision touches more parts.

Exam angle

Past exam · Midterm 252 · Q2 · 3 marksQ1

How could “managing design influences” be a challenge for software designers?

Show answer

Software projects have many stakeholders, each with specific wants and needs that influence the design. Some of these needs conflict with each other, and each stakeholder believes they are correct. Designers must therefore make design trade-offs to satisfy each customer, which is difficult in large-scale systems, because design decisions must accommodate all concerns without negatively affecting the project.

Slide 24 / 73

Slide 24

Notes

  1. 1

    Design decisions affect the environment and people who use the software: privacy, safety, accessibility, energy use. Designers are responsible for those effects.

  2. 2

    Designers must show strong ethical and professional practices so that the systems they produce are high quality, not just finished on time.

Slide 27 / 73

Software Design Process

Notes

  1. 1

    Software architecture is the macro-design approach: it creates models that show the quality and the function of the whole system, at the level of big parts rather than code.

  2. 2

    Its models are black boxes: each component is described by what it does and how it connects, not by its insides. That is enough to evaluate the system’s projected quality and functions early.

  3. 3

    It is designed from multiple perspectives (views), so stakeholders with different backgrounds can each check that their concerns are addressed. Chapter 2’s 4+1 views come from this idea.

  4. 4

    It defines the major structural components and interfaces of the system.

  5. 5

    It focuses on the quality aspects (performance, security, availability…) before detailed design or construction begins, because quality is hard to add later.

  6. 6

    It is a tool for communication, reasoning and analysis, and it lays the foundation for all later work in the life cycle.

Slide 28 / 73

Software Design Process

Notes

  1. 1

    Detailed design focuses on the internals of the components and interfaces that architecture identified.

  2. 2

    It begins after the architecture is specified, reviewed and judged complete enough. Starting detailed design on a shaky architecture means redoing it.

  3. 3

    It is a white-box approach: it opens each component and designs its internal structure and behaviour.

  4. 4

    It refines the architecture until the whole design (architecture + detailed design) is complete enough for construction to begin.

  5. 5

    Focus: detailed design is mostly about functional requirements (what the system does), while architecture is mostly about non-functional, quality requirements.

Extra: Abstraction vs. Encapsulation

Notes

  1. 1

    Purpose: abstraction hides unnecessary details and shows only the essential features; encapsulation hides the internal implementation and restricts direct access to data.

  2. 2

  2. 2

  3. 3
