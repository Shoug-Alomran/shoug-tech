L 01

What interaction design is

Definitions

  • Interaction design: designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives.
  • UX: how a product behaves and is used by people in the real world; you design for an experience, not the experience itself.

Usability goals

Effective · efficient · safe · having good utility · easy to learn (learnability) · easy to remember (memorability).

"E-E-S-U-L-M".

UX goals

Desirable (satisfying, enjoyable, fun, motivating, aesthetically pleasing) vs undesirable (boring, frustrating, annoying). Trade-offs between usability and UX goals are common.

L 2–3

Cognitive and emotional aspects

Cognitive processes

Attention · perception · memory · learning · reading, speaking and listening · problem solving, planning, reasoning, decision making.

  • Design implications: recognition over recall, chunk information (7 ± 2), reduce memory load, make important things salient.
  • Experiential vs reflective cognition.

Mental models and frameworks

  • Users' mental models guide interaction; designs should match them.
  • Norman's gulfs: gulf of execution (how do I do it?) and gulf of evaluation (did it work?).
  • Information processing, external cognition, distributed cognition, embodied interaction.

Emotional interaction

  • Norman's three levels: visceral (look and feel), behavioural (use), reflective (meaning, pride).
  • Expressive interfaces, annoying interfaces (unclear errors, pop-ups), persuasive technology, anthropomorphism (giving human qualities).
L 04

Interaction types and interfaces

Interaction types

Instructing (commands), conversing (dialogue, chatbots), manipulating (direct manipulation of objects), exploring (moving through virtual or physical spaces), responding (system initiates).

Interface types

Command line, GUI (WIMP), multimedia, VR/AR, web, mobile, voice, touch, gesture, haptic, wearables, tangible, brain-computer, smart interfaces.

Conceptual model

A high-level description of how the system is organized and operates: metaphors and analogies, concepts users are exposed to, relationships between them, mapping to the user's experience.

L 5–6

Design process and requirements

Four activities

  1. Discover requirements.
  2. Design alternatives.
  3. Prototype.
  4. Evaluate.

Iterative, with user involvement throughout.

User-centred design principles

Early focus on users and tasks · empirical measurement · iterative design.

Requirements

  • Functional, data, environment (physical, social, organizational, technical), user characteristics, usability and UX requirements.
  • Data gathering: interviews, questionnaires, observation, focus groups, studying documentation.
  • Capture with personas (rich fictional users), scenarios (stories of use), use cases, essential use cases, Volere shells.
L 08

Design principles and guidelines

Norman's principles

  • Visibility: functions can be seen.
  • Feedback: the result of an action is shown.
  • Constraints: limit possible actions (greyed-out menus).
  • Consistency: similar operations look and work alike.
  • Affordance: an attribute suggests how to use it (a button looks pressable).
  • Mapping: relationship between controls and effects (stove knobs laid out like burners).
Affordance = how it suggests use; mapping = how controls relate to their effects.

Laws and guidelines

  • Fitts's law: time to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with size (big, near buttons).
  • Hick's law: decision time grows with the number of choices.
  • Gestalt: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity.
  • Accessibility (WCAG): perceivable, operable, understandable, robust.
L 09

Prototyping and construction

Fidelity

  • Low fidelity: paper sketches, storyboards, card-based, Wizard of Oz. Cheap, fast, encourages changes, explores concepts.
  • High fidelity: looks and works like the product (Figma, code). Tests detailed usability, but costly and users comment on details.

Prototype types

  • Horizontal: many features, little depth. Vertical: few features in full depth.
  • Throwaway (evolutionary prototypes grow into the product).
  • Conceptual design → concrete design (icons, colours, layout) → construction.

Usability testing and experiments

  • Usability testing: users do typical tasks; measure time, errors, success, satisfaction; think-aloud; 5–12 users.
  • Experiments test a hypothesis: independent variable (what you change) and dependent variable (what you measure). Designs: between-subjects (different people per condition) vs within-subjects (same people, counterbalanced).
  • DECIDE framework: Determine goals, Explore questions, Choose approach, Identify practical issues, Decide on ethics, Evaluate and interpret.

Nielsen's 10 heuristics

  1. Visibility of system status
  2. Match between system and real world
  3. User control and freedom
  4. Consistency and standards
  5. Error prevention
  6. Recognition rather than recall
  7. Flexibility and efficiency of use
  8. Aesthetic and minimalist design
  9. Help users recognize, diagnose and recover from errors
  10. Help and documentation
Heuristic evaluation: 3–5 experts each inspect alone, then merge findings with severity ratings.

Walkthroughs

  • Cognitive walkthrough: experts step through a task asking: will users know what to do, see how, and understand the feedback? (learnability).
  • Pluralistic walkthrough: users, developers and experts step through together.
  • Formative evaluation (during design) vs summative (at the end).
