CH 1Project vs Program vs Portfolio vs Product vs Operations

What it is Key words to spot Time frame Example
Project A temporary endeavor to create a unique product, service, or result "temporary", "unique" Has a start and an end Building a mobile banking app

Strategic Management & Organizational Strategy

CH 2Strategic management, elements & TOWS matrix

Concept Explanation Example
Strategic Management Formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions to achieve organizational objectives. A university develops a strategy to become a leader in cybersecurity education.
Vision & Mission Establish what the organization wants to accomplish or become. A university aims to provide high-quality technology education.
Formulating, Implementing & Evaluating Develop strategies, put them into action, and evaluate results. Includes assessing strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. A university identifies demand for cybersecurity, launches a program, and evaluates enrollment.
Cross-functional Decisions Decisions involving multiple organizational departments. IT, Finance, and Academic Affairs collaborate on a university portal.
Achieving Objectives Ensuring activities and projects contribute to organizational goals. Developing an online learning platform to improve educational accessibility.
TOWS — SO (Maxi-Maxi) Use internal strengths to maximize external opportunities. A company uses its skilled developers to enter the growing AI market.
TOWS — ST (Maxi-Mini) Use strengths to minimize threats. A company uses its cybersecurity expertise to address increasing cyber threats.
TOWS — WO (Mini-Maxi) Minimize weaknesses by taking advantage of opportunities. A company trains employees in cloud computing to benefit from cloud adoption.
TOWS — WT (Mini-Mini) Minimize weaknesses and avoid threats. A company replaces outdated systems to reduce vulnerability to cyberattacks.

CH 2How projects reflect strategy

Organizational Strategy Project Explanation & Example
Technical or operating initiatives Construction or modernization Improve operations by upgrading factories or equipment.
Greater market penetration and acceptance New product development Develop a new product to attract customers and increase market share.
Streamlining and efficiency Reengineering Redesign an inefficient loan approval process to reduce time and costs.
Changing strategic direction or product portfolio New product lines A company enters the smartwatch market by introducing a new product category.
Creating strategic alliances Supplier/distributor negotiations Negotiate partnerships to reduce costs or improve product distribution.
Improving competitors' products Reverse engineering Examine a competitor's device to understand its functionality and develop an alternative.
Improving cross-organizational communication Enterprise IT Implement a system connecting suppliers, warehouses, and distributors.
Improving cross-functional coordination Concurrent engineering Design, development, and testing teams work simultaneously instead of sequentially.

Main connection: strategy defines what the organization wants to achieve; projects implement that strategy. The Airbus A380 case (slide 4) shows how strategic assumptions affect project selection: Airbus emphasized larger aircraft, while Boeing favored medium-sized wide-body aircraft.

Stakeholder Management

CH 2Stakeholder types, relationships & responsibilities

Stakeholder / Concept Explanation or Responsibility Example
Project Stakeholder An individual or group with an active stake in a project that can positively or negatively affect its development. Customers, employees, managers, or suppliers.
Stakeholder Analysis Identifies stakeholders and helps understand their interests, influence, and potential conflicts. Identifying that customers want faster delivery while Finance wants lower costs.
Internal Stakeholders Individuals or groups within the organization. Top management, accountants, functional managers, project team members.
Top Management Controls project managers and regulates their freedom of action. Management requires approval before increasing a project's budget.
Accountants Support and actively monitor project budgets. An accountant reviews project spending.
Functional Managers Provide employees temporarily assigned from functional departments. The IT manager assigns two developers to a project.
Project Team Performs the work required to complete the project. Developers, designers, and testers build a software system.
External Stakeholders Individuals or groups outside the organization. Clients, competitors, suppliers, intervenor groups.
Clients Want the project delivered quickly so their investment can generate returns. A company wants its e-commerce platform launched before a sales season.
Competitors Influence the external business environment. A competitor releases a similar product before the project's launch.
Suppliers Provide materials, equipment, or services. A cloud provider supplies hosting infrastructure.
Intervenor Groups External groups with the power to intervene in or disrupt projects. Environmental groups use legal action to delay construction.
Parent Organization Provides the organizational environment within which the project operates. A university establishes policies for its IT projects.

Main distinction: internal stakeholders belong to the organization; external stakeholders are outside it. Both can influence project success.

Organizational Structures

CH 2Organizational structure: elements & types

Concept Explanation Example
Organizational Structure Defines reporting relationships, employee groupings, and systems for coordination. A company organizes employees into IT, Finance, Marketing, and HR.
Formal Reporting Relationships Determines who reports to whom, hierarchy levels, and span of control. Developers report to a Development Manager, who reports to the CEO.
Departmental Groupings Organizes individuals into departments and departments into the overall organization. Developers belong to IT; accountants belong to Finance.
Communication & Coordination Systems Ensure departments communicate and integrate their efforts. IT and Finance coordinate the implementation of a payment system.
Functional Structure Groups employees performing similar activities into departments. All developers work in Development, regardless of the projects they support.
Project Structure Groups employees into dedicated project teams on temporary assignments. Developers, designers, and testers are assigned exclusively to Project Alpha.
Matrix Structure Creates a dual hierarchy combining functional and project authority. A developer reports to both a functional manager and a project manager.

CH 2All organizational structures: strengths, weaknesses & examples

Structure Strengths — explanation & example Weaknesses — explanation & example
Functional 1. No organizational disruption: existing departments can handle projects without restructuring. Example: IT develops an application using its current employees. 1. Functional siloing: departments resist collaboration. Example: Development ignores Marketing's input.
2. In-depth knowledge and intellectual capital: employees develop specialized expertise. Example: developers continuously improve their programming skills. 2. Lack of customer focus: departments prioritize internal goals over customer needs. Example: developers ignore usability complaints.
3. Standard career paths: clear advancement opportunities. Example: Junior Developer → Senior Developer → Development Manager. 3. Longer completion time: slow communication and structural problems delay projects. Example: waiting for another department's approval.
4. Varying commitment: departments prioritize projects differently. Example: IT considers a project urgent, while Marketing does not.
Project 1. Sole project manager authority: one manager controls project decisions. Example: the PM directly assigns tasks and priorities. 1. Expensive: dedicated teams require significant resources. Example: two projects each maintain separate testing teams.
2. Improved communication: dedicated team members communicate directly. Example: developers and testers work closely together. 2. Project loyalty over firm loyalty: employees identify more strongly with their project. Example: team members prioritize Project Alpha over organizational needs.
3. Effective decision-making: clear authority enables faster decisions. Example: the PM approves changes without multiple departmental approvals. 3. Difficulty maintaining intellectual capital: knowledge may leave with temporary staff. Example: contractors leave after completing the project.
4. Project management expertise: managing dedicated projects develops experienced PMs. Example: a manager becomes skilled after leading several projects. 4. Uncertain future: employees worry about what happens after the project. Example: a developer does not know whether another assignment is available.
5. Rapid market response: dedicated teams act quickly on opportunities. Example: launching a product before competitors.
Matrix 1. Dynamic environments: resources can be adjusted as needs change. Example: developers are reassigned between projects. 1. Two bosses: employees report through dual hierarchies. Example: a developer receives instructions from both the functional and project managers.
2. Equal emphasis on projects and functions: balances project objectives with functional efficiency. Example: developers support projects while keeping departmental expertise. 2. Resource negotiation: managers must negotiate resource sharing. Example: two projects request the same database specialist.
3. Cross-functional coordination: encourages departments to collaborate. Example: IT, Finance, and Marketing work together. 3. Competing demands: employees face conflicting project and departmental responsibilities. Example: a PM requests testing while a functional manager assigns maintenance work.
4. Maximizes scarce resources: specialists can support multiple projects. Example: one cybersecurity expert works across three projects.

Project Management Offices (PMOs)

CH 2PMO definition, responsibilities, levels, forms & models

Category Concept Explanation Example
Definition Project Management Office (PMO) Centralized unit that oversees or improves project management. A university establishes a PMO to support its technology projects.
Responsibilities Technical Support Provides technical project management assistance. Helping create project schedules.
Expertise Provides specialized project management knowledge. Advising project managers on planning techniques.
Repository Stores project information, templates, and resources. Maintaining project reports and lessons learned.
Center for Excellence Improves project management practices. Providing standardized methods and training.
Administrative Support Assists with scheduling, resource allocation, monitoring, and control. Preparing progress reports and tracking resources.
Levels Level 1 — Project Provides direct support to an individual project. A project office supports Project Alpha.
Level 2 — Business Unit Supports projects within a specific business unit. An IT PMO supports all IT department projects.
Level 3 — Corporate Provides organization-wide support. A corporate PMO supports IT, Finance, and Operations projects.
Forms — Control Supportive Low control: provides consultation, resources, and training. A PMO offers optional templates and training.
Controlling Moderate control: requires compliance with standards and procedures. All projects must use standardized reports.
Directive High control: directly manages projects. The PMO assigns PMs and manages project execution.
Models — Purpose Weather Station Monitors and tracks project status without directly controlling projects. Collecting cost, schedule, and progress reports.
Control Tower Improves project management methodology and helps PMs apply it. Establishing project management practices and guiding teams.
Resource Pool Maintains and supplies skilled project professionals. Assigning qualified PMs to projects.

Classification Question it answers Categories
PMO Levels Where does the PMO operate? Level 1, Level 2, Level 3

These classifications are independent. A corporate-level PMO, for example, can operate as a supportive PMO.

Organizational Culture

CH 2Culture: definition, characteristics, factors & effects

Category Concept Explanation Example
Definition Organizational Culture Unwritten rules and norms that guide organizational behavior. Employees are expected to collaborate even without a written policy.
Characteristics Unwritten Cultural expectations are often not formally documented. Employees know they should help colleagues.
Rules of Behavior Norms guide how employees respond to situations. Employees report mistakes rather than hide them.
Shared by Members Norms may exist within certain groups or the entire organization. Development encourages experimentation while Finance emphasizes caution.
Taught to New Members New employees learn culture by observing existing employees. A new developer learns the team's communication practices.
Development Factors Technology Technology influences how employees work and interact. Collaboration tools encourage digital communication.
Environment External conditions influence organizational behavior. A competitive industry encourages rapid decisions.
Geographical Location Location influences workplace norms and practices. International offices may have different communication practices.
Reward Systems Rewards encourage specific behaviors. Bonuses for teamwork promote collaboration.
Rules & Procedures Formal policies influence behavior. Strict approvals encourage cautious decisions.
Key Organizational Members Influential people shape organizational values. A CEO encourages innovation and experimentation.
Critical Incidents Important events reveal the organization's actual culture. A major system failure shows whether employees are blamed or supported.
Effects on PM Departmental Interaction Cooperative cultures improve collaboration between departments and projects. IT and Marketing coordinate to complete a project.
Employee Commitment to Goals Culture influences employee dedication and willingness to contribute. Team members work extra hours to meet an important deadline.
Project Planning Culture affects estimation honesty and planning accuracy. In a trusting environment, developers provide realistic time estimates.
Performance Evaluation Culture influences how performance and risk-taking are judged. A risk-averse company rewards cautious decisions rather than experimentation.

CH 3Five types of influence

Type Explanation Example Recognition keyword

Team Development Stages & Objectives

CH 3Tuckman's five stages of team development

Stage Explanation Team behavior Example
1. Forming Team members come together, get to know each other, and begin forming a group. Polite interactions, uncertainty, learning responsibilities. Students meet their software project teammates for the first time.
2. Storming Members compete for leadership, influence, or control, and test group processes. Disagreements, competition, confusion about authority. Two developers disagree about who should lead the technical work.
3. Norming The team reaches agreement about how it will operate. Established rules, accepted roles, improved cooperation. Members agree on coding standards, meetings, and task allocation.

Building Trust, Managing Conflict & Commitment

CH 3Building and maintaining trust

Principle Explanation Example
Trust is the foundation of teamwork Teams need trust to collaborate, share ideas, and resolve conflicts. A developer openly reports a mistake because they trust their teammates.
Vulnerability-based trust Members feel comfortable admitting mistakes, asking for help, and saying they do not know something. A junior developer admits they need assistance with database security.
Trust takes time Trust develops through consistent behavior, shared experiences, and fulfilled commitments. A PM repeatedly delivers on promises, gradually earning team confidence.
Trust must be maintained Trust can deteriorate if people become unreliable or dishonest. A PM communicates openly when a milestone cannot be met.
Behavioral profiling Tools such as Myers-Briggs can help members discuss strengths, weaknesses, and working preferences. A quiet teammate receives opportunities to contribute through written discussions.
Open communication Communication barriers create project risks and weaken trust. The PM shares schedule changes with the entire team.
Admit and correct mistakes Leaders should take responsibility and explain how they will avoid repeating errors. A PM acknowledges an incorrect estimate and revises the plan.
Lead by example Demonstrate expected behavior instead of relying only on authority. A PM follows the same deadlines and reporting rules as the team.
Fulfill commitments Complete promises on time or provide advance notice and an explanation. A developer warns the team early that an assigned task will be late.
Friendship vs. leadership Good relationships are valuable, but leaders still need boundaries and accountability. A PM addresses a friend's missed deadline professionally.

CH 3Healthy conflict vs. harmful conflict

Concept Explanation Example
Healthy Conflict Constructive, honest debate about issues, supported by trust. Developers challenge each other's architectural proposals using technical evidence.
Disagreement Different opinions that can still be discussed and resolved constructively. Two developers prefer different database technologies but remain open to discussion.
Conflict Positions become rigid and difficult to reconcile. Two developers refuse to cooperate because each insists their solution is the only acceptable one.
Conflict Norms Rules of engagement defining acceptable tone, language, timing, and emotional expression. The team agrees to criticize ideas rather than attack individuals.
Groupthink A group prioritizes harmony and agreement over critical evaluation. Everyone accepts a flawed design because nobody wants to disagree with the leader.
Suppressing Dissent Preventing or discouraging disagreement and criticism. A PM refuses to hear concerns about a risky release.
Neutrality Members outside the conflict should remain neutral even when they personally agree with one side. A team leader mediates without openly supporting either developer.
Conciliator A neutral person who helps conflicting parties communicate and resolve differences. A PM mediates a dispute between two developers.
Reconciliation Restoring cooperation between conflicting parties. Two developers agree on a technical evaluation process and resume working together.

CH 3Six conflict resolution techniques

Technique Explanation Example
Confronting / Problem Solving Address the root cause directly through open, honest discussion. Investigate why developers disagree about a database choice and evaluate technical requirements.
Compromising Reach a middle ground through mutual concessions. Two teams agree to divide a shared specialist's time between their projects.
Withdrawal / Avoidance Delay, sidestep, or temporarily avoid the conflict. A PM postpones a minor disagreement until after an urgent production issue is resolved.
Smoothing / Accommodating Preserve harmony by emphasizing agreement and downplaying differences. A PM reminds team members of their shared goal instead of focusing on a minor disagreement.
Collaborating Combine multiple perspectives to develop a creative solution. Developers combine useful features from two competing architecture proposals.
Forcing Impose a solution through authority or pressure. During a critical incident, the PM requires the team to follow a particular recovery plan.

Important distinction: compromising involves concessions, while collaborating seeks a solution that integrates different perspectives. Confronting focuses on addressing the underlying problem directly.

CH 3Achieving commitment, accountability & results

Concept Explanation Example
Clarity Everyone understands what was decided, why it matters, and what happens next. The PM confirms the selected database, responsible developer, and implementation deadline.
Buy-in Members support a decision even if they initially disagreed with it. A developer supports PostgreSQL after the team selects it, despite originally preferring MongoDB.
Consensus vs. Buy-in Buy-in does not require unanimous agreement; members can disagree and still commit. The team chooses one architecture, and all members support its implementation.
Peer Accountability Team members hold each other responsible instead of relying only on the PM. A tester reminds a developer about an agreed deadline.
Leader Accountability Leaders demonstrate transparency, fulfill commitments, and address underperformance. A PM discusses missed sprint commitments and improvement actions.
Focusing on Results Team outcomes take priority over individual or departmental interests. Developers prioritize delivering a reliable system rather than competing for personal recognition.
Visible Goals Desired results should be publicly clarified and kept visible. A project dashboard displays the team's milestones and delivery objectives.
