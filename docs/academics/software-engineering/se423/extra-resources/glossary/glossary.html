Ch 1

Introduction, Value Delivery and the 12 Principles

36 terms
A
Adaptability and resiliency (principle)
Embrace adaptability (responding to change) and resiliency (absorbing impacts and recovering quickly).
B
Benefit
A gain realized by the organization and its beneficiaries from the project's outcomes.
C
Change (principle)
Enable change to achieve the envisioned future state.
Complexity (principle)
Navigate complexity.
D
Deliverable
Any unique, verifiable product, result or capability a project must produce.
E
Enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)
Conditions not under the project team's control that influence or constrain the project.
External environment
Factors outside the organization: marketplace, social and cultural influences, regulations, commercial databases, academic research, industry standards.
F
Formal project management
A disciplined approach that brings better resource control, customer relations, shorter development, lower cost, higher quality, profit, coordination and morale.
I
Information flow
In the value delivery system, leadership shares strategy downward while projects and operations send performance information upward.
Internal environment
Factors inside the organization that affect a project: process assets, governance documents, data assets, knowledge, security and culture.
L
Leadership (principle)
Demonstrate leadership behaviors; leadership can come from anyone on the team.
O
Operations
Ongoing work that sustains the organization, as opposed to temporary projects.
Organizational governance
The structured way an organization provides direction and control through policies, processes and decision-making.
Organizational process assets (OPAs)
Plans, processes, policies, procedures and knowledge bases specific to the organization that the project can use.
Outcome
An end result or consequence of a process or project, such as a benefit.
P
PMI Code of Ethics
Four values for project professionals: responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty.
Portfolio
Projects, programs, subsidiary portfolios and operations managed as a group to achieve strategic objectives.
Product
An artifact that is produced, is quantifiable and can be an end item itself or a component.
Product life cycle
The stages a product passes through: introduction, growth, maturity and decline (retirement).
Product management
Managing a product across its whole life cycle (introduction, growth, maturity, decline/retirement); it may start programs or projects at any point.
Program
A group of related projects, subsidiary programs and activities managed in a coordinated way to obtain benefits not available from managing them separately.
Project
A temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service or result.
Project governance
The framework, functions and processes that guide project management activities to deliver value and meet goals.
Project management
Applying knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet the project's requirements.
Project management office (PMO)
A management structure that standardizes project-related governance processes and shares resources, methods, tools and techniques.
Project manager
The person assigned by the performing organization to lead the team responsible for achieving the project objectives.
Q
Quality (principle)
Build quality into processes and deliverables.
R
Risk (principle)
Optimize risk responses.
S
Stakeholders (principle)
Effectively engage with stakeholders.
Stewardship (principle)
Be a diligent, respectful and caring steward: act with integrity, care, trustworthiness and compliance.
System for value delivery
Portfolios, programs, projects, products and operations working together so that deliverables lead to outcomes, benefits and value.
Systems thinking (principle)
Recognize, evaluate and respond to system interactions.
T
Tailoring (principle)
Tailor the approach based on context.
Team (principle)
Create a collaborative project team environment.
V
Value
The worth, importance or usefulness of something; different stakeholders see value differently.
Value (principle)
Focus on value: continually check that the project delivers the intended benefits.
Ch 1.2

Project Performance Domains

48 terms
A
Adaptive approach
An agile approach for high uncertainty and volatility, working in short iterations (often 1–2 weeks).
Ambiguity
A state of being unclear; conceptual (people interpret the same term differently) or situational (several outcomes are possible).
C
Centralized management
Accountability for outcomes is held by one person (the PM) or a small group.
Complexity
A characteristic of a project that makes it hard to manage because of human behavior, system behavior and ambiguity.
Contingency reserve
Time or money for identified risks (known unknowns); part of the cost baseline and controlled by the PM.
Cost baseline
The approved, time-phased budget, including contingency reserve but excluding management reserve.
D
Definition of done (DoD)
A checklist of criteria a deliverable must meet before it is considered complete and ready for use.
Delivery cadence
The timing and frequency of deliveries: single, multiple, periodic or continuous.
Delivery performance domain
Delivering the scope and quality the project was undertaken to achieve.
Development approach and life cycle domain
Choosing the development approach, delivery cadence and life cycle phases.
Discretionary dependency
A dependency chosen from best practice (preferred or soft logic).
Distributed management
Management and leadership shared across the team, as in self-organizing teams.
E
Emotional intelligence
Recognizing and managing your own and others' emotions: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and social skill.
Explicit knowledge
Knowledge that can be written down and codified, such as words, numbers and pictures.
External dependency
A dependency on something outside the project, such as a vendor or regulator.
H
High-performing team factors
Open communication, shared understanding, shared ownership, trust, collaboration, adaptability, resilience, empowerment and recognition.
Hybrid approach
A mix of predictive and adaptive elements, used when some parts are certain and others are not.
I
Incremental development
Delivering the product in successive usable pieces that add functionality.
Information radiator
A visible display (physical or electronic) that gives the team and stakeholders up-to-date project information.
Internal dependency
A dependency between activities within the project.
Iterative development
Refining the product through repeated cycles until it is correct.
K
Key performance indicator (KPI)
A metric used to evaluate how successful a project is.
L
Lagging indicator
A metric that measures results after the fact, such as final cost.
Leadership styles
Autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, transactional and servant, chosen to fit the situation.
Leading indicator
A metric that predicts future changes or trends, such as the rate of change requests.
M
Management reserve
Budget for unknown events and unplanned in-scope work (unknown unknowns); added on top of the cost baseline.
Mandatory dependency
A dependency required by contract or by the nature of the work (hard logic).
Measurement performance domain
Assessing performance and taking action to keep it acceptable.
N
Net promoter score (NPS)
A stakeholder metric measuring how likely people are to recommend the product.
O
Opportunity strategies
Responses to positive risks: exploit, escalate, share, enhance, accept.
P
Performance domain
A group of related activities critical to delivering project outcomes. There are eight.
Planning performance domain
Organizing and coordinating the work: scope, WBS, estimates, schedule, budget, procurement.
Predictive approach
A waterfall approach used when requirements are known and fixed up front; plans are made early.
Project budget
Cost baseline plus management reserve.
Project work performance domain
Establishing processes, managing resources and fostering a learning environment.
R
Request for information (RFI)
A bid document asking sellers for more information about goods and services.
Request for proposal (RFP)
A bid document asking sellers for proposals on how they would meet a complex need.
Request for quote (RFQ)
A bid document asking sellers for price quotes on a defined need.
S
Servant leadership
Leading by serving the team: removing obstacles, shielding from diversions, encouraging and developing members.
SMART criteria
Metrics should be specific, meaningful, achievable, relevant and timely.
Stakeholder performance domain
Activities for building productive working relationships with stakeholders.
T
Tacit knowledge
Personal knowledge that is hard to express, such as experience, insight and know-how.
Team performance domain
Activities for the people who produce deliverables: culture, leadership, high performance.
Threat strategies
Responses to negative risks: avoid, escalate, transfer, mitigate, accept.
U
Uncertainty
A lack of understanding and awareness of issues, events, paths or solutions.
Uncertainty performance domain
Dealing with risk, ambiguity, complexity and volatility.
V
Volatility
The possibility of rapid, unpredictable change; handled with alternatives analysis and reserves.
W
Work breakdown structure (WBS)
A hierarchical decomposition of the total scope of work into manageable pieces.
Ch 1.3

Tailoring, Models, Methods and Artifacts

23 terms
A
ADKAR model
A change model: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement.
Adoption (tailoring check)
Whether the team can and does actually use the process.
Artifact
A template, document, output or project deliverable.
C
Coherence (tailoring check)
Whether roles, cadence, governance, methods and tools reinforce one another.
Compliance (tailoring check)
Whether mandatory policy, legal and regulatory obligations are met.
Copy-paste process (tailoring pitfall)
Reusing another project's process without adapting it to the current context.
Cynefin framework
A complexity model that classifies situations as obvious, complicated, complex, chaotic or disorder.
D
Drexler/Sibbet team performance model
A seven-stage model of how teams form and perform.
H
Hygiene and motivational factors
Herzberg's model: hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators such as achievement create satisfaction.
K
Kotter's 8 steps
A model for leading organizational change, from creating urgency to anchoring the change.
M
Method
The means for achieving an outcome, output, result or deliverable.
Model
A thinking strategy that explains a process, framework or phenomenon.
O
OSCAR model
A coaching model: outcome, situation, choices and consequences, actions, review.
P
Process tailoring
Adding, modifying, removing, blending or aligning processes.
S
Salience model
A stakeholder model based on power, urgency and legitimacy.
Situational Leadership II
A leadership model that matches style (directing, coaching, supporting, delegating) to the follower's competence and commitment.
Stacey matrix
A complexity model based on agreement on requirements and certainty of technology.
Sufficiency (tailoring check)
Whether the evidence or detail is adequate for its audience and consequence.
T
Tailoring
Deliberately adapting the approach, governance and processes to suit the environment and the work at hand.
Tailoring process
Four steps: select the initial development approach, tailor for the organization, tailor for the project, implement ongoing improvement.
Theory X, Y, Z
Motivation models: X assumes people avoid work, Y assumes they are self-motivated, Z stresses long-term security and involvement.
Tuckman ladder
A team development model: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.
W
What to tailor
Life cycle and development approach, processes, engagement, tools, and methods and artifacts.
Ch 2

The Organizational Context

27 terms
C
Control tower (PMO model)
A PMO that sets project management standards and enforces them.
Controlling PMO
A PMO with moderate control that requires compliance with frameworks and methods.
Culture effects on projects
Departmental interaction, employee commitment to goals, project planning, performance evaluation.
Culture factors
Technology, environment, geographic location, reward systems, rules and procedures, key organizational members.
D
Directive PMO
A PMO with high control that directly manages projects.
E
External stakeholders
Stakeholders outside the organization: clients, competitors, suppliers, intervenor groups.
F
Functional structure
Grouping people by function (engineering, finance); no disruption to the firm, but silos and slow responses.
H
Heavyweight project organization
A dedicated, autonomous project team with full authority, often in a separate location.
I
Internal stakeholders
Stakeholders inside the organization: top management, accountants, functional managers, project team members.
Intervenor group
An external group, such as environmental or regulatory bodies, that can step in and affect a project.
M
Matrix structure
Staff report to both a functional manager and a project manager; efficient, but two bosses and conflict.
O
Organizational culture
The unwritten rules of behavior, beliefs and values shared by an organization's members.
Organizational structure
The framework by which a company divides, groups and coordinates its work.
P
Project structure
Dedicated project teams under a PM with sole authority; fast decisions, but costly and loyal to the project.
R
Reengineering project
A project that radically redesigns business processes to achieve major improvements.
Resource pool (PMO model)
A PMO that maintains and assigns trained project managers to projects.
S
Skunk Works
Lockheed's famous heavyweight team: a small, isolated group working fast on innovative projects.
SO strategy (Maxi-Maxi)
Using strengths to take advantage of opportunities.
ST strategy (Maxi-Mini)
Using strengths to minimize threats.
Stakeholder
Any person or group that can affect, or is affected by, the project.
Strategic management
The science of formulating, implementing and evaluating decisions that let an organization achieve its objectives.
Supportive PMO
A PMO with low control that supplies templates, training and best practices.
SWOT analysis
Assessing an organization's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
W
Weak, balanced and strong matrix
Matrix variants that differ in how much authority the project manager holds.
Weather station (PMO model)
A PMO that tracks and reports on projects without directing them.
WO strategy (Mini-Maxi)
Minimizing weaknesses by taking advantage of opportunities.
WT strategy (Mini-Mini)
Minimizing weaknesses and avoiding threats.
Ch 3

Team

48 terms
A
Adaptive leadership
Leading when a problem has no known solution: the leader frames questions and mobilizes the team to learn.
Adjourning
Tuckman stage 5 (dissolving): the work ends and the team disbands.
Agile programming group
A democratic variant where some management decisions are delegated; in XP, pairs share code ownership.
Authority (influence)
Influence granted by the organizational hierarchy or role.
B
Business team
The most common team model: a technical lead plus a team of equal status.
C
Chief programmer (surgical) team
A team built around one expert who writes the core code, supported by specialists.
Collaborating
Integrating several perspectives into a creative solution.
Commitment
Clarity about what was decided plus buy-in to support it; buy-in does not require consensus.
Communication paths
n(n − 1) / 2 for n people; 50 people ≈ 1,225 paths.
Compromising
Reaching a middle ground through mutual concessions.
Conciliator
A neutral person outside a conflict who mediates between the two parties.
Conflict norms
The team's rules of engagement for debate: tone, language, timing, emotional expression.
Confronting (problem solving)
Resolving the root cause of a conflict through open, honest dialogue.
Consensus
General agreement where everyone feels heard and commits, even if not everyone agrees.
Creative team
A team built to generate new ideas or products; looser hierarchy and risk-taking.
D
Democratic team
A self-organizing team that decides by consensus; the leader is the external interface only.
E
Egoless programming
Team members strive to improve each other's programs.
Expert (influence)
Influence based on specialized knowledge or skills.
F
Forcing (directing)
Imposing a solution through authority or pressure.
Forming
Tuckman stage 1: the group comes together and members get to know one another.
G
Group cohesiveness
Members consider the group more important than any individual in it.
Groupthink
A cohesive group preserving harmony and itself at the expense of critical thinking and technical sense.
I
Interaction-oriented
A personality type motivated by the presence and actions of co-workers.
M
Maslow's hierarchy of needs
Physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-realization (self-actualization).
N
Norming
Tuckman stage 3: agreement is reached on how the group operates.
P
Peer accountability
Team members holding each other to standards instead of relying on the manager.
Performing
Tuckman stage 4: the group practices its craft and meets its objectives effectively.
Personal Software Process (PSP)
A formal approach where developers track their own time and work to improve quality, estimates and commitments.
Problem-resolution team
A team built for a complex, poorly defined problem; flexible roles and rapid decisions.
Punishment (influence)
Influence through the threat or use of punishment.
R
RACI
Responsible (does the work), Accountable (single owner who signs off), Consulted, Informed; RASCI adds Support.
Referent (influence)
Influence based on personal traits, charisma or likability.
Responsibility assignment matrix (RAM)
A resource planning tool showing who does what, for planning and tracking.
Reward (influence)
Influence through the ability to offer incentives.
S
Self-oriented
A personality type motivated by personal goals; work is a means to an end.
Skills matrix
A resource planning tool with resources on one axis and skills on the other.
Skunk works team
Talented developers placed away from the main organization; high ownership, little visibility.
Smoothing (accommodating)
Keeping harmony by playing down differences.
Staff selection factors
Domain, platform and language experience, problem solving, education, communication, adaptability, attitude, personality.
Storming
Tuckman stage 2: chaotic competition for leadership and trials of group processes.
SWAT team
A highly skilled team whose skills tightly match a specific goal.
T
Tactical-execution team
A team built to carry out a well-defined plan; clear roles and tasks.
Task-oriented
A personality type motivated by the work itself.
Task-to-people fit
Whether each person can perform the engineering task they are assigned.
Team needs pyramid
Trust → managed conflict → commitment → accountability → results; lower needs must be met first.
Technical leadership
Leading when a problem can be solved by known means: the leader directs and applies best practices.
V
Vulnerability-based trust
Trust where members are comfortable admitting mistakes, asking for help and saying “I don't know”.
W
Withdrawal (avoidance)
Delaying or sidestepping a conflict.
Ch 4

Development Approach

46 terms
A
Activities (methodology)
The meetings, reviews and other general activities a person must attend, generate or do.
Ad hoc (code and fix)
No formal process: write code and fix problems as they appear.
Adaptive process
Lean and agile approaches that respond to change instead of fixing a plan.
Agile Manifesto
Four values: individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan.
B
Backlog activities
Refinement, estimation, creation and prioritization of backlog items.
Backlog item states
Ready for consideration → ready for refinement → ready for implementation.
C
Collective ownership
Any developer can change any part of the code.
Construction
Unified process phase where most of the system is built.
Continuous integration
Integrating and testing code changes frequently, often several times a day.
D
Daily scrum
A short daily meeting to synchronize work and raise blockers.
DevOps
A culture and set of practices that join development and operations through shared ownership and automation.
DevOps metrics
Deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and mean time to recover (MTTR).
E
Elaboration
The most critical unified process phase: settles the architecture and the main risks.
Evolutionary (iterative) SDLC
Builds the system through repeated cycles; suits unclear requirements, new technology and high complexity.
Extreme Programming (XP)
An agile method built on practices such as pair programming, TDD, small releases and continuous integration.
I
Inception
Unified process phase that establishes the business case and scope.
M
Methodology
A defined set of practices, roles and artifacts for building software.
Methodology elements
Teams, roles, skills, techniques, activities, process, work products, milestones, standards and quality.
Milestone
An event marking progress or completion; an instant in time that is either fully met or not.
Minimum viable product (MVP)
The smallest product that delivers value and lets the team learn from real use.
O
On-site customer
A customer representative who works with the team full time.
P
Pair programming
Two developers at one computer, one writing and one reviewing.
Potentially shippable increment
The done, usable product produced at the end of a sprint.
Prescriptive process
A process that defines its steps in advance: sequential (waterfall), evolutionary or unified.
Process
A series of steps to achieve a goal; in software, the steps that turn requirements into a product.
Process model
An abstract description of a process, such as waterfall or iterative.
Product backlog
The ordered list of everything that might be built in the product.
Product owner
Scrum role focused on the product: owns and orders the product backlog.
Project life cycle
The series of phases a project passes through from start to finish.
R
Refactoring
Improving code structure without changing its behavior.
Rolling wave planning
Planning near-term work in detail and later work at a higher level, refining as you go.
S
Scrum
An agile management framework built on sprints, roles, events and artifacts.
ScrumMaster
Scrum role focused on the team: coaches it and removes impediments.
Software development life cycle (SDLC)
The phases of building software: requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, maintenance.
Spiral model
A risk-driven evolutionary model where each loop analyzes risk, often with a prototype.
Sprint
A time-box of 2–4 weeks that produces a potentially shippable increment.
Sprint backlog
The backlog items selected for a sprint plus the plan to deliver them.
Sustainable pace
Working hours the team can keep up indefinitely without burnout.
T
Test-driven development (TDD)
Writing automated tests before writing the code that makes them pass.
Transition
Unified process phase that delivers the system to users.
Triple constraint (agile vs waterfall)
Waterfall fixes scope and estimates time and cost; agile fixes time and cost and estimates scope.
U
Unified process
An iterative process with four phases: inception, elaboration, construction, transition.
User story
A short description of a feature from the user's point of view.
V
Velocity
The amount of work (story points) a team completes per sprint.
W
Waterfall (sequential) SDLC
Phases run one after another; suits clear, stable requirements, proven technology, low complexity and enough time.
Waterfall failure symptoms
Late integration, late risk resolution, adversarial stakeholders, protracted integration.
Ch 5

Risk Management

49 terms
8
80/20 rule (risk)
A few risks cause most of the damage, so focus on the high-exposure ones.
A
Accept
Acknowledge a risk without acting, often with a contingency plan or reserve.
Assumptions analysis
Testing the project's assumptions for inaccuracy, inconsistency or incompleteness.
Avoid
Remove the cause of a threat, for example by cutting the risky scope.
B
Brainstorming
A group technique for generating risks quickly.
Business risk
A risk that threatens the product's viability: market, strategy, sales, management, budget.
C
Cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram
A diagram that traces effects back to their possible causes.
Contingency plan
A plan put into action if an identified risk occurs.
D
Delphi technique
Experts give anonymous estimates over several rounds until they converge.
E
Enhance
Increase an opportunity's probability or impact.
Escalate
Pass a risk to a higher level when it is outside the project's authority.
Exploit
Make sure an opportunity happens.
F
Fallback plan
A backup used when the planned risk response fails.
Five-point probability scale
Highly unlikely < 20%, unlikely 20–40%, about even 40–60%, likely 60–80%, highly likely > 80%.
I
Influence diagram
A diagram showing causal influences, time ordering and relationships between variables.
Issue
A risk that has already occurred; a current problem.
K
Known risks
Risks uncovered by careful evaluation of the project plan and environment.
M
Miniature milestones
Small, frequent milestones that reveal slippage early and reduce risk.
Mitigate
Reduce a threat's probability or impact.
O
Opportunity
A risk with a positive effect.
P
Predictable risks
Risks extrapolated from past project experience, such as staff turnover.
Probability and impact matrix
A grid that combines probability and impact into an ordinal risk rating.
Project risk
A risk that threatens the project plan: schedule, cost, resources.
R
Residual risk
The risk that remains after a response has been applied.
Risk
An uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on project objectives.
Risk audit
A review of how effective risk responses and the risk process are.
Risk breakdown structure (RBS)
A hierarchical decomposition of risk categories, analogous to a WBS.
Risk exposure (RE)
Probability × size of loss; e.g. 25% × 4 weeks = 1 week.
Risk item checklist
Categories to check: product size, business impact, customer, process, technology, development environment, staff size and experience.
Risk management plan
Defines methodology, roles, budget, timing, categories, probability/impact definitions, matrix, tolerances, reporting and tracking.
Risk management process
Identification → analysis → planning → monitoring.
Risk register
The document that records identified risks, their analysis, owners and responses.
Risk tolerance
The amount of risk an organization or person is willing to accept.
Risk urgency assessment
Deciding how soon a risk could occur so near-term risks get responses first.
Risk-averse
Unwilling to take risks; prefers certainty.
Risk-seeking
Willing to accept risk for a potential reward.
RMMM
Risk mitigation, monitoring and management plan.
Root cause analysis
Finding the underlying cause of a problem or risk.
S
Secondary risk
A new risk created by implementing a risk response.
Share
Give an opportunity to a third party better able to capture it.
SWOT analysis (risk)
Identifying risks through strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
T
Technical risk
A risk that threatens the quality and timeliness of the software.
Threat
A risk with a negative effect.
Top 10 risk list
Actively managing only the ten most important risks at a time.
Transfer
Shift a threat's impact and ownership to a third party, such as insurance or a fixed-price contract.
U
Unknowable risks
Risks that cannot be foreseen; handled through crisis or disaster management.
Unpredictable risks
Risks that can occur but are extremely hard to identify in advance.
W
Watch list
Lower-priority risks kept under observation instead of being actively managed.
Workaround
An unplanned response to a risk that has occurred.
Ch 6

Estimation

43 terms
A
Activity resource estimation
Estimating who/what resources an activity needs, how much, and when.
Algorithmic estimation
Estimating with formulas from size measures such as LOC or function points (e.g. COCOMO).
Alternative analysis
Comparing ways to staff and deliver work: in-house, contracting or COTS.
Average staff size
Staff = E ÷ TDEV.
B
Basic COCOMO effort
E = a × (KLOC)^b staff-months.
Bottom-up estimation
Estimating each WBS task and summing; accurate but slow and needs a WBS.
C
Clock time
The elapsed calendar time a task takes.
COCOMO
The Constructive Cost Model: an algorithmic model that estimates effort and schedule from KLOC.
Code reuse rule
If more than 50% of reused code is modified, count it as new code.
Committed date
A date the team agrees to after real planning.
Complexity (influence) factor
The adjustment applied to function points based on 14 general system characteristics.
Cone of uncertainty
Early estimates can be off by 0.25× to 4×; they converge toward 1× as the project progresses.
Cost drivers
Ratings of product, hardware, personnel and project attributes that scale the COCOMO estimate.
D
Development time (TDEV)
TDEV = c × E^d months.
Double padding
Both the developer and the manager add safety buffers to the same estimate.
Duration
Elapsed working time; duration = effort ÷ number of people.
E
Effort
The total work needed, in person-hours, person-days or staff-months.
Embedded mode
Complex, tightly constrained projects (> 300 KLOC): a 3.6, b 1.20, c 2.5, d 0.32.
Estimate presentation
Present estimates with ± qualifiers or ranges, not as a single exact number.
Estimation
Predicting the size, effort and schedule of a project; the basic order is size → effort → schedule.
Estimation by analogy
Estimating from actual data of a similar past project.
Expert judgment
Estimating from the knowledge of experienced people.
F
Function points (FP)
A size measure based on the functions the software provides: inputs, outputs, inquiries, files and interfaces.
I
Intermediate COCOMO
Adjusts basic COCOMO effort with 15 cost drivers (multipliers).
K
KLOC
Thousands of lines of code.
L
Labor time
The actual effort (staff-hours) a task requires.
Lines of code (LOC)
A size measure counting lines of source code; language-dependent.
O
Organic mode
Small, simple projects (2–50 KLOC) with experienced teams: a 2.4, b 1.05, c 2.5, d 0.38.
P
Parkinson's law
Work expands to fill the time available; a risk of overestimation.
PERT estimate
E = (O + 4M + P) / 6; standard deviation = (P − O) / 6.
Priced to win
Setting the estimate by what wins the bid; its danger is being “priced to lose”.
Productivity
LOC ÷ effort, in LOC per staff-month.
Published estimating data
Empirical productivity and effort baselines from historical or published sources.
R
Real vs artificial deadline
A deadline driven by a genuine business need vs one set arbitrarily.
Reserve analysis
Determining contingency and management reserves for the estimate.
Resource calendar
Documents working and non-working days, including resource-specific holidays.
S
Semi-detached mode
Medium projects (50–300 KLOC) with mixed experience: a 3.0, b 1.12, c 2.5, d 0.35.
Student syndrome
Starting work only at the last moment, wasting any safety margin.
T
Target date
A date proposed by business or marketing; don't commit to it too soon.
Three-point estimate
Using optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) estimates.
Top-down estimation
Estimating the whole project from its overall characteristics; quick and good early, less accurate.
U
Unadjusted function points
The weighted count of functions before the complexity adjustment.
W
Wideband Delphi
Experts estimate anonymously, discuss differences and re-estimate until they reach consensus.

Scheduling and Tracking

43 termsNot on the final
A
Actual cost (AC)
The actual cost of the work completed.
B
Backward pass
Computing late start (LS) and late finish (LF) dates.
Brooks's law
Adding people to a late software project makes it later.
Burndown chart
A chart of work remaining (y-axis) against time (x-axis).
C
Cost performance index (CPI)
EV ÷ AC; below 1 means over budget.
Cost variance (CV)
EV − AC; negative means over budget.
Crashing
Adding resources to critical activities to shorten them; costs more.
Critical chain method (CCM)
A resource-aware improvement on CPM that pools padding into buffers.
Critical path
The longest path through the network; it sets the minimum project duration and its activities have zero slack.
Critical path method (CPM)
Computing early and late dates with a forward and a backward pass, ignoring resource limits.
E
Earned value (EV)
The budgeted value of work actually completed.
Earned value management (EVM)
A control method integrating scope, schedule and cost to measure performance.
F
Fast-tracking
Running sequential activities in parallel; adds risk and rework.
Feeding buffer
A buffer where a non-critical chain joins the critical chain.
Finish-to-finish (FF)
B can finish only when A finishes; common for implementation/integration pairs.
Finish-to-start (FS)
The default dependency: B starts when A finishes.
Forward pass
Computing early start (ES) and early finish (EF) dates.
G
Gantt chart
A bar chart of activities against time; easy to read, but it may hide dependencies.
H
Hybrid WBS
A WBS combining phases and deliverables.
L
Lag
A delay added to a dependency.
Lead
Allowing a successor to start before its predecessor finishes.
M
Mythical man-month
Brooks's point that effort and progress are not interchangeable: people and months don't trade.
N
Network analysis
Analyzing the network of activities and dependencies to plan the schedule.
P
Percent complete
Completed effort ÷ total effort; beware the illusion of progress.
PERT
Program Evaluation and Review Technique: expected time = (a + 4m + b) / 6.
Planned value (PV)
The budgeted value of work scheduled to date.
Planning, estimating, scheduling, tracking
Planning decides what work is needed, estimating sizes it, scheduling puts it on a calendar, tracking compares progress with the plan.
Precedence diagramming method (PDM)
A network diagram with activities on nodes linked by dependencies.
Precedence table
A table listing each activity, its duration and its predecessors.
Process WBS
An activity-oriented WBS (requirements, design, testing), used by the project manager.
Product WBS
An entity-oriented WBS listing the things being built, used by engineering.
Project buffer
A buffer at the end of the critical chain that protects the end date.
Project velocity
Story points completed per iteration; used to predict capacity.
R
Resource leveling
Adjusting the schedule so resources aren't over-allocated, which can extend the end date.
S
Schedule compression
Shortening the schedule without changing scope.
Schedule performance index (SPI)
EV ÷ PV; below 1 means behind schedule.
Schedule variance (SV)
EV − PV; negative means behind schedule.
Slack (float)
How long an activity can be delayed without delaying the project: LS − ES = LF − EF.
Start-to-finish (SF)
B can finish only once A has started; rare.
Start-to-start (SS)
B can start once A has started, often with a lag.
W
WBS techniques
Top-down, bottom-up, analogy and brainstorming.
What-if analysis
Evaluating scenarios to predict their effect on the schedule.
Work package
The lowest-level WBS item, typically 1–2 people for 1–2 weeks.

I

Characteristics users expect without stating them, such as ease of use and maintainability.
Indicator
A metric or combination of metrics that gives insight into the process or product.
M
MBWA

Measurement
The act of determining a measure.
Metric

P
Process metrics
Metrics about how the work is done; private (individual) or public (team).
Process spectrum
The range from lightweight to heavyweight processes; choose the right weight for the project.
Product metrics
Metrics about the product itself; static (e.g. size, complexity) or dynamic (measured while running).
Project metrics
Metrics used to track and control the project, such as effort and schedule.

On schedule, within budget, meets requirements and high quality.
Project success factors

Q
Quality assurance (QA)

Quality control (QC)

S
Say no

Size-oriented metrics
Metrics normalized by size, such as defects per KLOC.
Social factors (unsuccessful projects)
People and organizational problems that contribute to failure.
Software quality
Conformance to explicit requirements, development standards and implicit characteristics expected of professional software.
SQA group
The team responsible for software quality assurance: planning, reviews, audits and records.
SQA plan
The plan describing the quality activities, standards, reviews and audits for a project.
T
Technology factors (unsuccessful projects)
Technical and process problems such as informal testing, manual design and high requirements creep.
Testing
Executing software to find defects; it measures quality but does not deliver it.
Think small
Keep projects and scope small to raise the chance of success.

Change Management

30 terms
A
Adaptive maintenance
Changing software to work in a new environment, such as a new OS.
Agile change control
No CCB: the customer owns product changes and the team owns process changes.
Architectural transformation
Changing the system's architecture, e.g. from mainframe to client-server; driven by hardware cost and GUI expectations.
B
Back-out plan
How to return to the old system if a migration fails.
Baseline
An approved version of a work product that can only be changed through formal change control.
C
Change control board (CCB)
A formally chartered group that reviews, approves, delays or rejects changes and records the decisions.
Change control process
Recognize need → submit request → evaluate impact → change report → CCB decision → implement and audit → release.
Change prediction
Estimating where and how much change a system will need, using complexity and process metrics.
Change request (RFC / ECO)
A formal proposal to change a deliverable, document or baseline.
Configuration management
Version and change control of software configuration items.
Continuing change
A system in use must change or become progressively less useful.
Corrective maintenance
Fixing faults.
D
Data conversion
Converting existing data into the format the new system needs.
F
Flash-cut migration
Everyone switches to the new system at once.
I
Increasing complexity
As a system changes, its structure degrades unless work is done to simplify it.
Integrated change control
Reviewing all change requests and approving, managing and communicating changes across the project.
L
Lehman's laws
Laws of software evolution: continuing change, increasing complexity, large program evolution, organizational stability, conservation of familiarity.
M
Maintenance cost
Typically 2 to 100 times the development cost; driven by team stability, contracts, staff skills and program structure.
Migration
Moving users and data from an old system to a new one.
P
Parallel operation
Old and new systems run side by side for a period.
Perfective maintenance
Adding or improving functionality for new requirements.
Phased implementation
Rolling out one location or module at a time.
Pilot implementation
Trying the system first with a small group of users.
Project in trouble (drowning project)
A project far behind schedule; options are cut size, increase productivity or slip the schedule.
R
Re-engineering
Restructuring a system to make it easier to understand and change, without adding functionality.
Recovery steps
People steps (morale, roles), process steps (miniature milestones) and product steps (stabilize requirements, trim features).
Roll-out
Releasing the system to users, with a release checklist, training and documentation.
S
Software configuration item (SCI)
A work product (code, document, data) placed under configuration control.
Software maintenance
Changing software in response to new requirements while its structure stays the same.
V
Version control
Managing the versions of configuration items over time.
