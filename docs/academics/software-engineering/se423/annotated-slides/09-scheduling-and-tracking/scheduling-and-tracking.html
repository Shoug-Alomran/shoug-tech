Slide 25 / 80

Network Analysis Terminology

Notes

  1. 1

    Why the longest path? Because the project can’t finish until its longest chain of work is done. Every task on it has no spare time, so any delay there moves the end date.

  2. 2

    A milestone takes no time and uses no resources. It’s a checkpoint marking that something got done, such as a completed phase.

  3. 3

    Trap The critical path is the LONGEST path, not the shortest. Its length is the SHORTEST possible project time.

Explain it to me

The ideaThe critical path is the longest path through the network. Its activities have zero slack, so any delay on it delays the whole project.

ExamplePaths A-C-D-E (35 days) and A-B-D-E (30 days): A-C-D-E is critical.

Exam trapCritical means longest, not “most important” or “most expensive”.

Slide 34 / 80

Slack Time

Notes

  1. 1

    Slack is how much a task can slip without moving the end date. It’s worked out by comparing the latest and earliest finish.

  2. 2

    If there’s no spare time, any delay moves the whole project. So zero slack is how you recognize a critical task.

Explain it to me

The ideaSlack (float) is how long an activity can slip without delaying the project: LF − EF (or LS − ES).

ExampleEF = 10, LF = 14 → slack = 4 days.

Exam trapCritical activities have slack = 0. Negative slack means the schedule is already late.

Slide 35 / 80

Critical Path Method

Notes

  1. 1

    A task can’t start until everything before it is done, which is why you take the largest incoming finish time. Going forward tells you the earliest date the project can end.

  2. 2

    Going back from the end, a task must finish before its earliest successor needs to start, so you take the smallest. This tells you how late each task may be, without delaying the end.

  3. 3

    Both are spare time, but they differ in who’s affected. Using free float bothers nobody, while using up total float can squeeze later tasks, though the end date holds.

  4. 4

    Memory trick Forward = MAX of incoming EFs. Backward = MIN of outgoing LSs.

Explain it to me

The ideaCPM: a forward pass gives the early start and finish, a backward pass gives the late start and finish, and the difference is slack.

ExampleForward pass: ES of the next task = the largest EF of its predecessors.

Exam trapCPM ignores resource limits. That gap is what the critical chain method and resource leveling fix.

PredictionQ1

What is a Gantt chart, what does it show, and give two advantages and two limitations.

Compare with the model answer

A Gantt chart (Henry Gantt) is a task table plus a horizontal bar chart against a time axis. Each bar is an activity or WBS phase; it shows start/end dates, durations, planned vs actual progress, and can show milestones (zero-length diamonds, e.g. “requirements signed off”) and dependencies (arrows, e.g. testing starts after coding finishes). Advantages: easy to understand and communicate; easy to update. Limitations: low information density and unwieldy for large projects; dependencies and the critical path are not always visible, so you can’t see the effect of an early or late start.

Q6.1 · how crashing and fast-tracking affect cost and risk

PredictionQ1

How can crashing and fast-tracking affect overall project cost and risk?

Compare with the model answer

Both shorten the schedule without changing scope. Crashing adds resources (overtime, extra staff) to critical-path activities, which raises cost directly, gives diminishing returns, and new people can add communication overhead and quality risk (Brooks’s Law). Fast-tracking runs sequential activities in parallel; cost rises less, but risk rises because work starts on assumptions, causing rework when earlier results change. Neither can compress much more than ~25% (Boehm).
