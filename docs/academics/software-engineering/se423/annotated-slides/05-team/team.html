Overview

Team

The most examined chapter in SE423: both 251 midterms took most of their short questions from it, and both finals have a whole team-based collaboration question. It has three parts. Team development: Tuckman’s stages, team objectives and structures, and the team needs pyramid (trust → managed conflict → commitment → accountability → results) with RACI and skills matrices as the accountability tools. Team models: business, democratic, chief-programmer, skunk works, SWAT, and why large teams need hierarchy (n(n−1)/2 channels). Managing people: motivation and personality types, cohesiveness and groupthink, staff selection, working environments, shaping project culture, technical vs. adaptive leadership, and reaching consensus.

The instructor’s style: questions ask you to apply an idea to a team situation (“team members disagree: how do you encourage collaboration?”). Saying one word (“negotiation”) is not enough; explain what you do, how, and why.

Team pyramid

Trust → managed conflict → commitment → accountability → results. Each layer needs the one below; trust is the foundation.

Tuckman

Forming → storming (competing for leadership) → norming (agree how to work) → performing → adjourning.

RACI

R does the work, A owns the outcome (exactly one per task), C gives input (two-way), I is kept updated (one-way).

Team models

Business (tech lead + equals, most common), democratic (whole team decides), chief-programmer (surgical), skunk works, SWAT.

Leadership

Technical leadership for problems solvable by known means; adaptive leadership for new or complex ones. Most IT projects need both.

Consensus

Not unanimous agreement: everyone feels heard and the group moves forward committed. Persuade, don’t dictate.

Compare with the model answer

Team Skills, Influence, and Style

Notes

    The types of influence a PM can use. Formal (legitimate): authority granted by the organizational hierarchy or role. The skills table above lists the PM’s team skills (planning, negotiation, hiring, risk management, judgement) and the bottom table lists leadership styles (directing, facilitating, coaching, supporting, etc.).

  2. 2

    It works because people want the reward, and it stops working if you run out of things to offer. Reward also requires that you actually control something they value, such as bonuses or interesting assignments.

  3. 3

  4. 4

Slide 8 / 77

Tuckman's Model for Team Development

Notes

  1. 1

    New people are careful and polite, and they look to the leader for direction. Forming feels calm, but little gets done yet, because people are still working out what’s expected of them.

  2. 2

    Once people relax, they start to push back and test who has influence. Storming feels like something’s going wrong, but it’s a normal stage. The PM’s job here is to surface and manage the conflict, not suppress it.

  3. 3

    Arguments settle and the group agrees how it will work, such as how decisions get made. Norming is when people start saying “we” rather than “I” and accept each other’s strengths.

  4. 4

    People stop spending energy on how to work together and spend it on the work. Performing is what you’re aiming for, and the team needs little supervision at this point.

  5. 5

    People may feel loss when a good team breaks up, so a proper ending matters: adjourning is when you wrap up and capture what was learned. Order: form → storm → norm → perform → adjourn.

Tips & tricks

  • The order matters, and they rhyme: Form, Storm, Norm, Perform, then Adjourn.

Building Trust

Notes

  1. 1

    Everything else in the pyramid depends on this. If people don’t feel safe, they hide problems and avoid arguments, so trust comes first as the foundation.

  2. 2

    Most people assume that trust means “you’re reliable”. This kind goes further: it means being able to say “I messed up” and know nobody will use it against you. This list was a real midterm question, so learn vulnerability-based trust.

  3. 3

    Words can’t build it fast, as people need to see you keep your word several times. So it takes time, and since one broken promise can undo months, it must be maintained.

  4. 4

    Knowing how a teammate tends to work removes a lot of misunderstanding: “she’s direct, not rude”. Behavioral profiling is useful as a conversation starter, not as a label, and it lets the PM give people work that suits them.

Exam angle

Past exam · Midterm 251 · V1 · Short Q2 (also V2 Short Q1)Q1

What does vulnerability-based trust involve?

Compare with the model answer

Team members are comfortable being open with each other: admitting mistakes and weaknesses, asking for help, and saying “I don’t know”, without fear that it will be used against them. It takes time to build through consistent behavior, follow-through and shared experiences under pressure, must be maintained, and is the foundation of the team pyramid.

Past exam · Final 242 · Q5.2Q2

How does fostering trust and managing conflict within a software project team contribute to improved collaboration and project success?

Compare with the model answer

Trust lets members admit mistakes, share information and ask for help, so problems surface early and people cooperate instead of protecting themselves. Managed (healthy) conflict means ideas are debated openly and respectfully, which gives better technical decisions and avoids groupthink or disputes that turn personal. Together they lead to real commitment and collaboration, which drives quality and project success.

Slide 12 / 77

Establishing a basis of trust

Notes

  1. 1

    A title makes people obey, but it doesn’t make them trust you. You have to show them through what you do, so trust is earned, not granted, starting from a natural wariness.

  2. 2

    If people can’t tell you things, you find out about problems only when it’s too late. So open communication is risk management, and not just being friendly.

  3. 3

    If the PM hides their own mistakes, everyone learns to hide theirs. Be fallible by owning an error openly, because that makes it safe for others to do the same.

  4. 4

    People watch what you do far more than what you say. A PM who asks for punctuality and arrives late has told them what’s really expected. That’s why you lead by example.

  5. 5

    Nobody expects perfection, but they do expect honesty. Telling people in advance that you’ll miss a commitment keeps trust, while a surprise and an excuse damages it.

  6. 6

    Friendship ≠ leadership: warmth helps, but leading needs clarity and boundaries (repeated on slide 76).

Managing Conflict

Notes

  1. 1

    Think of healthy debate as a fight about ideas where nobody’s attacking the person. It only works if you trust that disagreement won’t be used against you, which is why healthy conflict sits above trust.

  2. 2

    If everyone is always comfortable, tough topics probably aren’t being raised. So some uncomfortable moments are a good sign, not something to fix.

  3. 3

    What’s normal in one team feels rude in another. Agreeing the rules up front, such as “challenge the idea, not the person”, stops arguments about how to argue. That’s what conflict norms are for.

  4. 4

    If nobody challenges a weak design because it would feel awkward, the mistake ships. That’s the cost of avoidance, and groupthink is what it looks like: everyone nods, and nobody really agrees.

Managing Conflict – How?

Notes

  1. 1

    This asks “what’s really causing this?” and tackles that. It takes longer, but the problem doesn’t come back, which is why confronting isn’t the same as being aggressive.

  2. 2

    Compromising: both sides give something up to reach a middle ground. Quick, but nobody gets what they needed.

  3. 3

    These two feel peaceful, but the cause stays there and tends to return bigger. Withdrawal can buy time when emotions are high. Smoothing can suit minor issues, or when the relationship matters more than the point.

  4. 4

    Each side’s view contributes something, so you may end up with a better answer than either started with. Collaborating takes time, so save it for decisions that matter.

  5. 5

    Sometimes there’s no time to discuss, like during an outage. Forcing gets a quick decision, but people who were overruled won’t feel committed, so use it rarely.

  6. 6

    Notice the first step: many teams solve the wrong problem because they jump to answers. The final step is the one often skipped: review whether it actually fixed the issue.

  • Sentence, in slide order: Cool Cats Will Still Calm Fights. Confronting, Compromising, Withdrawal, Smoothing, Collaborating, Forcing.

Exam angle

Past exam · Midterm 261 · ShortQ1

Team members disagree about the best solution to a problem. How can the project manager encourage collaboration? (Saying “negotiation” alone is not enough: explain.)

Compare with the model answer

Treat it as healthy conflict and use collaborating/confronting (problem solving) rather than forcing or smoothing. Bring the people together for an open discussion focused on the problem, not on people; define the real problem and agree the criteria for a good solution; ask each person to present their option with its reasoning and data; weigh the pros and cons of each option together; then converge on the option that best serves the project, or combine ideas into a better one. Work with those who preferred other options so they buy in. This gives a better decision and real commitment because everyone was heard.

Slide 17 / 77

Commitment

Notes

  1. 1

    Ever left a meeting and found that everyone understood something different? Commitment fails that way, so a decision needs to be both understood and accepted.

  2. 2

    A simple habit fixes most of this: end each discussion by stating what’s been decided and who does what next. Without that clarity, people act on their own assumptions.

  3. 3

    People can accept a decision they argued against, as long as they feel it was fair and their view was heard. That’s why buy-in comes after debate rather than instead of it.

  4. 4

    Waiting for everyone to agree can freeze a team. Great teams argue, decide, and then back the decision (“disagree and commit”). A common trap: “commitment requires everyone to agree” is false, because buy-in does not require consensus.

Tips & tricks

  • CuB: Clarity, Buy-in.

Exam angle

Past exam · Final 242 · Q5.1Q1

In the context of the Team Needs Pyramid, explain how a project manager can foster commitment among team members.

Compare with the model answer

In the pyramid (trust → managed conflict → commitment → accountability → results), commitment sits on trust and healthy conflict: people commit when they trust each other and have had their say. The PM fosters it with clarity (clear decisions, goals, roles and deadlines, confirmed at the end of each meeting, with no assumptions) and buy-in (letting everyone voice their views so they support the decision even if they disagreed; consensus isn’t required). Recording decisions and owners (e.g. in a RACI) then lets commitment be followed by accountability.

Slide 19 / 77

Embracing Accountability

Notes

  1. 1

    If only the manager enforces standards, you have one person chasing everyone. When teammates say “this isn’t up to what we agreed” to one another, problems get fixed faster. That’s accountability among peers.

  2. 2

    If the PM lets poor work slide, the team learns that standards are optional. So the leader needs a willingness to confront it, and doing that respectfully shows others how.

  3. 3

    Reviews work because the facts are in front of everyone. Note the tone in the slide’s example, about the commitment and what happened rather than who failed, in meetings and regular reviews.

Exam angle

Past exam · Midterm 251 · V2 · Short Q2Q1

What’s the best time to reinforce accountability?

Compare with the model answer

During meetings and regular reviews of accomplishments, such as sprint reviews and status meetings, where what was committed is compared with what was done (e.g. “we committed to five stories but completed three; let’s explore why”). Make commitments clear and public at the start and address a missed commitment when it happens rather than waiting for the end of the project, so peers hold each other accountable continuously.

Past exam · Midterm 251 · V1 · Case Q1 (also V2)Q2

During a sprint deployment, a key feature was discovered to be not thoroughly tested and caused issues after release. As the project manager, what steps would you follow to address this issue and ensure accountability within the team?

Compare with the model answer

Use a RACI matrix to establish who was Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for testing that feature (0.5). Hold a retrospective to find the root cause (0.25). Make the responsible roles clear without blaming individuals (0.25). Take corrective action: release a hotfix, redistribute tasks, and add automated testing so it can’t recur (0.25). Inform the stakeholders of the recovery plan (0.25).

Past exam · Final 242 · Q5.3Q3

Your software project team is experiencing delays due to unclear task ownership and inconsistent updates. As the project manager, how would you apply a team-based approach to improve accountability and transparency within the team? Describe specific steps you would take to enhance collaboration and increase the project’s chances of success.

Compare with the model answer

1) Clarify ownership with a RACI matrix so every task has a Responsible person and exactly one Accountable person. 2) Hold short daily stand-ups (done, next, blocked) so updates are consistent. 3) Put all tasks on a shared board (e.g. Jira or Kanban) with visible goals and deadlines so progress is transparent. 4) Review commitments against results in regular reviews, encourage peer accountability, and run retrospectives to fix what caused the delays.

Exam angle

Past exam · Final 242 · Q2.3Q1

You are responsible for managing a diverse team with varying availability and skill sets. Describe two strategies you would use to allocate your team’s resources efficiently across tasks. How would you adjust your resource plan if one hardware specialist becomes unavailable for 4 weeks?

Compare with the model answer

Slide 72 / 77

Technical vs. adaptive leadership

Notes

  1. 1

    Ask first: does anyone know how to solve this? The answer decides which approach fits, and using the wrong one either wastes time or gives false confidence.

  2. 2

    Technical says “here’s what I see for us”, and it works because they know the way. Adaptive doesn’t have that map, so it uses a sense of purpose: why it’s worth working through.

  3. 3

    Tool for motivation: technical relies on personality: charisma, expertise, confidence, decisiveness. Adaptive relies on progress: learning, iteration and visible movement toward clarity. In short: technical = “follow me, I know”; adaptive = “let’s figure it out together.”

Exam angle

Past exam · Midterm 251 · V1 · Short Q5 (also V2 Short Q5)Q1

How can a project manager balance technical leadership with adaptive leadership in a software team?

Compare with the model answer

First diagnose the problem: can it be solved by known means? When it can (a coding standard, a deployment the team has done before), use technical leadership: give direction from expertise and vision, or delegate it to someone who can lead it. When it can’t (new technology, unclear requirements), use adaptive leadership: frame the purpose, lead with questions, let the team experiment and learn, and motivate through visible progress. Most IT projects mix both, so the PM switches between the two as the situation changes, delegating the known parts and focusing on the unknown ones.

Working toward consensus

Notes

  1. 1

  2. 2

Exam angle

Past exam · Final 251 · Q5.2Q1

You are leading a cross-functional team tasked with selecting a continuous integration/continuous deployment (CI/CD) platform for an upcoming project. The team includes software developers, DevOps engineers, and QA testers, each with distinct expectations regarding automation workflows and pipeline management. Describe your approach to collaboratively choosing the most suitable CI/CD platform.

Compare with the model answer

Run the consensus steps. Pose the decision in a kickoff workshop where developers, DevOps engineers and QA testers each list their needs (build speed, deployment control, test automation). Agree the criteria and their weights together: integration with the repository and tools, automation and test support, pipeline flexibility, scalability, security, cost, ease of use and vendor support. Shortlist options, weigh each against the criteria (a weighted scoring table, or a short proof of concept), converge on the one with the most benefit for the project, then work with those who preferred others so they buy in. Don’t just vote or dictate, and watch for passive-aggressive agreement.
