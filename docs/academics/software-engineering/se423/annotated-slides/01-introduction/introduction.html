Introduction to Software Project Management

Chapter 1 gives the vocabulary for the whole course. It defines project management, places it in history, describes what a project manager does and needs to be good at, and then works through the PMBOK 7 standard: the 12 principles (how to behave) and 8 performance domains (what to manage), the key terms (portfolio, program, project, product, outcome, value), the system for value delivery with its governance, functions and environment, and how product management relates to projects. The last part gives each principle its own card.

Principles vs. domains

12 principles guide behaviour (stewardship, team, value, systems thinking…); 8 performance domains are areas of focus that run at the same time. Neither is a set of steps.

Portfolio, program, project

Portfolio = grouped for strategy (may be unrelated, includes operations). Program = related projects for extra benefits. Project = temporary + unique.

Value chain

Components → deliverables → outcomes → benefits → value. Value is the ultimate measure of success.

Coordination

Decentralized: authority spread across a self-organizing team. Centralized: a designated PM leads. Both can be mixed.

Slide 4 / 70

What is Project Management?

Notes

  1. 1

    Each of the four nouns does a different job. Knowledge is what you understand (how estimation works). Skills are what you can do well (running a tense meeting). Tools are the things you use (a Gantt chart, Jira). Techniques are the methods you follow (critical path analysis). The definition lists all four because owning a tool without the skill to use it isn’t project management.

  2. 2

    A PM guides the work rather than performs it. Think of a film director: the director doesn’t act or operate the camera, but without one, the actors and crew would not produce a coherent film. On a software project the developers write the code, while the PM keeps that work pointed at the outcome the organization wanted.

  3. 3

    The approach is a choice, not a rule. Predictive means plan it all up front, which suits fixed requirements such as a regulated banking system. Adaptive means build in short cycles and adjust, which suits a new app whose users’ needs are unclear. Hybrid mixes them. The slide’s point is that good project management does not depend on one method.

Tips & tricks

  • KiTTenS: Knowledge, Tools, Techniques, Skills.
  • Project management guides the work. If an option says the PM “does” or “writes” the work, be suspicious.

Exam angle

PredictionQ1

Project management is the application of ______ to project activities to meet project requirements.

  1. These come from governance, which guides projects. They are not what the definition says is applied.
  2. These are things a project manager manages or produces. The definition names what is applied to the work, not the items being controlled.
  3. These are the four nouns in the definition: what you know, what you can do, what you use and the methods you follow.
  4. This sounds right, but it is a general management phrase and not the definition. Products are what a project creates, not what it applies.

PredictionQ2

A PM spends the week removing blockers, aligning the team on priorities and checking that the work still leads to what the client needs. She writes no code. Which statement describes this?

  1. The slide defines project management as guiding the project work toward the intended outcomes, so a PM can do it without producing deliverables herself.
  2. Product management runs across a product’s whole life cycle. Guiding one project’s work toward its outcomes is project management.
  3. Producing deliverables is the team’s work. The PM’s contribution is guiding that work.
  4. General management runs ongoing operations. The lack of technical tasks doesn’t change that this is temporary project work.

PredictionQ3

A team must follow fixed regulations and has stable requirements, so it plans the entire project up front. Which approach is this?

  1. Decentralized describes how a team is coordinated, not how the work is planned.
  2. Hybrid mixes up-front planning with iterative work. Nothing here is iterative.
  3. Predictive means detailed planning up front, which suits stable, well-understood requirements.
  4. Adaptive works in short cycles and expects requirements to change, which is the opposite of this team’s situation.

Slide 9 / 70

History of Project Management

Notes

  1. 1

    The pyramids count as projects in the modern sense because they were unique, had a clear goal and eventually ended. “Some people argue” matters here: without written methods, nobody can show that they used project management as we know it.

  2. 2

    The Manhattan Project, the US effort to build the atomic bomb during the Second World War, is the usual starting point for modern project management. It involved huge scale, a hard deadline and work spread across many sites, so informal coordination could not work.

  3. 3

    Its key lesson is the split between a project manager and a technical manager. Knowing the physics and running a three-year, $2 billion program are different jobs. Software teams still do this: an engineering lead makes the technical decisions, while the PM handles schedule, people and budget.

Tips & tricks

  • Picture two chairs at the head of the table: one for managing the project, one for leading the technology. That image is the Manhattan Project’s lesson.

Exam angle

PredictionQ1

  1. Some argue it was a project, but the slide does not call it modern project management.
  2. Same as the pyramids: arguably a project, but not “modern” project management.
  3. It isn’t on the slide. It came later than the Manhattan Project, which holds this title.
  4. Most people consider it the first, partly because of its separate project and technical managers.

PredictionQ2

What was distinctive about how the Manhattan Project was organized?

  1. This merges the two roles, which is exactly what the project avoided.
  2. PMOs became common in the 1990s, decades later.
  3. This describes decentralized coordination, covered on slide 39. The Manhattan Project had designated managers.
  4. Separating managerial and technical leadership is the structural lesson the slide highlights.

PredictionQ3

  2. Size, time pressure and many sites are the conditions that make formal management necessary.

Slide 10 / 70

History of Project Management

Notes

  1. 1

    Companies in the 1990s suddenly had dozens of projects running at once, sharing the same people and budgets. Each PM could run their own project well and the organization could still end up with chaos, such as three projects needing the same database expert in the same week. PMOs were created to solve that.

  2. 2

    Focus on the word coordinating. A PMO doesn’t usually run each project. It gives projects a common way of working (templates, standards, training), sees across all of them, and tells senior management how they are doing. Think of a university registrar: it doesn’t teach courses, but it makes all of them work together.

Tips & tricks

  • PMO = air traffic control. Pilots still fly the planes (PMs run projects), while the tower keeps everyone from colliding.

Exam angle

Past exam · Final 242 · Q1.3Q1

What is a Project Management Office (PMO) and its role in organizations?

Compare with the model answer

A PMO is a centralized organizational unit that oversees and standardizes how projects are managed. It sets PM standards, methods and templates, trains and supports project managers, monitors and reports on project performance, and coordinates resources across projects. Its control ranges from supportive (consultative) to controlling (requires compliance) to directive (manages the projects directly).

  3. Operations are the organization’s routine work. A PMO’s focus is the project management function.
  4. This is the reason the slide gives: too many complex projects to coordinate informally.

PredictionQ3

  2. This is the slide’s definition. Note “throughout an organization”.

Global Issues

Notes

  1. 1

    Organizations struggle to find capable project and program managers, which matches the talent gap on slide 5. So they treat growing them as a strategic concern, through training, mentoring and career paths.

  2. 2

Ethics in Project Management

Slide 15 / 70

Project Managers

Notes

  1. 1

    The PM answers for anything that threatens the project, even when someone else caused it. Schedule, budget and quality pull against each other: finishing sooner usually costs more money or lowers quality, and much of the job is deciding which one to protect when you can’t keep all three. “Depending on the project scale” means a small project may blur these, while a large one may assign specialists to each.

  2. 2

    Delivery means the finished product reaches the customer in a usable state: deployed, documented and accepted, not merely “code complete”. Locking in resources means securing people and equipment early. Your best database engineer can be pulled onto another project unless you’ve secured them, and that can damage your schedule more than a technical bug would.

  3. 3

    New PMs expect to spend their days on strategy, and instead they ask “is that done yet?” all day. Each task’s status lives in someone’s head, and the PM can’t make decisions without it. That is why status meetings, dashboards and tracking tools exist.

Tips & tricks

  • SQuaBbLeD: Schedule, Quality, Budget, Locking in resources, Delivery.
  • Triangle trick: picture time, cost and quality at three corners. Pull one corner, and the others move.

Exam angle

Past exam · Final 242 · Q1.1Q1

Define the term ‘project constraints’ and explain how they influence project planning and execution. Provide at least three examples of common constraints and discuss their interrelationship.

Compare with the model answer

Project constraints are the limits a project must be delivered within. The classic three are scope, time and cost, often with quality, resources and risk. They drive planning because every schedule, budget and scope decision has to fit inside them, and during execution the PM keeps trading them off. They are interrelated (the triple constraint): cutting the schedule usually needs more cost or less scope, and adding scope needs more time or money; quality sits in the middle and suffers if they are squeezed.

PredictionQ2

  2. Efficiency helps, but ignoring the trade-off is how projects end up over budget or full of bugs.
  3. Time pressure usually reduces quality rather than improving it.
  4. These constraints trade against each other. Moving one affects the others.

  2. This concerns getting results to the customer, not securing staff.

  4. Status tells you what has been done. It doesn’t secure the people you’ll need.

  3. The team does that work. The PM guides it.

Slide 16 / 70

The Project Manager

Notes

  1. 1

    Notice that all four responsibilities (planning, scheduling, coordinating, working with people) involve other people or their work. Job titles and descriptions vary across companies, but this core stays the same, whatever the industry.

  2. 2

    The 97% statistic is about experience. A PM who has seen projects fail recognizes warning signs early, knows who to talk to, and can influence the conditions for success. You can’t get that from a textbook alone, which is why junior PMs benefit from working under senior ones.

  3. 3

Advantages of Using Formal Project

Notes

  1. 1

    Without formal PM, money, equipment and people get spent wherever someone shouts loudest. Notes

  1. 1

    Build into is the important phrase. Quality isn’t something you inspect at the end. Finding a requirement misunderstanding after launch might cost 100 times more than catching it in a review. So quality activities run from the start and match what stakeholders actually need and use.

  • Product + Process: quality covers both what you make and how you make it.
  • Compare with slide 21: “embedded from the start”, not “checked at the end”.

Slide 68 / 70

Optimize Risk Responses

Notes

  1. 1

    Most people think of risk as only bad. PMBOK’s definition includes opportunities: a vendor might deliver early, which you could use to your advantage. Exposure means how much is at stake. The goal is to get the best overall result, which means maximizing the good as well as minimizing the bad.

  2. 2

    Risks exist at two levels. An individual risk might be one supplier failing, while overall risk is how risky the whole project is. And an organization’s attitude, appetite and threshold shape the response: a hospital will accept far less risk than a startup. They’re addressed continually, since new risks appear throughout the project.

  3. 3

    Five tests for a good response. Appropriate: don’t spend a fortune on a small risk. Cost-effective: the response shouldn’t cost more than the damage. Realistic: can the team actually do it? Agreed: do stakeholders accept it? Owned: who’s responsible? A risk that everyone owns is a risk nobody handles. Chapter 5 covers the strategies themselves.

Tips & tricks

  • Response tests: mACAROn — Appropriate, Cost-effective, Agreed, Realistic, Owned.
  • Risk ≠ always bad. Opportunities count too.
  • “Optimize”, not “eliminate”: an exam answer saying “remove all risk” is wrong.

Embrace Adaptability and Resiliency

Notes

  1. 1

    The card says to build these in, so it’s something you prepare ahead of time, not something you hope for. Keeping backups, cross-training staff and working in small increments all make a project able to handle shocks.

  2. 2

Slide 70 / 70

Enable Change to Achieve the Envisioned

Notes

  1. 1

    A system can be delivered perfectly and still fail if nobody uses it. So this principle is about the people affected, not the technology: getting them ready, helping them adopt the new behaviors, and keeping them from sliding back. “Sustainment” is the often-forgotten part: people must stay with the change after the excitement has faded.

  2. 2

    A structured approach means planning the transition rather than just announcing it: explain why, train people, support them through the switch, and collect feedback. Change comes from inside (a new CEO) or outside (a new law). Either way, not everyone will welcome it, because people often lose familiarity or status in the process.

  3. 3

    Change fatigue is what happens when people get too much change too quickly. They start ignoring announcements and resisting even useful changes, since they’re worn out rather than because the change is bad. The fix is pacing and motivation: spacing out changes, engaging stakeholders, and showing them why it’s worthwhile.

Tips & tricks

  • Deliverable ≠ adoption. Ask: “Are people actually working the new way?”
  • Too much change, too quickly → fatigue. Treat it by slowing down and engaging people, not by pushing harder.
