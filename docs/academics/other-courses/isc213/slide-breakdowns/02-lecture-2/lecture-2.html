PART ONE — METHODOLOGY
SLIDE BLOCK 1

01 The 9 qualifications of a researcher

Resolving an emerging financial issue depends on Ijtihad — the total effort of a qualified jurist (Mujtahid) to deduce a ruling from detailed scriptural evidence. Before anyone can attempt that, they must meet 9 requirements.

  1. Comprehensive understanding of the Qur'an.
  2. Comprehensive understanding of the Prophetic Hadith (Sunnah).
  3. Knowledge of legal consensus (Ijma') — aware of where past consensus was reached, to avoid contradictory or invalid rulings.
  4. Deep mastery of the Arabic language — grammar, rhetoric, vocabulary, linguistic nuance.
  5. The skill of Ijtihad itself — a jurisprudence talent for understanding fiqh issues.
  6. Honesty in word and action — avoiding major sins, not persisting in minor ones.
  7. Awareness of the higher objectives of religion (Maqasid al-Shari'ah) — preserving faith, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth — plus understanding lived reality, to apply rulings effectively.
  8. Ability to elicit/derive rulings from contemporary scholars' books.
  9. Understanding of lived reality and its surrounding circumstances, to apply rulings effectively.
Mnemonic — two groups
Knowledge base (QHIA): Qur'an, Hadith, Ijma', Arabic language.
Practical qualities (SHMRU): Skill (ijtihad talent), Honesty, Maqasid-awareness, Research ability (contemporary books), Understanding of lived reality.
Say it as: "Master the texts (QHIA), then bring the right character and awareness (SHMRU)."
Common mistake
Students list only the "book knowledge" items (Qur'an, Hadith, Ijma', Arabic) and forget the character/awareness requirements — honesty, Maqasid, and lived reality are graded just as often. Knowing the texts isn't enough; the mujtahid also needs integrity and situational awareness.
From the slides — answer with the notes
The slides pack the same content into six conditions: (1) knowledge of the Qur'an, the Sunnah, the issues of consensus and controversy in jurisprudence, the fundamentals of jurisprudence, and Arabic; (2) being self-jurisprudent — a jurisprudence talent; (3) honest in speech and just in his religion, avoiding major sins and leaving minor ones; (4) aware of the Shari'a's purposes; (5) able to elicit rules from established rulings; (6) familiar with reality. Two small extras: knowing where scholars disagreed, and the fundamentals of jurisprudence. Exam questions follow the notes, so list the nine.
SLIDE BLOCK 2

02 The 8 steps to a ruling

Once a qualified scholar takes on a new financial case, this is the order of operations. Sequence matters here — exam questions may ask "what comes right after step X."

  • Pray to Allah, asking for inspiration and right foresight on the case.
  • Build a deep understanding of the subject — this is what allows a confident judgment.
  • Search the legal texts — the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah — for the emerging case.
  • Examine the case in light of the Companions' sayings and arguments.
  • Search the jurisprudence of the four imams / schools of thought.
  • Search new sources — recent scholars' articles.
  • Use his own opinion to reach the correct rule, if steps 1–6 haven't settled it.
  • If no legitimate rule can be reached at all: refrain from ruling (no Fatwa).
Mnemonic
"Pious Understanding Searches [through] Companions, Imams, [and] Sources, [forms an] Opinion, [or else] Refrains." — walk through the sentence and each clause is one step, in order.
Common mistake
Students think "using his own opinion" is step 1 or comes early. It's actually the second-to-last resort — only after Qur'an, Sunnah, Companions, the four imams, and recent scholarship have all been checked. And if even personal opinion can't produce a legitimate rule, the correct move is to refrain — not to guess.
From the slides — answer with the notes
The slides show seven steps: they skip "use his own opinion" and go straight from new sources to refraining, and word step 3 as "collating the case to the legitimate texts." Exam questions follow the notes, so give all eight in order.
PART TWO — INCORPOREAL RIGHTS
SLIDE BLOCK 3

03 Definition of Rights

"An exclusive entitlement by which the Law (Sharī'ah) establishes an authority or an obligation." — Definition of Rights

Notice the definition has two possible shapes: a right can be an authority (you're empowered to do/have something) or an obligation (someone else is bound toward you). Everything that follows in this lecture is really just: which kind of authority, over what, established how.

Mnemonic
"A Right = the Law's exclusive grant — either power TO, or a bond ON someone else." Two words to remember: authority and obligation.
From the slides
Who assigns rights? Rights in Islam are divine grants drawn from the sources of Shari'a rulings. There is no legitimate right without evidence, so the origin of every right is Allah — there is no ruler but Him and no legislation but His.
Authority can be over a person (child custody — ḥaḍānah — guardianship, a ruler's authority to govern) or over a specific object (ownership).
Obligation can be financial (repaying a debt) or a performance (an employee doing contracted work, a parent raising their children).
SLIDE BLOCK 4

04 Political vs. Civil rights

Rights split first into two broad types.

Political rights
Established by law for individuals to organize governance and its authorities — e.g. the right of election and nomination.
Civil rights
Aimed at establishing the individual's interests directly. This is the branch the rest of the lecture zooms into.
Mnemonic
"Political = you and the state. Civil = you and your own interests." Election and nomination are your two go-to examples for Political.
SLIDE BLOCK 5

05 General vs. Private rights

Civil rights divide again, into two divisions.

General rights
Surround the human person with obligated care and respect — also called "inherent personality rights." Denying them degrades human dignity. Examples: the right to bodily safety, the inviolability of one's residence.
Private rights
Arise from bonds between individuals, under private law (civil law, personal-status law, and others).
Mnemonic
"General = rights you have just by being human (body, home). Private = rights that come from a bond with someone else." No other person is needed to hold a General right; a Private right always involves a second party.
SLIDE BLOCK 6

06 Family vs. Financial rights

Private rights divide once more, into two divisions.

Family rights
Governed by the rules of personal affairs — e.g. the right of custody, the rights of divorce.
Financial rights
Rights that could be evaluated with money. This is the branch that splits into the three types you need cold for the exam.
Mnemonic
"Family = can't be priced. Financial = can be priced." That single test — "could this be evaluated with money?" — is how you sort any example into one bucket or the other.
SLIDE BLOCK 7

07 The 3 financial rights (Personal / Material / Incorporeal)

This is the payoff of the whole hierarchy — the three types of Financial rights.

Personal rights
A law relation between two people, like a creditor and a debtor. Also called "commitments."
Material rights
A direct authority given by law to a person over a material/physical thing. Also called "property rights."
Incorporeal rights
The "rights of innovation" — authority of a person over something incorporeal (non-physical), like intellectual creations.
Mnemonic — "P.M.I."
Personal = People (a bond between two persons — creditor/debtor).
Material = Matter (authority over a physical thing — property).
Incorporeal = Ideas (authority over something intangible — innovation/IP).
Common mistake
Students mix up Personal and Material rights because both sound like they involve "another person." The test is: does the right point at a person's obligation (Personal — e.g. someone owes you money) or at a thing itself (Material — e.g. you own the land)? Incorporeal rights are the easiest to isolate: if nothing physical or another person's debt is involved — it's Incorporeal.
