L 1–3

The Islamic economy and economic systems

Definition and bases

  • Islamic economy: knowledge of the practical religious legislations regulating earning, spending and investing money, from detailed Shari'ah proofs.
  • Three aspects: ideological, theoretical/analytical, applied (policy).
  • Bases: money belongs to Allah and people are trustees; economic balance and social justice are obligatory; public and private property are both protected.

Three characteristics

  • Dual property: balance individual and community interest.
  • Restricted economic liberty: rules on earning and spending.
  • Social solidarity: zakat, charity, benevolence.
DRS: a doctor's prescription for a healthy economy.

L 03

The economic problem

Relative scarcity

Cause: impossibility of meeting all needs in the right time, place and form. In Islam it is a motive to work and a test from Allah.

  • Factors of production: work, capital, natural resources.

Malthus

Claimed population outgrows resources; proven wrong: resources renew (forests, fish) and alternatives emerge.

Real causes of starvation: unequal distribution, misuse of resources, exaggerated needs, spiritual crisis, divine test.

Commodity types

Durable consumer (fridge) · single-use consumer (food) · durable production (machines) · single-use production (raw iron, fuel) · dual-use (flour, electricity).

L 4–5

Public revenue, expenditure and zakat

Islamic revenue sources

  • Periodic: zakat (2.5%), jizyah (non-Muslim adult free sane males; exempts women, children, elderly, poor, monks), kharaj (land tax).
  • Non-periodic: khums (1/5 of spoils; 4/5 to fighters), fay' (from non-Muslims without fighting), 'ushur (customs on passing traders, first imposed by Omar), ownerless wealth, waqf.

Non-Islamic revenue

Taxes (largest source), fees for specific services, public loans (with interest → national debt), public sector revenue, fines and grants.

Expenditure through the eras

  • Prophet ﷺ: no Bayt al-Mal needed; immediate distribution.
  • Abu Bakr: same; fought those who refused zakat.
  • Omar: established Bayt al-Mal and the diwan as revenue grew.
  • Othman: more revenue, salary increases and gifts. Ali: development and land cultivation.
  • Rules of spending: beneficial use, economic use (no extravagance), authorization.

Zakat

  • 3rd pillar, decided in the 2nd year of Hijra; 2.5% (1/40).
  • Due when wealth ≥ nisab for a lunar year, after essential needs.
  • Nisab: 85 g gold or 595 g silver; use the lower value.
gold 1 g = 150 SR -> nisab = 85 x 150 = 12,750 SR
savings 40,000 SR >= nisab -> zakat = 40,000 / 40 = 1,000 SR

Eight recipients (At-Tawbah 9:60)

Poor · needy · zakat administrators · hearts to be reconciled · freeing captives · debtors · in the cause of Allah · stranded travellers.

"People Need Allah's Rewards, Freed Debtors Can Travel".
Zakat is the 3rd pillar, not the 4th: Shahada, Salat, Zakat, Sawm, Hajj.
L 7–8

Production and consumption

Work

Every intentional organized physical or mental effort to create or increase a legitimate benefit.

  • Work ethics: mastery (excellence), suitability, knowing workers' needs, sincerity, honesty.
  • The state provides jobs and training, supervises employer–employee relations, can compel necessary professions and ban harmful ones.

Capital and land

  • Partnership forms: both give capital and labour; one capital + one labour (mudarabah); both capital, one manages; one capital, both labour; solo trade.
  • Musaqah: a worker irrigates and tends crops for a known share. Muzara'ah: a worker cultivates land for a known share. The share must be known and from the whole produce.
  • Saving (with zakat, invested) is good; hoarding is forbidden.

Consumption

  • Rationalization: avoid taraf (luxury indulgence), israf (exceeding moderation), tabdhir (spending on wrongful things); moderation between miserliness and waste.
  • Economic liberty within Shari'ah; gratification is material and spiritual; the commodity must be halal.
L 9–10

Market ethics and pricing

Nine market rules

  1. Fair weights and measures.
  2. No riba.
  3. Honesty, no cheating ("he who cheats is not one of us").
  4. No false bidding (najsh).
  5. No meeting caravans before they reach the market.
  6. No selling what you do not own.
  7. No exploiting the buyer's ignorance or need.
  8. No monopoly (ihtikar).
  9. Leniency in buying, selling and demanding payment.

Pricing

  • Price up → demand down; price up → supply up.
  • Natural price movements are Allah's decree; the Prophet ﷺ refused to fix prices.
  • The ruler may fix prices when high prices come from monopoly, greed or cheating, fairly to both buyer and seller.
L 11–12

Money, riba and banks

Money

  • Stages: commodity money → metal (gold, silver) → paper receipts (goldsmiths) → fiat money. Gold standard abandoned in 1931.
  • Gold and silver are ideal: reasonable rarity, no corrosion, divisible, portable.
  • Fiat types: coins and banknotes (legal tender), deposit money (not legal tender).

Riba

  • Loan riba: a conditional increase for delay.
  • Sales riba: exchanging the same commodity with an increase.
  • Six ribawi goods: gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates, salt.

Banks

  • A bank deals in money and credit.
  • Central (issuing) bank: heads the system, issues currency. Specialized banks: finance one sector (real estate, agriculture, industry). Commercial banks: deposits, loans, securities.
  • Islamic banks replace interest with profit-and-loss sharing (mudarabah, musharakah), murabaha and leasing.
