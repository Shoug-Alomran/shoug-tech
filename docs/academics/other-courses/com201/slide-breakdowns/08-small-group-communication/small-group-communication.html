Why Learn About Small Groups

Small groups are the basic building blocks of society—families, work teams, support groups, religious circles, and study groups are all examples. Learning to communicate effectively in groups matters for four reasons: groups fulfill William Schutz's needs for inclusion, affection, and control (see Chapter 6); group work is the norm in business and industry, so group skills are highly valued by employers; being an effective group member is a learnable, masterable skill, not an innate talent; and participating in small groups builds confidence in articulating beliefs, which strengthens civic and democratic participation.

Defining Small-Group Communication

Key Term
Small-group communication — the interaction among three to nine people who are working together to achieve an interdependent goal.

This definition implies: the group must be small enough that members are mutually aware they form a collective entity (research shows groups of three or four are often more productive than groups of five or more); the interaction itself is what holds the group together; and members are interdependent—they cannot achieve their shared goal without each other.

Types and Functions of Small Groups

Classification Definition Example
Assigned group Individuals are appointed to be members. A student union advisory board.
Emergent group Individuals choose to form the group out of personal need or desire. A friend group that meets at college.
Task-oriented group Formed to complete a task, such as solving a problem or making a decision. Students studying together for an exam.
Relationship-oriented group Usually long-term; exists to meet needs for inclusion and affection. A family.

These categories often blur in practice—an assigned, task-oriented class group can develop strong relational bonds over time, and a relationship-oriented group like a family still has to complete tasks together.

Establishing Culture in Small Groups

Group Norms

Key Term
Norms — informal rules for interaction, created and sustained through communication, that guide how group members are expected to act.

Norms often develop rapidly and implicitly—without anyone consciously "deciding" on a rule. If a member arrives late to a meeting and no one objects, the group has implicitly signaled that punctuality is not strictly enforced. Groups reinforce norms through feedback, such as a leader reminding members to put devices away.

Group Roles

A role is a consistent pattern of interaction or behavior a member exhibits over time. Roles can be formal (an assigned or positional role, such as treasurer or note-taker) or informal (a behavioral role that develops naturally based on personality and interaction, such as the group member who naturally becomes the facilitator).

Task, Maintenance, and Self-Centered Functions

Function Type Purpose Example Behaviors
Task functions Directly relevant to the group's purpose; affect productivity. Initiating, information giving/seeking, opinion giving, clarifying, summarizing, coordinating.
Maintenance functions Support cooperative, harmonious relationships among members. Gatekeeping, supporting, harmonizing, relieving tension, showing solidarity.
Self-centered functions Serve an individual's needs at the group's expense. Withdrawing, blocking, seeking status/recognition.

Both task and maintenance functions are essential to effective group communication; self-centered functions, by contrast, use the group to serve personal goals that compete with the group's own.

Group Cohesiveness and Groupthink

A group's climate—its emotional tone—is shaped by three factors: trust (task trust and interpersonal trust that others are working toward the group's goals, not hidden agendas), supportiveness (openness in which members care about each other, echoing Gibb's supportive categories from Chapter 6), and cohesiveness (the attachment members feel toward each other and the group). Highly cohesive groups tend to be more open, handle disagreement more effectively, and typically perform better.

Groupthink

Key Term
Groupthink — a phenomenon in which the desire for cohesion and agreement overrides critical analysis and discussion, often leading to poor decisions.

Observable warning signs of groupthink include an illusion of invulnerability, an unquestioned belief in the group's morality, rationalizing away faulty decisions, stereotyping opposing viewpoints as weak or evil, self-censorship of dissenting opinions, direct pressure on members who express disagreement, and the emergence of "mind guards" who screen out contradictory information.

Preventing Groupthink
Seek all pertinent information, carefully assess the credibility of that information, assign members to argue counterpositions, and maintain a firm commitment to finding the best-supported outcome—even if it is not the most popular one in the room.

Diversity and Group Culture

Key Term
Group culture — the socially negotiated system of rules that guides group behavior; unlike national or ethnic culture, group culture is relatively unstable and dissolves when the group does.

Within-group diversity can be observable (visible differences such as able-bodiedness, perceived gender, or age) or implicit (unseen differences such as values, political beliefs, or educational background). Group polarization around identity differences can create "in-group" and "out-group" conflict; the common in-group identity model suggests finding and emphasizing what members have in common rather than focusing on differences.

Leadership in Small Groups

Group leaders must attend to four issues: identifying the group's task, creating cohesiveness, monitoring and adapting member behavior, and keeping the group focused. Leadership is the process of using communication to influence the behaviors and attitudes of others to meet group goals.

  • Designated leader — appointed or elected to a formal leadership role (chair, coordinator, facilitator).
  • Emergent leader — an informal leader who exerts influence without holding the formal role.

Sources of Power

Power Type Description
Reward power The ability to give followers what they want or need.
Punishment power The ability to withhold what followers want or need, including coercion.
Referent power Power based on others' admiration and respect (e.g., charisma).
Expert power Power that arises from others valuing a person's knowledge or expertise.
Legitimate power Power granted because of an official title, position, or role—most often used by designated leaders.

Leadership Styles

  • Democratic leaders encourage members to participate in group decisions.
  • Laissez-faire leaders take almost no initiative in structuring group discussion.
  • Autocratic leaders maintain strict control, making assignments and giving orders with little member input.

Most people in the U.S. report greater satisfaction in democratically led groups, but the "best" style depends on the situation—an urgent deadline may call for a more directive approach than a democratic one would allow.

Planning Effective Meetings

Effective leaders know the task at hand, know the people in the group, collect information in advance, distribute leadership when appropriate, and organize the discussion using a clear agenda (approval of past minutes, announcements, reports, new business, old business).

Problem Solving and Decision-Making

Groups are usually (though not always) better problem solvers than individuals because they combine more information and broader perspectives—but only when the process is systematic and organized.

Determining the Discussion Question

Question Type Asks Example
Questions of fact Whether something is true or can be verified. "How many first-generation students are enrolled?"
Questions of value Whether something is good/bad, better/worse. "Does our legal system provide justice for all?"
Questions of policy What action should be taken (the word "should" is stated or implied). "Should the state's drunk-driving laws be changed?"

Effective groups start with a problem question ("How can we reduce parking complaints?") rather than a solution question ("How can we increase parking spaces?"), because solution questions bias the group toward one option before alternatives are considered.

Identifying Criteria

Criteria are the standards used to judge potential solutions. Absolute criteria must be met (no leeway); important criteria should be met but allow flexibility. Solutions that fail absolute criteria are rejected outright; remaining options are ranked by how well they satisfy important criteria.

Brainstorming and Evaluating Solutions

Brainstorming works best when members defer all judgment—positive or negative—until every idea has been generated ("no evaluation" is the core rule), because critical evaluation during idea generation kills creativity. Only after ideas are exhausted does the group move to evaluating each option against its established criteria.

Other Functions of Groups

Beyond problem solving, groups also make routine decisions, effect broader change, negotiate conflict, foster creativity ("two heads are better than one"), and maintain ties among stakeholders—people with an interest in an organization's actions.

Technology and Group Communication

Tools such as shared documents, file-sharing platforms, project-management apps, video conferencing, and team-chat applications help groups collaborate, especially in remote or hybrid settings. Technology does not automatically improve group communication, however—computer-mediated groups must work harder to explicitly establish norms and structure, since those cues develop less naturally online than face-to-face.

Chapter Summary

  • Small-group communication involves three to nine interdependent people working toward a shared goal.
  • Groups can be assigned or emergent, and task-oriented or relationship-oriented—though these categories often overlap.
  • Group culture develops through norms and roles (formal/informal), expressed through task, maintenance, and self-centered functions.
  • Trust, supportiveness, and cohesiveness shape group climate; excessive cohesion risks groupthink, which has recognizable warning signs and prevention strategies.
  • Leadership can be designated or emergent, draws on five sources of power, and follows democratic, laissez-faire, or autocratic styles.
  • Systematic problem solving moves through determining a problem-focused discussion question, setting absolute/important criteria, brainstorming without judgment, and then evaluating solutions.
