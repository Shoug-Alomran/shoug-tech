Data Communications

Notes

  1. 1

    Data communication is the smallest picture in the course: two devices exchanging information over a transmission medium. The medium is whatever physically carries the signal: a copper cable, a glass fibre, or radio waves through the air. Everything else in the course (networks, the Internet) is many of these two-device exchanges put together. For it to be useful, the data must arrive at the right device, arrive correctly, and arrive on time.

Five components of data communication (Figure 1.1)

Notes

    Learn the five as answers to five questions. What is sent? The message. Who sends it? The sender. Who gets it? The receiver. Over what? The transmission medium. Following which rules? 1.1.2 Message

Notes

  1. 1

    A network can only carry bits, so every kind of information is turned into bits first. Text is stored as a bit pattern using a code such as Unicode (each character gets a number, and the number is sent in binary). Numbers are converted directly to binary. Images are a grid of pixels, and each pixel becomes a bit pattern (1 bit for black and white, more bits for colour). Audio is sound, which is continuous, so it is sampled into numbers. Video is many images shown quickly one after another (plus audio). The exam point: the form differs, but on the medium it is all bits.

Slide 6 / 32

1.2 Networks

Notes

  1. 1

    A node is any device on the network. It can be a host, a device people actually use (computer, printer, phone), or a networking device whose job is to move data (router, switch, modem). A link is the pathway between two nodes, and it can be a cable or a wireless channel. So a network is just nodes plus links.

  2. 2

    There are only two ways to use a link. Point-to-point: the link joins exactly two devices, and its full capacity belongs to them. Multipoint (also called multidrop): more than two devices share one link, so they must share its capacity, either at the same time (spatially) or by taking turns (temporally). Sharing is cheaper, but it means devices can get in each other’s way, which is why Chapter 3 needs multiple-access protocols.

  3. 3

    A network is judged on three criteria. Performance asks how fast: it is measured by throughput (how much data actually gets through per second) and delay (transit time across the network, and response time to a request). It depends on the number of users, the medium, the hardware and the software. Reliability asks how dependable: it is measured by the frequency of failure and the time a link takes to recover from one. Security asks how protected: keeping out unauthorised users, preventing damage to data, and having policies to recover from breaches and data loss. A common trap is mixing up the measures: failure frequency is reliability, not performance.

  4. 4

    Trap More throughput and less delay pull against each other: pushing more data in raises the delay.

  5. 5

    Memory trick Criteria = “PRS”: Performance, Reliability, Security.

Slide 9 / 32

Data flow (direction of data flowing in the link) (Figure 1.2)

Notes

  1. 1

    Simplex: data only ever travels in one direction, like a one-way street. One device only sends and the other only receives. Examples: a keyboard sending to a computer, a monitor receiving from it, a radio broadcast. The receiver can never answer on that channel.

  2. 2

    Half-duplex: both devices can send, but not at the same time. They take turns, and whoever is sending gets the entire capacity of the channel. A walkie-talkie is the standard example: you press the button, speak, say “over”, and only then can the other person reply. If both press at once, neither gets through.

  3. 3

    Full-duplex: both devices send and receive at the same time, like a phone call where both people can talk at once. The catch is that the channel’s capacity is shared between the two directions all the time, either because the link has two separate paths (one per direction) or because the capacity is divided. Tutorial 1 asks exactly this: “capacity shared by both directions at all times” means full-duplex.

  4. 4

    Memory trick Half-duplex = “over” on a walkie-talkie. Full-duplex = talking over each other on the phone.

Slide 10 / 32

Categories of topology (Figure 1.7)

Notes

  1. 1

    Topology is the shape of the network: how the devices and links are physically arranged, or how devices are interconnected. It matters because the shape decides how much cable you need, what happens when something breaks, and how easy it is to add a device. Each topology on the next slides is a different trade-off between cost and robustness.

  2. 2

    There are four basic topologies: mesh (everyone linked to everyone), star (everyone linked to a centre), bus (everyone on one shared cable) and ring (everyone linked to two neighbours in a loop). Real networks often combine them, which is called a hybrid topology (slide 15).

Slide 11 / 32

Fully connected mesh topology (for five devices) (Figure 1.8)

Notes

  1. 1

    In a fully connected mesh every device has its own dedicated link to every other device. To count the links: each of the N devices connects to the other N-1, which gives N(N-1), but that counts every link twice (A to B and B to A are the same cable), so divide by 2: N(N-1)/2. Each device needs one I/O port (one NIC) per link it has, so N-1 ports per device. Example: 5 devices need 5 x 4 / 2 = 10 links and 4 ports each.

  2. 2

    Why is mesh expensive? Because the number of cables and ports grows roughly with the square of the number of devices: 10 devices need 45 links, 50 devices need 1,225. Every link is a cable to buy, install and maintain, which is also why it is hard to install and update. What you get for that cost: dedicated links mean no traffic problems and better privacy (nobody else sees your link), a broken link only affects the two devices on it, and faults are easy to find. That is why mesh is used where reliability matters most, such as backbone links between main routers.

  3. 3

    Memory trick N = 8 → 8 × 7 / 2 = 28 links, 7 ports per device.

  4. 4

    Trap Ports per device is N-1, not N(N-1)/2.

Slide 12 / 32

Star topology (Figure 1.9)

Notes

  1. 1

    In a star, each device has a dedicated point-to-point link only to the central controller (a hub or switch), never directly to another device. To send from A to B, the data goes A to the hub, then hub to B. That is why it needs only N links for N devices, one per device, and each device needs only one port. It is cheaper than mesh, easy to install, and adding or removing a device means plugging one cable into the hub.

  2. 2

    Star is called robust because if one cable breaks, only the device on that cable is cut off; everyone else keeps working, and the broken link is easy to identify. But the whole network depends on the centre: if the hub or switch fails, nothing works. That is called a single point of failure. In exam answers, mention both: robust against link failures, not against hub failure.

  3. 3

    Trap Robust ≠ fault-proof: one bad link only takes out its device, but the hub failing takes out everything.

Slide 13 / 32

Bus topology (Figure 1.10)

Notes

  1. 1

    A bus is one long cable, called the backbone or main cable, running past all the devices. Each device connects to it through a short drop line attached with a tap. So for N devices you need 1 main cable + N drop lines. Signals travel along the whole backbone and every device hears every transmission. The ends of the cable need terminators so the signal doesn’t bounce back. Bus needs less cable than mesh or star because the backbone can take the most efficient route.

  2. 2

    The weakness is the shared backbone. A single break in the main cable stops all transmission, because the signal can no longer pass and it reflects off the break. Faults are hard to isolate since one cable serves everybody, and adding devices is hard because each tap weakens the signal and the backbone has a limited length. This is why the old shared-cable Ethernet LANs were replaced by star (switched) LANs.

  3. 3

    Tip Formulas for N devices: mesh N(N-1)/2 links and N-1 ports each · star N links · ring N links · bus 1 main cable + N drop lines.

Slide 14 / 32

Ring topology (Figure 1.11)

Notes

  1. 1

    In a ring, each device has a dedicated point-to-point link to exactly two neighbours, one on each side, forming a closed loop, so N devices need N links. Data travels around the ring in one direction, passing from device to device. Each device contains a repeater that regenerates the signal and passes it on if the data isn’t addressed to it. Faults are easy to find: if a device stops getting a signal within the expected time, it raises an alarm, so the break is located quickly.

  2. 2

    Ring is not robust: since data must travel around the loop, one broken link or one failed device can stop the whole ring (in a simple, one-direction ring). The usual fix is a dual ring, a second loop that runs in the other direction and takes over when the first one breaks. Compare with star: in a star, a broken cable cuts off one device; in a ring, it can cut off everyone.

1.3 The Internet

Notes

  1. 1

    The Internet is a collection of many different networks, LANs and WANs, joined by connecting devices and switching stations, that all run the TCP/IP protocol suite. TCP/IP is what makes it one Internet rather than many separate networks: it is the common language every connected network agrees to speak.

  2. 2

    For a host to count as being on the Internet, it needs three things: it must run TCP/IP, it must have an Internet (IP) address so others can reach it, and it must be able to send IP packets to other machines on the Internet. A computer on a private network with no IP route out is not on the Internet, even if it runs TCP/IP internally.

Internet Service Providers (ISPs)

Notes

  1. 1

    ISPs are arranged in tiers. Tier 1 ISPs are the backbones of the Internet: international, very few of them, they own their own links and routers across continents, and they connect directly to each other, so they never pay anyone to reach the rest of the Internet. Examples: Sprint, AT&T, Lumen.

  2. 2

    Tier 3 ISPs are local ISPs. They don’t have international links of their own; they take service from the national providers and sell access to homes and businesses. So traffic from your laptop typically climbs tier 3, tier 2, tier 1 and back down to reach a server abroad.

  3. 3

    Memory trick Tier number goes UP as you get closer to the customer: 1 = global, 2 = national, 3 = local.

1.4 Internet Standards

Notes

  1. 1

    In the early 1970s, IBM, Digital Equipment and other vendors each built their own networking architecture that only worked with their own computers. A company with IBM machines and DEC machines could not connect them without major translation software. The problem this created is the reason standards exist: devices from different makers have to agree on common rules.

  2. 2

    A protocol is a set of rules that controls how data is exchanged between two entities. An entity is anything that can send and receive information, such as an application (an email program, a browser). A system is the physical machine it runs on (a computer, a terminal, a remote sensor). Tutorial 1’s trap: “A protocol is the structure or format of the data” is false, because format (syntax) is only one part of a protocol; a protocol also covers meaning (semantics) and timing.

    Trap “A protocol is the structure or format of the data” ✗. That is only syntax, one element of a protocol.
