Data Link Layer: Multiple Access Protocols

How many stations share one link: random access (CSMA and its three persistence methods, CSMA/CD), controlled access (reservation, polling, token passing) and channelization (FDMA, TDMA, CDMA). The favourite questions compare them: who is fair, who can collide, and who is stuck at R/M even when alone on the link.

Slide 1 / 34

Data-Link Layer

Notes

  1. 1

    The whole deck answers one question: when many stations share one link, who gets to send, and when? There are three families of answer. Random access (the CSMA family): nobody is in charge and stations compete, so collisions can happen. Controlled access (reservation, polling, token passing): stations take turns, so there are no collisions. Channelization (FDMA, TDMA, CDMA): the channel is cut into pieces by frequency, time or code. Two letters come up throughout: R is the channel’s full rate in bps and M is the number of stations, so R/M is one station’s fair, equal share.

  2. 2

    Tip R = the channel rate, M = the number of stations. R/M is a fair equal share.

Slide 4 / 34

Multiple Access

Notes

  1. 1

    The problem with sharing: if two or more nodes transmit at the same time, their signals add together on the medium and both frames are destroyed. That is a collision, and the bandwidth used during it is wasted. So the nodes need some way to coordinate who sends when.

  2. 2

    The solution is a protocol that coordinates the active nodes. These are called Medium (or Multiple) Access Control protocols, MAC protocols, and they belong to the MAC sublayer, the lower part of the data link layer. (MAC here is the same “MAC” as in MAC address: both belong to this sublayer.)

Random Access

Notes

  1. 1

    In random access, no station is superior to another and none controls another: there is no master and no schedule. A station with a frame to send uses the link directly, following the protocol’s own rules for deciding whether to send right now. Because each station decides on its own, two may decide to send at the same moment, so collisions are possible. The protocols differ in how they reduce that risk and what they do after a collision.

Slide 8 / 34

Random Access – Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA)

Notes

  1. 1

    CSMA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access) means listen before you talk: a station senses the medium first, and only sends if nobody else is transmitting. It relies on the fact that in a LAN the propagation time is very small, so when one station starts sending, the others hear it almost immediately and wait.

  2. 2

    Sensing reduces collisions but cannot eliminate them. The reason is propagation delay: when station B starts sending, its signal takes some time to reach station C. During that time C hears silence, thinks the medium is free, and starts too, and the two frames collide somewhere in the middle (the figure shows this).

  3. 3

    So a collision can only happen if two stations start within one propagation time of each other. If C starts after B’s signal has reached it, C will hear B and wait.

  4. 4

    Trap “CSMA eliminates collisions” ✗. It only reduces them.

Slide 12 / 34

Nonpersistent CSMA

Notes

  1. 1

    Non-persistent CSMA: sense the medium. If it is idle, transmit. If it is busy, back off: wait a random amount of time without listening, then sense again. Non-persistent stations are deferential: they politely walk away from a busy line instead of waiting at it.

  2. 2

    Performance: because waiting stations pick different random delays, they are unlikely to return at the same moment, so there are fewer collisions. The cost is wasted bandwidth: when the current transmission ends, everyone may still be in the middle of their random wait, so the medium sits idle even though stations have frames to send. The longer the backoff, the more time is wasted.

Slide 13 / 34

1-persistent CSMA

Notes

  1. 1

    1-persistent CSMA was designed to avoid that idle time. Sense the medium. If it is idle, transmit immediately. If it is busy, keep listening continuously, and the moment it becomes idle, transmit immediately, with probability 1 (always).

  2. 2

    1-persistent stations are called selfish: they grab the channel as soon as they can. The weakness: if two or more stations were waiting for the same busy channel, they all see it go idle at the same instant and all send at once, so a collision is guaranteed. Under heavy load that happens all the time, which is why 1-persistent has the worst throughput on slide 16.

  3. 3

    Memory trick Non-persistent = polite (walks away). 1-persistent = selfish (stares at the line).

Slide 14 / 34

P-persistent CSMA

Notes

  1. 1

    p-persistent CSMA divides time into slots, each typically equal to the maximum propagation delay. Sense the medium. If it is idle, transmit with probability p, or with probability 1 - p wait one time slot and sense again. For example, with p = 0.25, a station sends in a given slot with a 25% chance and waits with a 75% chance, like rolling dice each slot.

  2. 2

    If the medium is busy, keep listening continuously until it is idle, then apply step 1 (the probability rule).

  3. 3

    It combines the two others’ strengths: it reduces collisions like non-persistent (waiting stations don’t all jump in at once, since each only sends with probability p) and reduces idle time like 1-persistent (stations keep listening instead of walking away).

Slide 17 / 34

CSMA/CD (Collision Detection)

Notes

  1. 1

    Plain CSMA has an inefficiency: once a collision happens, the stations involved keep sending their whole frames, even though those frames are already ruined, so the channel stays unusable until both finish. CSMA/CD (with Collision Detection) fixes this: the sender keeps listening while it transmits, and as soon as it detects a collision it stops transmitting, so far less channel time is wasted.

  2. 2

    CSMA/CD was the access method of classic shared Ethernet: a bus cable, or a star topology built around a hub (a hub repeats every signal to every port, so it behaves like a shared bus). Modern switched, full-duplex Ethernet doesn’t need it (Chapter 4).

Slide 19 / 34

CSMA/CD Protocol

Notes

  1. 1

    CSMA/CD uses one of the three persistence methods (non-, 1- or p-persistent) to decide when to send. If a station detects a collision while sending: (1) it aborts its transmission; (2) it sends a short jam signal (48 bits in these slides), which makes sure every station, including the farthest one, notices the collision and discards the damaged frame. (3) It backs off for a random amount of time (random, so the colliding stations don’t retry at the same moment and collide again); (4) then it tries again using the same persistence method. Order to remember: abort, jam, back off, retry.

  2. 2

    Memory trick CSMA/CD collision steps: “Abort, Jam, Back off, Retry.”

Slide 20 / 34

Performance of Random Access Protocols

Notes

  1. 1

    Advantages of random access: it is simple and easy to implement; it is decentralised, so there is no central device whose failure would bring down the whole network; and when traffic is low, frames get through with little delay. The downside appears under heavy traffic: collisions multiply and delay has no upper limit.

  2. 2

    It is also an unfair protocol: nothing guarantees a turn, so a station may keep losing and never get to send. On the other hand, if a node is the only one with frames, it can send continuously at the full channel rate R: nobody is competing, so nothing slows it down. If M nodes all want to send, collisions eat into the capacity, so each node’s average throughput is less than R/M, the share it would get from a perfect equal split with no collisions or overhead.

Controlled access (scheduling)

Notes

  1. 1

    Controlled access (scheduling) gives stations in-order access: every station gets its turn, so it is a fair protocol. Because only one station sends at a time, it eliminates collisions completely. The trade-off: each node is limited to R/M, even if it is the only one with data, because it still has to wait for its turn. There are three methods: reservation, polling and token passing. Compare with random access: random can collide and is unfair, but a lone station gets all of R; controlled never collides and is fair, but caps everyone at R/M.

  2. 2

    Trap Random = can collide, unfair, R when alone. Controlled = never collides, fair, R/M.

Slide 23 / 34

2- Polling

Notes

  1. 1

    Polling has two forms. In centralised polling, one device is the primary station and the rest are secondary stations; all data goes through the primary, and secondaries only send when the primary allows them.

  2. 2

    When the primary wants to send to a secondary, it sends a SELECT frame with that secondary’s address (to check it is ready), and then the data. When the primary is ready to receive, it sends a POLL frame to each secondary in turn, asking whether it has data: the secondary replies with data if it has some, or with a NAK (negative acknowledgement) if it has nothing. Polling can go in round-robin order or a predetermined order. Memory aid: select = the primary sends; poll = the primary collects. In distributed polling there is no primary. Stations follow a known polling order (set by the protocol); the station with the highest priority sends first and then passes the right to send to the next station on the list, and so on. Reservation and token passing are both examples of distributed polling.

  3. 3

    Memory trick SELect = primary SEnds. POLL = primary coLLects.

Slide 25 / 34

Token-passing network

Notes

  1. 1

    Token passing is a distributed polling system. A special small frame called the token travels around a (logical) ring from station to station. A station that wants to send must wait for a free token to reach it, then captures it: marks it busy, attaches its data and the destination address. The destination copies the data and the token is set back to free so the next station can use it. Since only the token holder can send, there are no collisions.

Slide 26 / 34

Token-passing procedure (Figure 13.13)

Notes

  1. 1

    The procedure: wait for the token; capture it; send frames as long as there is data left and the station’s allocated time hasn’t run out; then release the token to the next station. The time limit stops one station from keeping the token forever, which is what makes token passing fair.

Channelization: FDMA

Notes

  1. 1

    FDMA (Frequency Division Multiple Access): the medium is divided into M separate frequency bands, one per station. Each station transmits continuously on its own band, at an average rate of R/M. The drawback: a station is limited to R/M even when it is the only node with frames to send. The other bands sit empty, but it isn’t allowed to use them.

Slide 31 / 34

Channelization: TDMA

Notes

  1. 1

    TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access): the entire bandwidth is one channel whose capacity is shared in time between M stations, each owning one slot per cycle. A node must always wait for its own slot, even when it is the only one with frames; the other slots go unused. So, like FDMA, it is limited to an average of R/M even when alone. Random access is the only family here that lets a lone station use all of R.

  2. 2

    Trap FDMA, TDMA (and controlled access) cap a lone station at R/M; random access lets it use all of R.

Slide 32 / 34

Channelization: CDMA

Notes

  1. 1

    CDMA (Code Division Multiple Access): one channel carries all transmissions at the same time, on the same frequencies. Each station codes its data with its own unique code before sending, and a receiver uses the sender’s code to recover that station’s data from the mixture. It is like a room where several pairs talk at once in different languages: you can follow your own conversation because you only understand your own language.

  3. Separated by time.
  4. Separated by frequency.

Slide 33 / 34

Simple idea of communication with code (Figure 12.23)

Notes

  1. 1

    How the codes work: each station multiplies its data d by its code c and sends d·c. The channel carries the sum of everything: d1·c1 + d2·c2 + d3·c3 + d4·c4. A receiver that wants station 1’s data multiplies the sum by c1; the codes are chosen so that the other stations’ parts cancel out, leaving d1. That is how simultaneous transmissions are separated.

  3. Not needed.
  4. The other stations’ parts cancel.

FDMA vs. TDMA vs. CDMA
