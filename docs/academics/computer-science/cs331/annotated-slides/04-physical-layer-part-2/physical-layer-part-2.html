Slide 1 / 29

Physical Layer

Notes

  1. 1

    Transmission media come in two families. Guided (wired) media keep the signal inside a physical path: twisted pair, coaxial cable and fibre-optic cable. Unguided (wireless) media send the signal through the air: radio waves, microwaves and infrared.

  2. 2

    Exam questions on this deck are mostly “which medium fits this situation?” scenarios. For each medium, know what it is made of, one or two advantages, one or two disadvantages, and a typical use; for the wireless ones, also know the frequency range, whether it is omnidirectional or line-of-sight, and whether it passes through walls.

Slide 2 / 29

Transmission medium and the physical layer

Notes

  1. 1

    The transmission medium (or channel) is the physical path between the transmitter and the receiver: a cable or the air. It sits below the physical layer (some books call it layer 0) and is controlled by the physical layer, which decides what signals to put on it. So the medium itself isn’t a layer; it is what the bottom layer uses.

Slide 3 / 29

Classes of transmission media

Notes

  1. 1

    Guided media guide the signal along a physical conductor, so it goes only where the cable goes: twisted pair and coaxial (both copper, carrying electrical signals) and fibre-optic (glass or plastic, carrying light).

  2. 2

    Unguided media have no physical conductor: the signal spreads through the air (or space) and any device with a suitable receiver can pick it up. The three types are radio waves, microwaves and infrared, listed from lowest to highest frequency.

  3. 3

    Memory trick Guided = the signal is GUIDED along a physical path. Unguided = it just spreads through the air.

Slide 6 / 29

Twisted-pair cable

Notes

  1. 1

    A twisted pair is two insulated copper wires twisted around each other in a regular spiral. The two wires together form one communication link: one carries the signal and the other is the reference, and the receiver looks at the difference between them.

  2. 2

    Why twist them? To reduce electrical interference (noise and crosstalk) from other pairs close by and from outside sources. Twisting makes both wires pick up the same interference, which then cancels out at the receiver (slide 8 shows how). The tighter the twisting, the better the cancellation, so more twists per length means better quality.

  3. 3

    Twisted pair is used in the telephone network between a house and the local exchange (the subscriber loop) and in LANs, where it is the standard Ethernet cable.

Twist length: Category 3 vs. Category 5 UTP

Notes

Slide 8 / 29

Why twisting cancels interference

Notes

  1. 1

    (a) With two parallel wires, one wire is always closer to the noise source, so it picks up more noise than the other. The receiver reads the difference between the wires, and an unequal amount of noise leaves a leftover (+6 in the figure) that corrupts the signal. (b) With a twisted pair, the two wires keep swapping positions along the cable, so over its length both pick up the same total noise. When the receiver subtracts one from the other, the noise cancels (a difference of 0), and only the real signal remains.

Exam angle

PredictionQ1

Compare with the model answer

UTP and STP cables

Notes

  1. 1

    UTP is Unshielded Twisted Pair: the pairs sit directly inside a plastic cover. STP is Shielded Twisted Pair: the pairs are wrapped in a metal shield (foil or braided mesh) inside the plastic cover. The shield blocks outside electromagnetic interference before it reaches the wires.

Slide 10 / 29

STP vs. UTP

Notes

  1. 1

    STP: the metal braid or covering reduces interference, so it suits electrically noisy places such as factories. The price: it is more expensive and harder to handle, because it is thicker and heavier and the shield must be grounded properly.

  2. 2

    UTP: the cheapest cable and the easiest to install, which is why almost every office LAN uses it. The trade-off: it suffers from external electromagnetic interference (EMI). So the choice depends on the environment: an ordinary office uses UTP, a site full of motors or heavy equipment uses STP (or fibre).

  3. 3

    Trap UTP is not “worse” for every case: it is the default in LANs because it is cheap and easy.

Slide 11 / 29

Categories of unshielded twisted-pair cables

Notes

  1. 1

    The categories are standards for how good a twisted-pair cable is. A higher category supports a higher bandwidth (in MHz, how high a frequency it can carry) and so a higher data rate: CAT5e 100 MHz / 1 Gbps, CAT6 250 MHz / 1 Gbps, CAT6a 500 MHz / 10 Gbps, CAT7 600 MHz / 10 Gbps, CAT8 2000 MHz / 25 or 40 Gbps. CAT5e to CAT6a can be UTP or STP.

  2. 2

    From CAT7 upward the cable must be shielded. At such high frequencies, interference and crosstalk are too strong for unshielded pairs. Even then, the top speed only works over short runs: shielded CAT8 reaches 40 Gbps only up to about 24 m, which is why it is used inside data centres.

The RJ-45 connector pinout

Notes

  1. 1

    In 10 and 100 Mbps Ethernet, only four of the eight pins carry data. Pins 1 and 2 transmit: TD+ and TD- (Transmit Data positive and negative). Pins 3 and 6 receive: RD+ and RD-. Each signal uses a pair (+ and -) because the receiver reads the difference between the two wires, as in slide 8.

  2. 2

    Pins 4, 5, 7 and 8 are N/C, meaning No Connection or not connected: unused in 10/100 Ethernet. Gigabit Ethernet uses all eight pins, sending on all four pairs at once.

Slide 14 / 29

Crossover cable

Notes

  1. 1

    A crossover cable connects one end’s transmit pins (1, 2) to the other end’s receive pins (3, 6), and the other way round: 1 to 3, 2 to 6, 3 to 1, 6 to 2. That way, what one device sends lands on the other device’s receive pins.

Slide 15 / 29

Ethernet cabling: straight-through vs. crossover

Notes

  1. 1

    Straight-through cables wire each pin to the same pin at the other end (1 to 1, 2 to 2, 3 to 3, 6 to 6). They connect different kinds of device: a PC to a switch or hub, or a router to a switch or hub. This works because a switch already swaps transmit and receive internally, so the PC’s transmit pins meet the switch’s receive pins.

  2. 2

    Crossover cables connect the same kind of device: PC to PC, hub to hub, switch to switch, hub to switch. Two identical devices both transmit on pins 1 and 2, so a straight cable would connect transmit to transmit and nothing would be received; the crossover swaps the pairs. Memory rule: unlike devices use straight, like devices use cross. (Many modern ports detect and swap automatically, a feature called auto-MDIX, but exams expect the rule.)

  3. 3

    Memory trick “Unlike → straight, like → cross.” (Router ↔ PC counts as “like”: both are end devices.)

Coaxial cable

Notes

  1. 1

    Coaxial cable has a central copper wire (the inner conductor) inside a layer of insulation, surrounded by an outer conductor of copper mesh (braid or foil), and an outer plastic jacket. The outer conductor completes the circuit and shields the inner wire from interference, so coax carries higher frequencies than twisted pair. It is used for cable TV and was used in the old bus Ethernet (10Base5 thick coax and 10Base2 thin coax).

Slide 17 / 29

Optical Fiber

Notes

  1. 1

    An optical fibre has three layers from the inside out. The core is one or more very thin strands of glass or plastic, and it is what carries the light. Around the core is the cladding, glass or plastic with different optical properties from the core (a lower refractive index). That difference makes light that hits the boundary bounce back into the core instead of escaping. The outer jacket is plastic that protects the fibre from moisture, crushing and other damage.

Slide 19 / 29

Fiber Optics Properties

Notes

  1. 1

    Fibre’s advantages: higher bandwidth (data rates of hundreds of Gbps); smaller size and lighter weight than copper; lower attenuation, so repeaters can be much farther apart, at least 50 km compared with about 100 m for UTP; no crosstalk, since no light leaks from one fibre to another; it is not affected by electromagnetic noise, because light isn’t disturbed by electrical fields; and it is highly secure, because no light leaks out for someone to tap.

  2. 2

    Its disadvantages: installing it needs special skills (fibres have to be cut, polished and joined precisely), and it is more expensive (the cable, connectors and equipment). A common exam trap lists “cheap” or “easy to install” as advantages of fibre; they are its disadvantages.

  3. 3

    Trap Fiber is NOT cheaper or easier to install. Those are its two disadvantages.

Slide 24 / 29

Radio waves

Notes

  1. 1

    Radio waves are below 300 MHz. They are omnidirectional: the antenna sends the signal out in all directions, so the sending and receiving antennas don’t need to be lined up. They can travel long distances and pass through walls. That makes them suited to broadcasting: AM and FM radio and television, where one transmitter reaches everyone in the area. The downside is that anyone can receive them and nearby transmitters on the same frequency interfere.

Slide 26 / 29

Microwaves

Notes

  1. 1

    Microwaves run from 300 MHz to 300 GHz, the upper part of the radio range.

  2. 2

    Microwaves are unidirectional: they travel in a straight line, so the antennas must see each other, which is called line-of-sight propagation. That makes them good for point-to-point links, for example between two buildings to connect their LANs, with a dish on each roof pointing at the other. Because they are high frequency, they have a short wavelength and have trouble passing through walls: higher frequency means shorter wavelength, which means more difficulty getting through obstacles.

  3. 3

    Trap Radio is OMNIdirectional; microwave is UNIdirectional (line of sight).

Slide 28 / 29

Infrared (IR Wireless)

Notes

  1. 1

    Infrared runs from 300 GHz to 400 THz, just below visible light. It has a very large bandwidth, but it cannot penetrate walls, so it only works for short-distance, local communication, within one room. It also needs line of sight (directional): the remote has to point at the TV.

  2. 2

    It is used for local point-to-point links: remote controls, some wireless headphones and some wireless computer peripherals. Devices use the IrDA (Infrared Data Association) protocol. It cannot be used under the sun, because sunlight contains a lot of infrared that interferes with the signal. For most device-to-device links it has been replaced by Bluetooth, which uses radio and doesn’t need line of sight.

PredictionQ1

Compare radio waves, microwaves and infrared by direction and typical use.

Compare with the model answer

Radio (below 300 MHz): omnidirectional, long range, multicast such as radio and TV. Microwave (300 MHz to 300 GHz): unidirectional line of sight, unicast such as cellular, satellite and wireless LANs. Infrared (300 GHz to 400 THz): line of sight, short range inside a room, such as remote controls.

PredictionQ2

An infrared signal in one room will not interfere with an infrared signal in the next room.

  1. Infrared can’t pass through walls.
  2. The statement is true: because infrared stays in its room, it doesn’t reach the next one.
