Signals: data must become electromagnetic signals

4
Exam weight ●●○ Medium
  1. Data can’t travel as-is: it must be turned into an electromagnetic signal (voltage on copper, light in fiber, radio in air).
🎯 How this can come up · 1 question
True / False

To be transmitted, data must be transformed to electromagnetic signals.

True.

A Transmission System

Transmission: communication of data by propagation and processing of signals through a communication channel

Signal: electromagnetic energy that moves through the transmission medium and carries some information.①

Transmitter

  • Converts information into a signal suitable for transmission
  • Injects energy into the communications medium or channel
    • Telephone converts voice into electric current
    • NIC converts bits into electric current or pulses of light②

Receiver

  • Receives energy from the medium
  • Converts the received signal into a form suitable for delivery to the user
    • Telephone converts current into voice
    • NIC converts electric current or pulses of light into bits③
5
Exam weight ●○○ Low
  1. Signal = electromagnetic energy carrying information.
  2. The NIC is both: transmitter (bits → current/light) and receiver (current/light → bits).

Analog and Digital

  • Analog Data : Continuous value data (sound, light, temperature)①
  • Digital Data: Discrete value (text, integers, symbols)
  • Analog Signals : Continuously varying electromagnetic wave
    • have an infinite number of values over a period of time
  • Digital Signals : Series of voltage pulses (square wave)
    • have only a limited number of values over a period of time.②
    • Maintains a constant level then changes to another constant level
  • Signals are classified into:
    • Periodic Signals : Consists of repeated patterns over identical period of times (cycles)
    • Aperiodic Signals : change without a pattern that repeats over time③
6
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. Analog = infinitely many values (continuous). Digital = a limited set of levels (discrete, square wave).
  2. Periodic = repeats in identical cycles. Aperiodic = no repeating pattern.
Exam trapAnalog/digital is about the VALUES; periodic/aperiodic is about the PATTERN over time. They are separate classifications.

Note: frequency

Frequency is the rate of change with respect to time.

Change in a short span of time means high frequency.①

Change over a long span of time means low frequency.②

9
Exam weight ●○○ Low
  1. Fast changes = high frequency. Slow changes = low frequency.
  2. A signal that never changes has frequency 0.

Note: frequency and period

Frequency and period are the inverse of each other: f = 1 / T and T = 1 / f①

10
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. f in hertz (cycles per second), T in seconds.
Memory trickUnits pair up: s ↔ Hz, ms ↔ kHz, µs ↔ MHz, ns ↔ GHz.

MCQ

An analog signal has a frequency of 5 kHz. Its period is:

Tutorial 3, Q5: T = 1/5000 = 0.0002 s = 200 µs.

Written

An analog signal has a frequency of 5 kHz. What is its period, and how are frequency and period related?

They are inverses: T = 1/f. T = 1/5000 Hz = 0.0002 s = 200 µs.

Example

The power we use at home has a frequency of 60 Hz. The period of this sine wave is:

T = 1/f = 1/60 = 0.0166 s = 0.0166 × 10³ ms = 16.6 ms

1 second = 1000 ms①

11
Exam weight ●○○ Low
  1. Multiply seconds by 1000 to get ms.

Example

The period of a signal is 100 ms. What is its frequency in kilohertz?

Step 1: convert to seconds: 100 ms = 100/1000 = 0.1 s

Step 2: f = 1/T = 1/0.1 = 10 Hz

Step 3: convert to kHz: 10/1000 = 0.01 kHz①

Simple and composite signals

Signals that have just one frequency are called simple signals: for example, the sine signal (each cycle takes the same time)①

Signals that are composed of multiple frequencies are called composite signals (each cycle takes different time)②

13
Exam weight ●●○ Medium
  1. Simple = one sine wave = one frequency. Composite = many sine waves added together.

Bit rate and bit interval

Bit interval (digital signal period): the time required to send (represent) one single bit (time units)①

Bit rate (digital bandwidth) = transmission speed = link capacity: number of bits sent per second (bps)②

16
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. Bit interval = time for ONE bit. Bit rate = bits per second. Bit rate = 1 / bit interval.

Example: bit interval

A digital signal has a bit rate of 2000 bps. What is the duration of each bit (bit interval)?

The bit interval is the inverse of the bit rate.

Bit interval = 1/2000 s = 0.000500 s = 0.000500 × 10⁶ µs = 500 µs① (microseconds)

Attenuation

  • Attenuation: Loss of energy due to resisting the

medium (Signal strength falls off with distance)①

  • Increases with signal frequency
  • Ex. A wire carrying electrical signal becomes warm after some time②
  • Amplifiers (analog signals) and repeaters (digital signals) are used to handle attenuation③
21
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. Attenuation = LOSS OF ENERGY (the wire heats up). Worse with distance and with higher frequency.
  2. Fix: amplifiers for analog, repeaters for digital (they regenerate a clean copy).
Exam trapAmplifiers boost noise too; repeaters regenerate the original bits.

Written

What does a repeater do and why is it needed in digital transmission?

It receives a weakened or distorted digital signal, regenerates it and sends a clean copy on. Signals lose strength over distance (attenuation), so repeaters let digital data travel further without losing its form.

Distortion

  • Distortion: Signal changes in shape
  • Example: In a video call, distorted images or audio delays may result from signal distortion during transmission.①

Noise

  • Thermal Noise due to random motion of electrons in a wire which will create an extra signal①
  • Induced Noise: caused by motors and electrical equipments.②
  • crosstalk noise: Two wires beside each others (hearing another conversation in the background while talking with the phone)③
  • Impulse noise: irregular pulses or noise spikes of short duration and high amplitude
    • caused by power lines or lightning
    • Very critical in case of digital signals (primary source of error in digital data communication)④
23
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. Thermal: random motion of electrons. Induced: motors and electrical equipment.
  2. Crosstalk: one wire picks up a neighbour’s signal (hearing another call).
  3. Impulse: short high spikes from power lines or lightning; the main error source for digital data.
Memory trickCrosstalk = “cross talk”: someone else’s conversation crossing into yours.

🎯 How this can come up · 3 questions
Scenario

On a phone call you hear a faint conversation from another call. Which impairment is this, and under which category?

Crosstalk, which is a kind of noise: a signal from one line leaking into another nearby line.

Scenario

A long-distance link has many errors because of nearby electrical equipment. Which impairment, and how would you reduce it?

Induced noise (electromagnetic interference), a kind of noise. Reduce it with shielded or twisted-pair cable, by moving cables away from the equipment, by using fiber, and with error detection.

MCQ

Which noise is the primary source of error in digital data communication?

Correct: Impulse noise.

Performance: propagation time and speed

  • Propagation time = time required for a bit to move between two nodes.①
    • Propagation time = distance of the link (between the two nodes) / propagation speed
  • Propagation speed: speed of light in the medium.
  • Depends on the medium
    • Light travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum (free space), lower in the air and much lower in a cable (2/3 of vacuum)②
24
Exam weight ●●● High
  1. Propagation time = distance / speed. It does NOT depend on the message size.
  2. Speed: 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum, about 2 × 10⁸ m/s in cable.
Exam trapConvert km to m before dividing by m/s.

    Other Physical layer definitions – Transmission Time

    • Transmission time = the time it takes the sender to put all the bits of a frame onto the link (from NIC to medium)①
      • Transmission time (s) = length of frame (message) in bits / bandwidth (bit rate) (bps)
    • Queuing time = time needed for each intermediate device to hold the message before it can be processed.②
    • Latency (delay): total message delivery time = transmission time + propagation time + processing time + queuing time③
    26
    Exam weight ●●● High
    1. Transmission time = size / bandwidth: depends on message size, not distance.
    2. Latency = propagation + transmission + queuing + processing.
    Memory trickPropagation = how FAR. Transmission = how BIG. Queuing = how BUSY.
    Exam trap1 MB = 8 Mb: multiply bytes by 8 first.

    MCQ

    Which delay depends on the size of the message?

    Correct: Transmission time.

    Written

    List the four components of latency.

    Propagation time + transmission time + queuing time + processing time.

    Example

    What are the propagation time and the transmission time for a 2.5-kbyte message (an e-mail) if the bandwidth of the network is 1 Gbps? Assume that the distance between the sender and the receiver is 12,000 km and that light travels at 2.4 × 10⁸ m/s.

    27
    Exam weight ●○○ Low
    1. Short message + fast link: guess which delay wins before you calculate.

    Example (continued)

    Propagation time = distance / propagation speed = (12,000 × 1000) / (2.4 × 10⁸) = 50 ms①

    Transmission time = message size in bits / bandwidth = (2500 × 8) / 10⁹ = 0.020 ms②

    Note that in this case, because the message is short and the bandwidth is high, the dominant factor is the propagation time, not the transmission time. The transmission time can be ignored.

    28
    Exam weight ●●○ Medium
    1. Small message on a fast link: propagation dominates.

      ★ Tutorial

      Bandwidth-delay product (Figure 2.7)

      We can think about the link between two points as a pipe. The cross section of the pipe represents the bandwidth, and the length of the pipe represents the delay. We can say the volume of the pipe defines the bandwidth-delay product.①

      bandwidth-delay product = length of the link in bits

      • Bandwidth = how wide the pipe is → how many bits can enter per second.
      • Delay = how long the pipe is → how long a bit takes to travel from one end to the other.
      • Bandwidth-delay product = how many bits fit inside the pipe.②
      32
      Exam weight ●●● High
      1. BDP = bandwidth × delay = how many bits are “in the pipe” at once.

      • Wavelength: the distance a simple signal can travel in one period (or the distance occupied by one cycle)①
