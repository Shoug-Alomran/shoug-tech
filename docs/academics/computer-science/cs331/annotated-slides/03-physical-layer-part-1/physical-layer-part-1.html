Physical Layer

Notes

  1. 1

    The physical layer defines the physical characteristics of the interfaces and the media: how devices are connected to each other and to the link (topology), the shape, size and number of pins on connectors, the voltages and currents, the type of medium (copper, fibre, wireless) and the transmission mode (simplex, half- or full-duplex). You saw this list in Chapter 1 Part 2; this chapter goes into detail.

  2. 2

    Three more duties. Representation of bits: bits are encoded into electrical or optical signals. Data rate (transmission rate, also called bandwidth in this course): how many bits are sent per second. Synchronisation of bits: the sender’s and receiver’s clocks must match, so the receiver reads each bit at the right instant.

  3. 3

    Devices that work at this layer: the network card (its signalling part), the hub and the repeater. They handle signals only; they never read an address.

    Seen again Ch 1 Part 2, slide 26: First look at the physical layer

A Transmission System

Notes

  1. 1

    Transmission is communicating data by sending signals through a channel. A signal is electromagnetic energy that moves through the medium and carries information. The basic system has three parts: a transmitter, a communication channel and a receiver.

  2. 2

    The transmitter turns information into a signal suited to the medium and injects energy into the channel: a telephone turns your voice into electric current, a NIC turns bits into electric current or light pulses. The receiver takes the energy from the medium and turns it back into a usable form: the phone turns current back into sound, the NIC turns current or light back into bits. A NIC is both a transmitter and a receiver.

Slide 6 / 35

Analog and Digital

Notes

  1. 1

    Analog data is continuous: it can take any value in a range, like the exact loudness of a sound or the temperature. Digital data is discrete: it takes separate values, like text characters or whole numbers. The same split applies to signals. An analog signal is a smoothly varying wave that can have an infinite number of values in a range over time. A digital signal is a series of voltage pulses (a square wave) with only a limited number of values: it stays at one constant level, then jumps to another.

  2. 2

    Separately, a signal can be periodic, meaning it repeats the same pattern over identical time periods called cycles, or aperiodic, meaning it changes without a repeating pattern. These are two different classifications: analog/digital is about which values the signal can take, periodic/aperiodic is about whether its pattern repeats over time. So a signal can be periodic analog, aperiodic digital, and so on.

  3. 3

    Trap Analog/digital is about the VALUES; periodic/aperiodic is about the PATTERN over time. They are separate classifications.

Note: frequency

Notes

  1. 1

    Frequency is how fast a signal changes over time: the number of complete cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). If the signal changes a lot in a short time, the frequency is high.

  2. 2

    If the signal changes slowly, over a long time, the frequency is low. Two extremes: a signal that never changes at all has a frequency of zero, and a signal that jumps instantly from one level to another has (in theory) an infinite frequency.

Slide 10 / 35

Note: frequency and period

Notes

  1. 1

    Frequency and period are inverses: f = 1/T and T = 1/f. The period T is the time one cycle takes, in seconds; the frequency f is the number of cycles per second, in hertz. If one cycle takes 0.5 s, then 2 cycles fit into a second, so f = 2 Hz. Tutorial 3, Question 5 asks exactly this: f = 5 kHz = 5,000 Hz, so T = 1/5,000 = 0.0002 s = 200 µs.

  2. 2

    Memory trick Units pair up: s ↔ Hz, ms ↔ kHz, µs ↔ MHz, ns ↔ GHz.

Simple and composite signals

Notes

  1. 1

    A simple signal is a single sine wave, so it has exactly one frequency: every cycle is identical and takes the same time. A composite signal is made of several sine waves with different frequencies (and possibly different amplitudes and phases) added together, so its cycles don’t all look the same. Real signals, such as voice or a digital bit stream, are composite. This is Tutorial 3, Question 8.

Slide 15 / 35

Two digital signals, one with two and one with four bit-levels

Notes

  1. 1

    A digital signal can use more than two levels. With 2 levels, each level carries 1 bit (low = 0, high = 1), so 8 levels sent in one second carry 8 bits: 8 bps. With 4 levels, each level carries 2 bits (00, 01, 10, 11), so 8 levels in a second carry 16 bits: 16 bps. The rule: with L levels, each level carries log₂L bits. 8 levels carry 3 bits, 16 levels carry 4. More levels means more bits per second without changing how fast the signal changes level.

Slide 16 / 35

Bit rate and bit interval

Notes

  1. 1

    Two measures for digital signals. The bit interval is the time needed to send one bit, the digital version of the period. The bit rate is the number of bits sent per second (bps), the digital version of frequency. It is also called digital bandwidth, transmission speed or link capacity. They are inverses: bit rate = 1/bit interval. Tutorial 3, Question 1: bit interval = 50 µs = 0.00005 s, so bit rate = 1/0.00005 = 20,000 bps = 20 Kbps.

Advantages of Digital Transmission

Notes

  1. 1

Slide 21 / 35

Attenuation

Notes

  1. 1

    Attenuation is a loss of energy. As the signal travels, the medium resists it, and some of the energy is turned into heat (the slide’s example: a wire carrying a signal gets warm). So the signal gets weaker with distance. It also increases with frequency: higher-frequency signals lose strength faster, which is one reason high-speed links need shorter cables or better media.

  2. 2

    The fix depends on the signal. Amplifiers are used for analog signals: they boost the signal, but they also boost any noise in it. Repeaters are used for digital signals: they read the weak signal, work out which bits it carried, and regenerate a clean new copy. Tutorial 3, Question 2 asks what a repeater does and why it is needed: it regenerates a weakened or distorted digital signal so the data can travel farther without losing its form.

  3. 3

    Trap Amplifiers boost noise too; repeaters regenerate the original bits.

Slide 23 / 35

Noise

Notes

  1. 1

    Noise is unwanted energy that gets added to the signal. Thermal noise comes from the random motion of electrons in the wire, which creates an extra signal that was never sent; it is always present. Induced noise comes from outside sources such as motors and electrical equipment (like a microwave or a large machine), which act as antennas sending interference into the cable. Tutorial 3, Question 9 is this case: errors caused by nearby electrical equipment are induced noise.

  2. 2

    Crosstalk is the effect of one wire on another: two wires lying beside each other, and one picks up the signal of the other. It is like hearing another conversation in the background of your phone call (Tutorial 3, Question 6). Twisting the wire pairs, as in twisted-pair cable, reduces it.

  3. 3

    Impulse noise is irregular, short spikes with high amplitude, caused by power lines or lightning. It is very critical for digital signals, and it is the primary source of errors in digital data communication, because a single spike can flip a whole run of bits.

  4. 4

    Memory trick Crosstalk = “cross talk”: someone else’s conversation crossing into yours.

Slide 24 / 35

Performance: propagation time and speed

Notes

  1. 1

    Propagation time is the time one bit needs to travel from one node to the other: propagation time = distance / propagation speed. It depends only on how far the bit goes and how fast signals move in the medium. It does not depend on the size of the message or the bandwidth.

  2. 2

    The propagation speed is the speed of light in the medium: 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum, a little less in air, and much less in a cable, about 2/3 of that, so roughly 2 × 10⁸ m/s. Tutorial 3, Question 4: 2,000 km = 2,000,000 m, so 2,000,000 / (2 × 10⁸) = 0.01 s = 10 ms. Always convert km to m before dividing by m/s.

  3. 3

Slide 26 / 35

Other Physical layer definitions – Transmission Time

Notes

  1. 1

    Transmission time is the time the sender needs to push all the bits of a frame onto the link, from the NIC into the medium: transmission time = message size in bits / bandwidth (bps). It depends on how big the message is and how fast the link is, not on distance. Tutorial 3, Question 7: 1 MB = 8 Mb (multiply bytes by 8), and 8 Mb / 1 Mbps = 8 s.

  2. 2

    Queuing time is the time each intermediate device (router, switch) holds the message in a queue before it can process and forward it. It depends on how busy the device is, so it changes all the time. Latency (delay) is the total time to deliver a message = transmission time + propagation time + processing time + queuing time. A simple way to remember what each term depends on: propagation = how far, transmission = how big, queuing = how busy, processing = how much work each device does.

  3. 3

    Memory trick Propagation = how FAR. Transmission = how BIG. Queuing = how BUSY.

  4. 4

    Trap 1 MB = 8 Mb: multiply bytes by 8 first.

Slide 32 / 35

Bandwidth-delay product (Figure 2.7)

Notes

  1. 1

    Think of the link as a pipe. Its cross-section (how wide it is) is the bandwidth: how many bits can enter per second. Its length is the delay: how long a bit takes to get from one end to the other. Its volume, bandwidth × delay, is the bandwidth-delay product: the number of bits that fit inside the link at once, before the first one arrives. Tutorial 3, Question 10: 5 bps × 5 s = 25 bits. A 10 Mbps link with a 2 ms delay holds 10⁷ × 0.002 = 20,000 bits. This matters later because a sender should keep that many bits in flight to use the link fully.

Other Physical layer definitions - Wavelength

Notes

  1. 1

    Wavelength is the distance a simple signal travels in one period, or the distance one cycle occupies in the medium. It depends on the frequency and on the medium (through its propagation speed): wavelength = propagation speed × period = propagation speed / frequency. Example: a 1 MHz signal at 2 × 10⁸ m/s has a wavelength of 2 × 10⁸ / 10⁶ = 200 m. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
