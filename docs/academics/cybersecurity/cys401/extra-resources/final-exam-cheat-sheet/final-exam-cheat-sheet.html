CH 01–02

Introduction, foundations, CIA and attacks

Recap only. These two chapters supply about a fifth of the MCQs and most of the scenario questions. The full version with past questions is in the Chapters 1–5 sheet.

Seven critical characteristics of information

Confidentiality, integrity, availability, accuracy, authenticity, utility, possession.

"CIA + AAUP": the triad, then Accuracy, Authenticity, Utility, Possession.
A confidentiality breach always breaks possession. A possession breach does not always break confidentiality (a stolen laptop that is still encrypted).

Five components of an information system

  • Software: hardest to secure
  • Hardware
  • Data: most valuable asset
  • People: weakest link
  • Procedures

McCumber Cube (3 dimensions)

  • Goals: confidentiality, integrity, availability
  • States: storage (at rest), transmission (in transit), processing
  • Controls: policy, education, technology
A "correct the false statement" question swaps a state for a control. "Encryption" is a technology, not a state.

SDLC vs SecSDLC

Planning (investigation), analysis, design (logical/physical), implementation, maintenance/support. The SecSDLC follows the same phases but also identifies specific threats and the controls that counter them.

Defense in depth layers

Physical, personnel, operations, communications, network, data/information security. If one layer fails, the next one still protects.

Three pillars and four protection mechanisms

  • Pillars: people, policies (process), technology. Data is what they protect, not a pillar.
  • Protection mechanisms: layering, abstraction, data hiding, encryption.

CIA triad: attacks and methods (repeated topic)

PropertyAttacks / threatsMethods to ensure it
ConfidentialityPassword theft, shoulder surfing, sniffing, port scanning, privilege escalation, social engineeringEncryption, access control, 2FA, biometrics, tokens, steganography, air gaps
IntegrityUnauthorized modification, malware altering files, man-in-the-middlePermissions, version control, checksums and hashing, digital signatures, backups
AvailabilityDoS/DDoS, device failure, software error, disasters, ransomwareOff-site backups, redundancy, firewalls and WAF, disaster recovery

Threat damage types (Ch 2)

  • Interruption: asset unusable or unavailable (availability)
  • Interception: unauthorized access (confidentiality)
  • Modification: tampering (integrity)
  • Fabrication: counterfeit objects (authenticity/integrity)

Vocabulary pairs

  • Vulnerability: a weakness
  • Threat: circumstances that could cause harm
  • Attack: exploiting a vulnerability
  • Control: removes or reduces a vulnerability
  • Risk: threat × vulnerability (likelihood of harm)

AAA (five elements)

Identification (claim) → authentication (prove) → authorization (permissions) → auditing (record) → accounting/accountability (review logs, tie actions to a person).

Malware

Virus (attaches, needs a host), worm (self-spreads), Trojan (looks legitimate), spyware/keylogger, ransomware (WannaCry, NotPetya), adware, rootkit. Symptoms: high CPU, slowness, crashes, strange files or pop-ups. Countermeasures: antivirus with current definitions, no untrusted attachments, patching, backups.

Threat modeling, information assets and governance

Recap. Due care vs due diligence (13 practice questions) and data destruction (9) are the most-tested items here.

Threat modeling

  • Proactive (defensive): during design, preferred. Reactive (adversarial): after deployment or an incident.
  • Steps: identify, analyze, determine impact, define controls (also: visualize, identify, validate; execution comes after).
  • Models: STRIDE, PASTA (risk-centric, controls weighed against asset value), LINDDUN (privacy), CVSS, attack trees, Persona non Grata, OCTAVE.
  • Risk elements: threat, vulnerability, asset, damage.

Security plans (repeated)

PlanHorizonAnswers
Strategic3–5 yearsWhy: mission and goals
Tactical~1 yearWhat controls: projects, hiring, budget
OperationalMonthly/quarterlyHow it runs day to day

Governance vs management (repeated)

  • Governance: strategic oversight, "are we doing the right things?" Board and senior leadership.
  • Management: tactical execution, "are we doing things right?" CISO, managers, analysts.
  • Top-down approach: senior management starts policy and sets acceptable risk; the CISO reports to senior management.

Due care vs due diligence

  • Due care: doing the reasonable protective thing (patching, password policy, building the security structure).
  • Due diligence: investigating and keeping it working (risk assessments, audits, vendor checks, monitoring).
  • Both protect against negligence. Care sets it up, diligence keeps checking.
Final A Q21 wanted a definition of each, an example of each, and the relation between them.

Classification

  • Government: Top Secret (exceptionally grave damage), Secret (serious), Confidential (damage), Unclassified.
  • Commercial: Confidential/Restricted, Private/Internal, Sensitive, Public.
  • Criteria: business value, legal/regulatory, reputational, operational impact.
"Critical" is not a government level (Final A Q4).

Data destruction, weakest → strongest

Erasing (delete, recoverable) → formatting → clearing (overwrite) → purging (repeated overwrite, degaussing) → destruction (shred, incinerate). Declassification = releasing media to a lower level.

Degaussing works on magnetic HDDs and tapes, not SSDs. To reuse an HDD with nothing recoverable: degauss (Final A Q19).

Data roles and data states

  • Owner (dept head): classifies, approves access. Steward: data quality. Custodian (IT): implements controls. User: follows the rules. System owner: system security plan.
  • At rest: AES-256, access control, physical. In transit: TLS/VPN, monitoring. In use: access control, endpoint security, memory protection.
  • Laws: GDPR (EU), CCPA/CPRA, HIPAA (PHI), GLBA (finance), Saudi PDPL (SDAIA, fines up to SAR 5 million).
CH 05

Classical and symmetric cryptography

Formula recap. The worked Playfair, Hill and transposition examples are in the Chapters 1–5 sheet and the Cipher Cheat Sheet.

Formulas to write from memory

Caesar      C = (P + k) mod 26      P = (C - k) mod 26     (A = 0)
ROT13       k = 13 (encrypt = decrypt)
Vigenere    C = (P + K_i) mod 26    keyword repeated over the text
Hill        C = K x P mod 26        P = K^-1 x C mod 26
Blocks      blocks = ceil(n / b)    padding = blocks x b - n
3DES enc    C = E_K3( D_K2( E_K1(P) ) )
3DES dec    P = D_K1( E_K2( D_K3(C) ) )
Key space   2^k   (DES 2^56, AES 2^128, 3DES 2^168)
3DES decryption undoes the last step first: decrypt with K3, encrypt with K2, decrypt with K1 (Final A Q6).

Playfair rules

  1. Build the 5×5 square: keyword without repeats, then the rest of the alphabet (I/J share a cell).
  2. Split into pairs. A doubled letter gets an X between; an odd length gets a trailing X.
  3. Same row → take the letter to the right. Same column → letter below. Otherwise → rectangle corners (same row, other column).

Block ciphers

CipherBlockKey

AES128128 / 192 / 256

Modes of operation

  • ECB: each block alone; identical blocks leak patterns.
  • CBC: XOR with previous ciphertext (IV first).
  • CFB / OFB: turn a block cipher into a stream cipher.
  • CTR: encrypt a counter, parallel.

Terms and principles

  • Three dimensions: number of keys (symmetric/asymmetric), operation (substitution/transposition), processing (block/stream).
  • Cryptanalysis by what the attacker has: ciphertext only, known plaintext, chosen plaintext, chosen ciphertext.
  • Kerckhoffs: the algorithm is public, only the key is secret.
  • One-time pad: random key as long as the message, used once. Unbreakable.
  • Keyword cipher: keyword (no repeats) then the remaining alphabet.
CH 06

Asymmetric cryptography, RSA, hashing and PKI

New since the major and the most calculation-heavy chapter. Expect at least one RSA question (keys, encrypt, or sign), plus hash vs MAC vs signature and the PKI roles.

The golden rule of key direction

GoalSender usesReceiver usesGives
Confidentiality (encrypt)Receiver's public keyReceiver's private keyOnly the receiver can read
Signature (authenticate)Sender's private keySender's public keyIntegrity, authentication, non-repudiation
"What creates a digital signature?" Sender's private key. "If the public key encrypts…" then only the private key can decrypt. "Why revoke a certificate?" The private key was compromised, not the public key.

Symmetric vs asymmetric

  • Symmetric: one shared key, ~30,000× faster, problem = key distribution. Bulk data.
  • Asymmetric: public/private pair built on a trapdoor one-way function (easy forward, infeasible to invert without the secret). Key exchange, signatures.
  • RSA security = difficulty of factoring n. 512-bit is broken; use 2048-bit minimum.

Hash vs MAC vs digital signature (repeated)

HashMACSignature
KeyNoneShared secretPrivate key signs, public verifies
IntegrityYesYesYes
AuthenticationNoYesYes
Non-repudiationNoNoYes
ExampleSHA-256HMACRSA, DSA
Each step adds one property: hash = I, MAC = I + A, signature = I + A + N.

Hash functions

  • One-way, fixed-length digest, collisions infeasible. Used for passwords and integrity checks.
  • Collision: two inputs, same digest. Breaks integrity.
  • SHA-1 160-bit (deprecated) · SHA-2 256/512 · SHA-3 224–512 (Keccak sponge) · MD2/MD4/MD5 128-bit. MD4 is faster and weaker; MD5 was made to fix it.
  • Hashing vs encryption: hash is fixed length and irreversible. Ciphertext grows with input and is reversible with the key.

RSA step by step

Key generation and use

1. pick primes p, q            n = p x q
2. phi(n) = (p - 1)(q - 1)
3. pick e: 1 < e < phi(n), gcd(e, phi(n)) = 1
4. d = e^-1 mod phi(n)         (e x d mod phi(n) = 1)
   public key  {e, n}          private key {d, n}

encrypt   C = M^e mod n        decrypt   M = C^d mod n
sign      S = M^d mod n        verify    M = S^e mod n
To pick a valid e, check gcd with φ(n), not with n. For n = 33, φ = 20: e = 10 fails (gcd 10), e = 11 works (Final B Q26).

Finding d with the extended Euclidean algorithm

Example: e = 7, φ(n) = 60.

60 = 8 x 7 + 4
 7 = 1 x 4 + 3
 4 = 1 x 3 + 1        (gcd = 1, so d exists)

back-substitute:
1 = 4 - 3
  = 4 - (7 - 4)       = 2 x 4 - 7
  = 2 x (60 - 8 x 7) - 7
  = 2 x 60 - 17 x 7
d = -17 mod 60 = 43   check: 7 x 43 = 301 = 5 x 60 + 1
For small numbers just try multiples: e × d = k·φ(n) + 1. For e = 3, φ = 20: 21 = 3 × 7, so d = 7.

Worked: slides example (p = 7, q = 11)

n = 77        phi = 6 x 10 = 60
e = 13        gcd(13, 60) = 1
d = 37        13 x 37 = 481 = 8 x 60 + 1
M = 5:  C = 5^13 mod 77
   5^2 = 25,  5^4 = 625 mod 77 = 9,  5^8 = 81 mod 77 = 4
   5^13 = 5^8 x 5^4 x 5 = 4 x 9 x 5 = 180 mod 77 = 26
decrypt: 26^37 mod 77 = 5
Square-and-multiply: split the exponent into powers of two (13 = 8 + 4 + 1), reduce after every step.

Worked: signature (Final A Q22)

p = 5, q = 7, M = 2, e = 5
n = 35        phi = 4 x 6 = 24
d: 5 x 5 = 25 = 24 + 1  ->  d = 5
S = M^d mod n = 2^5 mod 35 = 32
verify: 32^5 mod 35 = 2  (= M)

PKI and certificates

PKI players

  • CA: issues, manages and revokes certificates. The basis of trust. Signs certificates with its private key; anyone verifies them with the CA's public key.
  • RA: verifies identity before the CA issues (enrol, approve, revocation requests, hand out tokens).
  • Repository: public store of certificates and CRLs.
  • Certificate policy: what a certificate means; the basis of trust between unrelated parties.

X.509 certificate fields

Version, serial number, signature algorithm, issuer, validity (not before / not after), subject, subject public key, issuer/subject unique IDs, extensions, CA signature. Life cycle states: in use, revoked, expired.

Revocation

  • CRL: periodic list of revoked serial numbers published by the CA (X.509). Downloaded and checked, so there is latency.
  • OCSP: real-time status query for one certificate.
  • Revoke when the private key is compromised, details change, or the CA was misled. A second website is not a reason (Final B Q13).

PKI services

Privacy (encryption), authentication (certificates), integrity (signatures), non-repudiation (private key proves authorship). S/MIME exchanges keys using X.509 certificates.

Not in the current slides, but asked on both 221 finals

Diffie-Hellman key exchange

public: prime p, generator g
Alice secret a: A = g^a mod p     Bob secret b: B = g^b mod p
shared key K = B^a mod p = A^b mod p

Final B Q29: p = 13, g = 7, A = 8, b = 3
K = 8^3 mod 13 = 512 mod 13 = 5
Man in the middle (Final B Q24–25): Malory runs a separate exchange with each side. p = 13, g = 5, Mark a = 4, Malory 6: K1 = 12^4 mod 13 = 1. Ava 3, Malory 2: K2 = 8^2 mod 13 = 12. DH has no authentication; signatures or certificates stop it.

IPsec and ARP spoofing

  • IPsec tunnel mode wraps the whole original packet. ESP encrypts it (confidentiality); AH gives integrity and origin authentication; together they give non-repudiation.
  • ARP / MAC poisoning: detect with ARPwatch (changed MAC–IP bindings); prevent with static ARP entries or switch port security.
CH 07

Security design principles and models

Least privilege, Bell-LaPadula vs Biba, Take-Grant and the access matrix are the favourites. Final B Q22 had you apply the Bell-LaPadula rules to named people.

The first nine are on the slides with detail: "Smart Students Defend Lots, Deny Failures, Separate, Distrust, Zero-in".

Fail secure vs fail safe

  • Fail secure: blocks access. Risk: denial of service. Security exams prefer it.
  • Fail safe: disables the control. Risk: illegitimate access. Chosen when people's safety comes first (fire exits).

Separation of duties forms

  • Task splitting: the task is divided between people.
  • Task shadowing: a second person observes or duplicates the work.

Confinement vs isolation vs bounds

  • Confinement: a process may read/write only allowed locations (sandboxing). Confidentiality. (Final A Q12)
  • Process isolation: each process has its own memory space; one cannot affect another. Integrity.
  • Bounds: the authority level a process runs at (ring 0 kernel vs ring 3 user). Access control.

Design and coding flaws

  • Covert timing channel: leaks via timing of shared CPU/RAM use. Covert storage channel: leaks by writing to shared storage.
  • Maintenance hook: developer backdoor that skips authentication.
  • Salami (incremental) attack: many tiny thefts (rounding fractions of a cent).
  • Data diddling: altering data before or during entry (40 hours typed as 400).
  • Privileged programs: grant extra rights, so they enable escalation.
  • Defences: trusted recovery and input validation.

Bell-LaPadula vs Biba

Bell-LaPadulaBiba
ProtectsConfidentiality (military)Integrity (finance, databases)

Applying the rules (Final B Q22)

Levels: Top Secret: Duke · Secret: Claire · Confidential: Kevin · Unclassified: Emme.

  • Simple security rule on Claire → she may read her own level and everything below (Secret, Confidential, Unclassified).
  • Star rule on Kevin → he may write at his own level and everything above.
  • Strong star rule on Duke → his own level only.

Memory hooks

Bell-LaPadula is a spy: can't read files above their clearance, can't leak secrets downward. Biba is a chef: won't read a junior cook's recipe (no read down, no contamination) and can't scribble in the head chef's book (no write up).
Biba is the exact mirror image. If a question says "integrity", the answer flips both rules.

Other models

Access control matrix

Subjects in rows, objects in columns.

  • Column = ACL, bound to the object: "who can access File 1?"
  • Row = capability list, bound to the subject: "what can Alice do?"

Take-Grant

Rights as a graph with take, grant, create, remove rules. Used to find whether rights can leak to an unauthorized subject (Final A Q10: "reason about rights and find whether leakage occurred").

Model list

TCB, state machine (always in a secure state), information flow, noninterference (high actions invisible to low users), Take-Grant, access control matrix, Bell-LaPadula, Biba, Clark-Wilson (integrity via well-formed transactions and separation of duties), Brewer-Nash / Chinese Wall (prevents conflicts of interest, Final B Q11), Goguen-Meseguer, Sutherland, Graham-Denning.

CH 08

System architecture vulnerabilities and countermeasures

A long list chapter. Learn each layer's main threat and two defences, and the mnemonics.

Processing

  • Multitasking (OS-level), multicore (one chip, many cores), multiprocessing, multithreading.
  • SMP: processors share one OS and memory. MPP: each processor has its own OS and memory.
SMP = Shared, MPP = My own.

Protection rings and states

  • Ring 0 kernel (most privileged) → rings 1–2 drivers/OS components → ring 3 user apps.
  • Supervisory state = privileged. Problem state = user mode, every request checked (Final B Q9).
  • Process states: ready, running, waiting, terminated.
Lower ring number = more privilege, so ring 1 processes reach more than ring 2 (Final B Q8).

Memory

  • ROM (non-volatile): PROM (write once), EPROM (UV erase), EEPROM (electrical erase, holds BIOS), flash.
  • RAM (volatile): real, cache, registers.
  • Protection: paging/segmentation, ASLR, DEP, protection keys, kernel protection, buffer-overflow mitigation, process isolation.
  • Secondary storage: encryption at rest, immutable backups, air-gapping, access control, physical security.

Firmware and I/O

  • BIOS on EEPROM; updating = flashing. Phlashing = malicious code planted in BIOS. UEFI replaced BIOS and adds Secure Boot.
  • Firmware attacks survive OS reinstalls.
  • I/O risks: malicious USB, hardware keyloggers, rogue routers. Defences: physical security, port control, updates, authentication, segmentation, TLS, input validation.

Client vs server

  • Client: EDR, browser security (CSP, HttpOnly/Secure cookies), input sanitization, code obfuscation, patching.
  • Server: NGFW + WAF, patch management, MFA + RBAC, encryption, IDS/IPS, hardening, DDoS mitigation.
Server = "NPMEDIS": NGFW/WAF, Patch, MFA, Encryption, Detection, Isolate (harden), Stop DDoS.

Web threats (most tested in Ch 8)

ThreatMitigation
SQL injectionValidate input, parameterized queries
XSSEscape output, Content Security Policy
DDoSMitigation services, rate limiting
Broken access controlStrict authentication, RBAC
"Host it in the cloud" does not stop SQL injection. The flaw is in how queries are built (Final B Q10).

Database

Threats: SQL/NoSQL injection, insiders, misconfiguration (default passwords, open ports), weak encryption, ransomware ("SIM-WR"). Practices: least privilege, data masking, encrypted backups, vulnerability scanning, physical security.

Cloud

  • SaaS (use the app) · PaaS (build the app) · IaaS (manage from the OS up).
  • Hypervisor creates VMs; elasticity grows and shrinks resources.
  • Shared responsibility: provider secures of the cloud (hardware, network, virtualization); customer secures in the cloud (data, apps, identities).
  • Top threat: misconfiguration. Also account hijacking, insecure APIs, insiders. Tools: IAM + MFA, encryption, CSPM, CWPP/CNAPP.

IoT

Weak firmware, no encryption, default credentials, gateway into the network, unsupported software. Best practice "CUNZD-M": Change defaults, Update firmware, Network segmentation, Zero trust, Disable unused features, MFA.

Mobile and BYOD

  • Device: full encryption, remote wipe, lockout, screen lock, GPS, app control, storage segmentation.
  • Apps: key management (most crypto failures), credential management, whitelisting (deny by default).
  • BYOD concerns: data and support ownership, patching, antivirus, forensics, privacy, on/offboarding.
  • Alternatives: COPE (company owned, personally enabled), CYOD (choose from an approved list), VDI/VMI.

OS protection

  • Technical: layering, abstraction, data hiding, process isolation, hardware segmentation.
  • Policy: least privilege, separation of duties, accountability ("LSA").
  • Hardening: remove unneeded software, change defaults, firewall, strong passwords, MFA, auditing.
CH 09

ICS/SCADA security for cyber-physical systems

CPS/ICS/SCADA definitions and the SCADA components are the most repeated topics on the whole course list. Remember that the CIA order flips here.

Definitions

  • CPS: ICT fused with the physical world through sensors (input) and actuators (output).
  • ICS: umbrella term for industrial control systems; SCADA and DCS are types.
  • SCADA: Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition: monitors and controls geographically spread equipment from a centre.
  • Applications: smart grid, water, oil and gas, transport, telecom, manufacturing.

Components (repeated)

  1. Field instruments: sensors (measure) and actuators (act).
  2. PLCs and RTUs: PLC runs control logic; RTU collects telemetry and sends it to the MTU.
  3. Communication network: wired, wireless, satellite, fibre.
  4. Control centre: MTU (the heart; initiates all communication, stores data), HMI (operator screen), OPC server (translator between Windows and field hardware), database/historian.
"Fancy People Cook Soup": Field, PLC/RTU, Communications, SCADA centre.

IT vs OT

ITOT
FocusDataPhysical process
PriorityConfidentiality firstAvailability and safety first
DowntimeTolerableNear zero
PatchingFrequentSlow, rare
Life3–5 years15–30+ years
FailureData lossPhysical harm
Security objectives in ICS are A → I → C: availability, then integrity (operators must trust readings), then confidentiality.

Purdue model (6 levels)

L0 physical process · L1 sensors and actuators · L2 PLCs and RTUs · L3 SCADA and HMI · L4 enterprise IT · L5 cloud and external.

Protocols

Modbus 502 (no encryption or authentication) · DNP 19999 / DNP3 20000 (secure authentication) · EtherNet/IP 2222 · PROFINET 34962–64 · OPC UA and IEC 61850 (high security).

Threats

  • Unintentional: human error, machine failure, natural disasters.
  • Purposeful: disgruntled employees, espionage, sabotage, hackers, viruses/worms, theft, terrorism.
  • Attacks: eavesdropping, DoS, wireless jamming, man-in-the-middle, replay.
  • Why vulnerable ("SNAIL"): no real-time Scanning, No patch speed, weak Authentication, Ignorance of device capabilities, Lack of traffic visibility.

Objectives and requirements

  • Redundancy; graceful degradation: normal → emergency → manual; early detection.
  • Field instruments: fences, locks, CCTV. Servers: MFA, whitelisting, failover, patch management. Perimeter: surveillance, mantrap. Network: NSM, NDR, SIEM, segmentation, encryption.

Governance and planning

  • Standards: NIST SP 800-82, NISTIR 7628 (smart grid), NERC CIP 002–009, DHS catalog, ISO 27001.
  • Seven phases: assess, document, train, segment, control access, harden, monitor and maintain.
"A Dog Trains Security Controls Harder, Monitoring".

Traditional vs AI SCADA

Reactive → predictive; scheduled → predictive maintenance; human → AI-assisted decisions; rule/signature detection → machine-learning anomaly detection.

CH 10

Authentication and access control

The matching question (6 marks of scenarios) and the FAR/FRR/CER short answer came from this chapter in both finals. Learn the models by who decides.

Identification → authentication → authorization

  • Identification: claim (username). Must be unique, standard naming, non-descriptive.
  • Authentication: prove it.
  • Authorization: what you may do.
  • Two phases (Final B Q16): policy definition (authorization is decided) and policy enforcement (identification, authentication and accountability happen at access time).

Three factors and MFA

  • Type 1 knowledge: password, PIN.
  • Type 2 ownership: token, smart card.
  • Type 3 characteristic: biometrics.
  • MFA needs different types. Password + PIN is still one factor.

Biometric errors (asked on both finals)

  • Type I = FRR: a genuine user is rejected. Annoying.
  • Type II = FAR: an impostor is accepted. Dangerous, the one to minimize.
  • CER/EER: the point where FRR = FAR. Used to compare devices; lower CER = more accurate.
  • Physiological: face, fingerprint, hand, iris/retina, DNA. Behavioural: keystroke, signature, voice.
Type 1 rejects the Right one, Type 2 accepts the Wrong one.

Passwords and OTP

  • Attacks: electronic monitoring (sniffing), password-file access, brute force, dictionary, social engineering.
  • Synchronous OTP: time-based, token and server clocks agree. Asynchronous: challenge–response.
  • OTPs resist sniffing and guessing because each is used once.

Authorization concepts

  • Criteria: roles, groups, location, time, transaction types (controls data touched and commands allowed, Final B Q6).
  • Authorization creep: new rights added, old ones never removed (Final A Q11, Q23).
  • Default to zero, need to know, ACLs.

SSO

  • One login for all authorized resources. Technologies: Kerberos, SESAME, security domains, directory services, dumb terminals.
  • Pros: one log-on, stronger passwords, central admin. Cons: single point of compromise, legacy systems, implementation difficulty.

"Why is RADIUS less secure than TACACS+?" Two contrasts: only the password is encrypted vs everything; UDP (unreliable) vs TCP.

Administration and techniques

  • Centralized: one authority, consistent (RADIUS, TACACS+, Diameter). Decentralized: control near the resource, inconsistent (Final B Q7).
  • Techniques: rule-based (firewalls), constrained interfaces, access control matrix (capability table per subject, ACL per object).

Accountability

  • Audit review tools: event-oriented, real-time, audit reduction, variance detection, attack signature.
  • Keystroke monitoring: records every keystroke (privacy, must be disclosed).
  • Scrubbing: deleting incriminating entries from logs (an attack on accountability).
  • Best practice: deny anonymous accounts, strict criteria, suspend inactive accounts, replace defaults, rotate passwords, audit, protect logs.

The scenario answer template (Activities 2–4)

Real questions from the 221 finals and quizzes, plus practice in the same style. Answer before opening.

Final 221 A

All of the following are cybersecurity pillars except __.

  1. People
  2. Policies
  3. Technologies
  4. Data
Answer
d) Data

The pillars are people, policies (process) and technology. Data is what they protect.

Final 221 A

Frequent emails impersonating a bank, aimed at convincing you specifically to disclose credentials, are called __.

  1. Phishing
  2. Spear phishing
  3. Whaling
  4. Pretexting
Answer
b) Spear phishing

"Specifically" makes it targeted. Whaling would need an executive target.

Final 221 B

MD4 is the __ algorithm and __ secure than MD5.

  1. slower / more
  2. faster / less
  3. faster / more
  4. slower / less
Answer
b) faster / less

MD5 was designed to repair MD4's weaknesses at some cost in speed.

Final 221 B

Processes in ring __ can access more resources and interact with the OS more directly than processes in ring __.

  1. 3 / 1
  2. 2 / 1
  3. 1 / 2
  4. 3 / 0
Answer
c) 1 / 2

Lower ring number = more privilege.

Final 221 B

The __ model makes sure conflicts of interest are recognised and people cannot use data they should not see.

  1. Biba
  2. Brewer and Nash
  3. Clark-Wilson
  4. Take-Grant
Answer
b) Brewer and Nash (Chinese Wall)
Final 221 A

Given p = 5, q = 7, e = 5, sign M = 2 with RSA.

Answer
S = 32

n = 35, φ = 24, d = 5 (25 ≡ 1 mod 24). S = 2⁵ mod 35 = 32. Verify 32⁵ mod 35 = 2.

Final 221 B

Dexter picks p = 11, q = 3, e = 3. Find d, then sign M = 3.

Answer
d = 7, S = 9

φ = 20; 3 × 7 = 21 ≡ 1. S = 3⁷ mod 33 = 2187 mod 33 = 9 (33 × 66 = 2178).

Final 221 B

Diffie-Hellman with p = 13, g = 7. Alice's public value is 8, Bob's secret is 3. Shared key?

Answer
K = 5

K = 8³ mod 13 = 512 mod 13 = 5. Bob's public value would be 7³ mod 13 = 5 as well.

Final 221 A

Explain how biometric accuracy is measured with FAR, FRR and CER. (3 marks)

Answer
FRR (Type I): genuine user rejected. FAR (Type II): impostor accepted. CER: the setting where FRR = FAR; lower CER = more accurate device.

One mark per definition. Add that FAR is the dangerous one.

Final 221 A

Differentiate Biba and Bell-LaPadula by aim and mechanism.

Answer
Bell-LaPadula: confidentiality, no read up / no write down. Biba: integrity, no read down / no write up.
Final 221 B

Where can you check whether a certificate was revoked?

Answer
The CA's Certificate Revocation List (periodic, in a repository) or OCSP (real-time query).
Final 221 B

Which keys issue and validate an X.509 certificate, and who issues it?

Answer
The CA signs with its private key; anyone verifies with the CA's public key. The (root) CA issues it.
Final 221 B

A company blocks youtube.com but staff still reach it. How, and which trust property is exploited?

Answer
Through a VPN, proxy, SSH tunnel or Tor; the company only trusts the endpoint it can see (the transitive-trust problem). Firewalls that inspect tunnelled traffic stop it.
Practice

An ICS operator must choose between patching a PLC now (2 hours offline) or next scheduled outage. Which security objective drives the decision, and why?

Answer
Availability (and safety) first.

ICS priority is A → I → C. Patches wait for a planned window and are tested first because downtime affects a physical process.

Which mechanism provides integrity and authentication but not non-repudiation?

In the cloud shared-responsibility model, who secures the customer's data and IAM settings in an IaaS deployment?
