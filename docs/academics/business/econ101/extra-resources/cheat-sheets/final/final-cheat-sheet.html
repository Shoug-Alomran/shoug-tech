EXAM PLAN

How each question type is marked, and how to answer it

A comprehensive paper: MCQ across every chapter, calculations (elasticity, policies, utility, costs, profit), and graph reading for indifference curves and market structures.

A

Multiple choice

  1. Underline the trigger word: own price, substitute, inferior, below equilibrium, elastic.
  2. Decide the curve or the formula before reading the options.
  3. Cross out options that confuse a movement with a shift, or MR with price.
  4. For a number, compute it in the margin; most wrong options are the inverse or the forgotten average.
B

Short answer

  1. Define the term in one sentence from the slides.
  2. Apply it to the case in the question (name the curve, the shift, the direction).
  3. Show the numbers or the steps if there are any.
  4. Conclude with the effect on price, quantity, revenue or profit.
C

Calculations

  1. Write the formula in words first.
  2. Show each step on its own line; never round in the middle (the handwriting).
  3. Report elasticity as a positive number, and interpret it.
  4. State the unit (SAR, units, %).
CheckElasticity with the midpoint method: going up or down must give the same answer.
D

Graph questions

  1. Label both axes and every curve.
  2. MR = MC gives quantity; climb to demand for price (monopoly, monopolistic competition).
  3. Shade profit as (P − ATC) × Q.
  4. For indifference curves: tangency where MRS = relative price.

PRACTICE

Exam-style questions with worked answers

Answer each one on paper first, then open the answer. Show all answers at the top opens every one.

Headline · Ch 3

"The price of coffee rises sharply." What happens to the demand for tea, and for sugar used in coffee?

Show answer

Tea is a substitute: demand for tea increases (shifts right). Sugar is a complement: demand for sugar decreases (shifts left).

Calculation · Ch 3

Qd = 19 − 3P and Qs = 1 + 3P. Find the equilibrium, then the situation at P = 4.

Show answer

19 − 3P = 1 + 3P → P = 3, Q = 10. At P = 4: Qd = 7, Qs = 13 → a surplus of 6, so the price falls back toward 3.

Short answer · Ch 3

Explain the difference between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded.

Show answer

A change in the good's own price causes a change in quantity demanded, a movement along the curve. A change in any other factor (RIPE) causes a change in demand, a shift of the whole curve.

Calculation · Ch 4

Price rises from 8 to 12 and quantity falls from 50 to 30. Find ε, classify demand, and check with total revenue.

Show answer

%ΔQ = 20 ÷ 40 = 50%. %ΔP = 4 ÷ 10 = 40%. ε = 50 ÷ 40 = 1.25 → elastic.

TR falls from 8 × 50 = 400 to 12 × 30 = 360: price up, revenue down, which confirms elastic demand.

Calculation · Ch 4

Income rises 10% and the quantity of bus rides demanded falls 5%. Income elasticity? What kind of good?

Show answer

−5 ÷ 10 = −0.5. Negative, so bus rides are an inferior good.

Short answer · Ch 4

State three factors that influence the price elasticity of demand.

Show answer

The closeness of substitutes, the proportion of income spent on the good, and the time elapsed since the price change.

Calculation · Ch 5

Demand starts at a price of 12, supply at 2, and equilibrium is P = 7, Q = 10. Find CS, PS and total surplus.

Show answer

CS = ½ × 10 × (12 − 7) = 25. PS = ½ × 10 × (7 − 2) = 25. Total surplus = 50.

Calculation · Ch 6

Demand P = 50 − Q, supply P = 10 + Q. A tax of 10 per unit is imposed. Find the quantity, buyers' and sellers' prices, revenue and deadweight loss.

Show answer

No tax: 50 − Q = 10 + Q → Q = 20, P = 30.

Wedge: (50 − Q) − (10 + Q) = 10 → 40 − 2Q = 10 → Q = 15.

Buyers pay 50 − 15 = 35; sellers get 10 + 15 = 25 (split 5 / 5 because the slopes are equal).

Revenue = 10 × 15 = 150. DWL = ½ × 10 × (20 − 15) = 25.

Calculation · Ch 6

In the rent example (D: P = 5,000 − 50Q, S: P = 1,000 + 50Q), a ceiling of 3,500 is set. What happens?

Show answer

Equilibrium rent is 3,000, so a ceiling of 3,500 is above equilibrium: it doesn't bind and nothing changes (40 thousand apartments at 3,000).

Short answer · Ch 6

Explain why a production quota gives farmers an incentive to cheat.

Show answer

The quota cuts output below equilibrium, so the price (39) is above the marginal cost of the last crate (29). Each farmer could earn more than the extra crate costs by producing over the quota.

Calculation · Ch 8

Using Huda's tables (novel 24, juice 12, income 96), show that 3 novels and 2 cups is not the best choice.

Show answer

At 3 novels and 2 cups: MUN/PN = 42/24 = 1.75; MUJ/PJ = 42/12 = 3.5.

Juice gives more utility per riyal, so she should move spending from novels to juice, reaching 2 novels and 4 cups (both ratios 2, TU 252 vs 240).

Short answer · Ch 8

Explain the paradox of value.

Show answer

Water gives a large total utility but, because we consume so much, a small marginal utility; diamonds the opposite. Price reflects marginal utility, so water is cheap and diamonds are expensive.

Calculation · Ch 9

Rakan's income is 144, a session costs 18 and fruit 12/kg. Write the budget equation, his real income in fruit and the relative price. What happens if income falls to 72?

Show answer

18Qs + 12Qf = 144, so Qf = 12 − 1.5Qs. Real income = 144/12 = 12 kg; relative price = 18/12 = 1.5.

At 72: parallel shift inward, same slope 1.5, real income 6 kg (at most 4 sessions).

Short answer · Ch 9

Split the effect of the session price falling from 18 to 8 into substitution and income effects.

Show answer

Total price effect: 4 → 9 sessions (+5). Cutting income to 96 keeps him on the original curve: he picks 6 sessions, so the substitution effect is +2. Restoring the 48 SAR moves him to 9: income effect +3. Both positive, so sessions are a normal good.

Calculation · Ch 11

Qamar Bakery: what is the MC of the cakes made by the 3rd baker, and the ATC with 4 bakers?

Show answer

3rd baker: ΔTC = 2,040 − 1,680 = 360, ΔQ = 40 − 30 = 10 → MC = 36 SAR.

4 bakers: ATC = 2,400 ÷ 48 = 50 SAR (the minimum).

Short answer · Ch 11

Why is the ATC curve U-shaped?

Show answer

Two forces: AFC falls as the fixed cost is spread over more output, and AVC eventually rises because of diminishing returns. Up to 48 cakes the falling AFC wins, then the rising AVC wins.

Decision · Ch 12

The date price falls to 38. Should Al-Bustan produce, and why?

Show answer

Yes. 38 is above minimum AVC (34), so producing (6 boxes) loses less than shutting down, which would lose the whole TFC of 40.

Short answer · Ch 12

Why is MR equal to price for a perfectly competitive firm?

Show answer

The firm is a price taker: it can sell any amount at the market price, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to total revenue.

Calculation · Ch 13

From Sahra's table, find MR from 4 to 5 tours and explain why the 5th tour isn't run.

Show answer

TR rises from 480 to 500 → MR = 20. MC of the 5th tour is 50 > 20, so profit falls by 30 (180 → 150).

Short answer · Ch 13

Why can a monopoly keep its economic profit in the long run?

Show answer

Barriers to entry (natural, ownership or legal) stop new firms entering, so nothing competes the profit away.

Calculation · Ch 14

In summer Bunn Café sells 50 cups at 10 SAR with ATC 14. Profit or loss, and what happens in the long run if this lasts?

Show answer

(10 − 14) × 50 = −200 SAR a day, a loss. If losses persist, some cafés exit; the rest gain customers until they break even.

Compare · Ch 14

Name two ways monopolistic competition is like monopoly and two ways it is like perfect competition.

Show answer

Like monopoly: downward-sloping demand and the same short-run graph (MR = MC, price from demand). Like perfect competition: many firms and free entry, so profit disappears in the long run.
