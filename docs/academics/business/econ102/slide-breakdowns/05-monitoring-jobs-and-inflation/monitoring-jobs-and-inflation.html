The big picture

In one sentenceUnemployment and inflation are the two other big macro indicators: the labor force survey sorts people into employed, unemployed and not in the labor force to give three rates, unemployment is frictional, structural or cyclical, and the CPI measures the price level whose percentage change is the inflation rate.

Chapter 5 is on Major I and it's almost all calculation. The group work uses the textbook problems: the unemployment rate, participation rate and employment-to-population ratio; tracking flows into and out of unemployment; naming the type of unemployment in a news clip; and building a CPI basket to find the CPI and the inflation rate. Learn the three formulas and the CPI steps cold.

Where this fits

Chapter 4 measured output. This chapter measures jobs and prices. Chapter 6 explains long-run growth, and Chapter 8 explains where inflation comes from (too much money growth).

By the end you can

  • Classify people as employed, unemployed or not in the labor force
  • Calculate the unemployment rate, the labor force participation rate and the employment-to-population ratio
  • Explain frictional, structural and cyclical unemployment, and natural unemployment
  • Link unemployment to the output gap
  • Explain why inflation and deflation are problems
  • Construct the CPI and calculate the inflation rate, and explain the CPI's biases

Lesson 1

Why unemployment matters and who is unemployed

Must knowSlides 4-9

Unemployment is a problem for two reasons:

  • Lost incomes and production: an unemployed worker loses income, and the economy loses the output that worker could have produced.
  • Lost human capital: prolonged unemployment destroys skills and harms health and self-esteem.

Every month the Current Population Survey sorts the population:

To be counted as unemployed, a person must be in one of three categories:

  1. Without work, but has made specific efforts to find a job within the previous four weeks
  2. Waiting to be called back to a job from which they were laid off
  3. Waiting to start a new job within 30 days
Exam trap

Not looking = not in the labor force

Someone without a job who isn't looking (a retiree, a full-time student, a discouraged worker) is not in the labor force. They're not unemployed. Only people actively searching (or waiting to be recalled or to start) count as unemployed.

Try itPredicted question: classify each person: a retiree; a student looking for a part-time job; a worker waiting to start a new job in two weeks; someone who stopped looking last year

Retiree: not in the labor force. Student looking for work: unemployed. Worker starting in two weeks: unemployed (waiting to start within 30 days). Stopped looking: not in the labor force (a discouraged or marginally attached worker).

Remember
  • Unemployment costs lost income, lost output and lost human capital
  • Labor force = employed + unemployed
  • Unemployed: no job, available, and looked in the past four weeks (or waiting for recall or to start)
  • Not looking means not in the labor force
Lesson 2

The three labor market indicators

Must knowSlides 10-15
The three rates
Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100
Labor force participation rate = labor force ÷ working-age population × 100
Employment-to-population ratio = employed ÷ working-age population × 100
Memory trick

"U over L, L over W, E over W"

Unemployment rate: U ÷ Labor force. Labor force participation: L ÷ Working-age pop. Employment-to-pop: E ÷ W. Only the unemployment rate divides by the labor force; the other two divide by the working-age population.

Step by step

Group work, Problem 1: labor force 159 m, employment 148 m, working-age population 261 m

  1. Unemployed = 159 − 148 = 11 million.
  2. Unemployment rate = 11 ÷ 159 × 100 = 6.9%.
  3. Labor force participation rate = 159 ÷ 261 × 100 = 60.9%.
  4. Employment-to-population ratio = 148 ÷ 261 × 100 = 56.7%.
Step by step

Group work, Problem 2: Sandy Island (July: 10,000 employed, 1,000 unemployed, 5,000 not in the labor force)

  1. July: labor force = 11,000; working-age population = 16,000. Unemployment rate = 1,000 ÷ 11,000 = 9.1%. E/P ratio = 10,000 ÷ 16,000 = 62.5%.
  2. August flows: 80 lost jobs and stopped looking (E −80, out of the labor force); 20 quit and retired (E −20, out); 150 unemployed were hired (U −150, E +150); 50 quit the labor force (U −50); 40 entered to look for work (U +40).
  3. Unemployed at the end of August = 1,000 − 150 − 50 + 40 = 840.
  4. Employed = 10,000 − 80 − 20 + 150 = 10,050.
  5. Unemployment rate = 840 ÷ (10,050 + 840) = 840 ÷ 10,890 = 7.7%.
Exam trap

Losing a job doesn't always raise unemployment

In Problem 2, the 80 people who lost their jobs didn't look for new ones, so they left the labor force instead of becoming unemployed. Always ask: "are they looking?"

Over the business cycle, the unemployment rate rises in recessions and falls in expansions. The participation rate and the E/P ratio show a long-term trend: rising for women, falling for men.

Remember
  • Unemployment rate = U ÷ labor force
  • Participation rate = labor force ÷ working-age population
  • Employment-to-population ratio = E ÷ working-age population
  • Track each flow: who enters or leaves employment, unemployment and the labor force
Lesson 3

Broader measures of unemployment

Good to knowSlides 16-21

The official (U-3) rate misses some underused labor:

  • Marginally attached workers: people who currently are neither working nor looking but have looked in the past 12 months. A discouraged worker is a marginally attached worker who has stopped looking because of repeated failure.
  • Part-time workers who want full-time jobs (part time for economic reasons).

The BLS publishes six measures, U-1 to U-6. U-3 is the official rate. U-4 adds discouraged workers, U-5 adds all marginally attached workers, and U-6, the broadest, also adds part-time workers who want full-time work.

Memory trick

Higher U-number, bigger net

U-1 is the narrowest and U-6 the widest net. Each step up catches more under-used workers.

Remember
  • Discouraged workers aren't counted as unemployed in the official rate
  • So the official rate understates under-used labor
  • U-6 adds marginally attached workers and involuntary part-timers
Lesson 4

Frictional, structural and cyclical unemployment

Must knowSlides 22-28
Type Cause Example
Frictional Normal labor turnover: people entering the labor market and searching for jobs, firms searching for workers A new graduate looking for her first job
Structural Changes in technology or international competition change the skills or locations jobs need Bank tellers replaced by apps; factory jobs moved abroad
Cyclical The higher-than-normal unemployment of a business cycle recession Workers laid off because sales fell in a slowdown
  • Natural unemployment = frictional + structural. It's the unemployment that arises when there's no cyclical unemployment.
  • Full employment is the situation in which the unemployment rate equals the natural unemployment rate. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • The natural rate changes over time with the age distribution of the population, the scale of structural change, the real wage rate and unemployment benefits.
Memory trick

"FSC": Fresh, Skills, Cycle

Frictional: fresh job search. Structural: skills or place mismatch. Cyclical: the business cycle. Natural = F + S (everything except the cycle).

Exam trap

Structural lasts longer than frictional

Frictional unemployment is short and healthy (matching people to jobs). Structural unemployment usually lasts longer, because workers must retrain or move.

Try itGroup work, Problems 6–7: Singapore's unemployment rises from a slowdown and from technology widening the skills gap

Two types: cyclical (the slowdown in growth) and structural (technology means workers' skills no longer match what employers need). Officials worry more about structural unemployment: it lasts longer and won't disappear when growth picks up, since employers are still struggling to fill posts.

Which couldn't be prevented even with no slowdown? Frictional and structural unemployment, the natural unemployment that exists even at full employment.

Remember
  • Frictional: normal job search; structural: skills or location mismatch; cyclical: recessions
  • Natural unemployment = frictional + structural
  • Full employment: unemployment rate = natural rate, not zero
Lesson 5

Unemployment and real GDP over the cycle

Good to knowSlides 29-30

Potential GDP is the real GDP produced at full employment. The output gap is real GDP minus potential GDP, as a percentage of potential GDP.

  • When real GDP is below potential (a negative output gap, a recession), unemployment is above the natural rate: cyclical unemployment is positive.
  • When real GDP is above potential, unemployment is below the natural rate.
Memory trick

Gap and joblessness move opposite ways

Output gap down (negative), unemployment up. They mirror each other through the cycle.

Remember
  • Output gap = (real GDP − potential GDP) ÷ potential GDP
  • Negative gap: unemployment above the natural rate
  • Positive gap: unemployment below the natural rate
Lesson 6

Why inflation and deflation are problems

Must knowSlides 31-33

The price level is the average level of prices. Inflation is a rising price level; deflation a falling one. Low, steady and anticipated inflation or deflation isn't a big problem. Unpredictable inflation is, because it:

  1. Redistributes income: workers' wages lag behind prices (or run ahead of them).
  2. Redistributes wealth: unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders, because loans are repaid in money worth less.
  3. Lowers real GDP and consumption: hard-to-predict inflation makes planning harder.
  4. Diverts resources from production: people spend time and effort forecasting inflation and protecting themselves from it instead of producing. In hyperinflation (50% a month or more) people stop using money altogether.
Memory trick

Unexpected inflation: Borrowers Benefit, Lenders Lose

Borrowers benefit and lenders lose, because the money paid back buys less than the money lent.

Try itPredicted question: you lend SAR 1,000 for a year at 5% and inflation turns out to be 8%. Who gains?

The borrower gains and you (the lender) lose: you're repaid SAR 1,050, but it buys less than SAR 1,000 did a year ago, so your real return is about −3%.

Remember
  • Unpredictable inflation, not inflation itself, is the main problem
  • It redistributes income and wealth (borrowers gain, lenders lose)
  • It lowers real GDP and diverts resources into forecasting inflation
Lesson 7

The Consumer Price Index and the inflation rate

Must knowSlides 34-44

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the average of the prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed basket of consumer goods and services. It's defined to equal 100 in the reference base period; a CPI of 271.7 means prices are 171.7% higher than in the base period.

Constructing the CPI takes three steps: select the CPI basket (from the Consumer Expenditure Survey), do a monthly price survey, and calculate the CPI.

CPI and the inflation rate
Cost of basket = Σ (base-period quantity × price)
CPI = cost of basket at current prices ÷ cost of basket at base-period prices × 100
Inflation rate = (CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year × 100
Step by step

Group work, Problems 8–9: Coral Island buys juice and cloth. 2020: $60 on juice at $2 a bottle, $30 on cloth at $5 a yard. 2021: juice $4, cloth $6

  1. CPI basket: juice = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 bottles; cloth = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 yards.
  2. Share of budget on juice in 2020 = 60 ÷ 90 × 100 = 66.7%.
  3. Cost of the basket in 2021 = (30 × $4) + (6 × $6) = 120 + 36 = $156.
  4. CPI 2021 = 156 ÷ 90 × 100 = 173.3 (2020 = 100).
  5. Inflation rate in 2021 = (173.3 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 73.3%.
Step by step

Group work, Problem 10: CPI June 2019 = 256.1, June 2020 = 257.8, June 2021 = 271.7

  1. Year to June 2020: (257.8 − 256.1) ÷ 256.1 × 100 = 0.66%.
  2. Year to June 2021: (271.7 − 257.8) ÷ 257.8 × 100 = 5.39%.
  3. The inflation rate increased sharply in 2021.
Exam trap

The basket quantities stay fixed

The CPI keeps the base-period quantities and changes only the prices. Don't recalculate quantities with the new prices.

Exam trap

CPI level vs inflation rate

A CPI that is still rising but more slowly means a falling inflation rate, not deflation. Deflation means the CPI itself falls.

Remember
  • CPI = 100 in the base period
  • CPI = cost of the fixed basket now ÷ cost in the base period × 100
  • Inflation rate = percentage change in the CPI
  • The basket's quantities are fixed at base-period amounts
Lesson 8

Is the CPI biased? Alternative price indexes

Good to knowSlides 45-53

The CPI may overstate the true inflation rate, because of four biases:

Bias Why it overstates inflation
New goods bias New goods (a smartphone replacing a landline) are pricier but better, and enter the basket late
Quality change bias Part of a price rise pays for better quality, not higher prices
Commodity substitution bias When a good's relative price rises, people switch to cheaper substitutes, but the fixed basket doesn't
Outlet substitution bias People shift to cheaper stores and online sellers

The bias was estimated at about 1.1 percentage points a year (the Boskin Commission, 1996). It distorts private contracts and raises government outlays, since pensions and benefits are linked to the CPI.

Alternative measures:

  • Chained CPI: uses current and previous-period quantities, which reduces substitution bias.
  • Personal consumption expenditure (PCE) price index: uses data from the national accounts on all consumption spending.
  • Core inflation: excludes the volatile prices of food and fuel, to show the underlying trend. The Fed watches core PCE inflation.
Memory trick

"New Quality Substitutes Outlets" (NQSO)

New goods, Quality change, commodity Substitution, Outlet substitution: the four reasons the CPI overstates inflation.

Try itPredicted question: which bias? Smartphones replace landlines; people switch to discount online stores

Smartphones: new goods bias. Discount online stores: outlet substitution bias.

Remember
  • The CPI overstates inflation: new goods, quality, commodity and outlet substitution biases
  • Chained CPI reduces substitution bias
  • PCE index uses national accounts data
  • Core inflation excludes food and fuel
