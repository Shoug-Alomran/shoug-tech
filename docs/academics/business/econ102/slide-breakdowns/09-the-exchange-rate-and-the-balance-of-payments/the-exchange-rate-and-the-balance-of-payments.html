The big picture

In one sentenceThe exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market; central banks can let it float, fix it or let it crawl, and the balance of payments records how a country pays for its trade and whether it's a net borrower or lender.

Chapter 9 is on Major II. The group work uses textbook problems: deciding whether a currency appreciated or depreciated, shifting demand and supply of a currency, explaining a currency peg and what happens to official reserves, calculating the three balance of payments accounts, and the sector balances identity (net exports = private balance + government balance). Get the shifters of demand and supply right; that's where most marks are lost.

Where this fits

Chapter 7 built the loanable funds market and Chapter 8 the money market. This chapter opens the economy to the world: interest rate differences drive currency flows, and net exports feed into aggregate demand in Chapter 10.

By the end you can

  • Define the exchange rate, appreciation and depreciation
  • Explain the demand for and supply of a currency and why the curves slope as they do
  • Predict how the exchange rate changes when demand or supply shifts
  • Explain arbitrage, interest rate parity, purchasing power parity and the real exchange rate
  • Compare flexible, fixed and crawling peg exchange rate policies
  • Calculate the balance of payments accounts and the sector balances

Lesson 1

The foreign exchange market

Must knowSlides 4-9

To buy goods, services or assets produced in another country, we need that country's currency. We get it in the foreign exchange market, where one currency is traded for another.

The exchange rate is the price at which one currency exchanges for another, for example 3.75 riyals per U.S. dollar.

  • Appreciation: a currency's value rises in terms of another currency. You get more of the other currency for one unit.
  • Depreciation: a currency's value falls. You get less of the other currency.
Try itGroup work, Problem 1: the U.S. dollar went from 1.24 to 1.29 Canadian dollars and from 121 to 108 yen

Against the Canadian dollar, one U.S. dollar buys more (1.29 > 1.24), so it appreciated. Against the yen it buys fewer (108 < 121), so it depreciated.

Memory trick

"More for one = Mighty"

If one unit of your currency buys more foreign currency, your currency got mighty (appreciated). Buys less: it got weaker (depreciated).

Exam trap

Watch which currency is in the denominator

If the dollar appreciates against the yen, the yen depreciates against the dollar. The two always move in opposite directions. Read the quote carefully: "yen per dollar" rising means the dollar is appreciating.

An exchange rate is a price, determined by demand and supply. The demand for one money is the supply of another: people who want dollars offer their own currency in exchange.

Remember
  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another
  • Appreciation: buys more foreign currency; depreciation: buys less
  • One currency's appreciation is the other's depreciation
  • The demand for one currency is the supply of another
Lesson 2

Demand and supply in the foreign exchange market

Must knowSlides 10-21

Demand for U.S. dollars comes from foreigners who want to buy U.S. exports and U.S. assets. It depends on the exchange rate, world demand for U.S. exports, interest rates in the U.S. and abroad, and the expected future exchange rate.

The demand curve slopes downward because of two effects:

  • Exports effect: a lower exchange rate makes U.S. exports cheaper for foreigners, so they buy more, and need more dollars.
  • Expected profit effect: for a given expected future exchange rate, a lower rate today means a bigger expected profit from buying dollars now.

Supply of U.S. dollars comes from Americans who want to buy imports and foreign assets. The supply curve slopes upward because of:

  • Imports effect: a higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper for Americans, so they buy more and sell more dollars to get foreign currency.
  • Expected profit effect: a higher rate today means a bigger expected profit from selling dollars now and buying foreign currency.

Equilibrium: the exchange rate where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Above it there's a surplus of dollars and the rate falls; below it, a shortage and the rate rises.

Memory trick

"Exports demand, Imports supply"

Foreigners buying our exports create demand for our currency. We buying imports create supply of our currency.

Remember
  • Demand for dollars: foreigners buying U.S. exports and assets; slopes down
  • Supply of dollars: Americans buying imports and foreign assets; slopes up
  • Exports effect and imports effect, plus expected profit effects, explain the slopes
  • Surplus above equilibrium pushes the rate down; shortage below pushes it up
Lesson 3

Changes in the exchange rate

Must knowSlides 22-30

A change in the exchange rate itself is a movement along the curves. These shift them:

Influence Demand for dollars Supply of dollars
World demand for U.S. exports rises Increases
U.S. demand for imports rises Increases
U.S. interest rate differential rises (U.S. rate − foreign rate) Increases Decreases
Expected future exchange rate rises Increases Decreases

The U.S. interest rate differential is the U.S. interest rate minus the foreign interest rate. A bigger differential makes U.S. assets more attractive: foreigners demand more dollars and Americans supply fewer.

Change Exchange rate
Demand increases or supply decreases Appreciates (rises)
Demand decreases or supply increases Depreciates (falls)
Try itGroup work, Problem 5: global demand for roses rises and Colombia's central bank raises its interest rate

a) Demand for pesos increases: foreigners need pesos to buy more Colombian roses, and peso assets now pay more. b) Supply of pesos decreases: with higher interest at home, Colombians move less money abroad. c–d) As the exchange rate rises, people move along the new curves: the quantity of pesos demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises relative to the old rate, until they're equal again. e) The peso appreciates against the U.S. dollar. (The equilibrium quantity of pesos traded may rise or fall.)

Memory trick

Higher interest rate → Hotter currency

A rise in a country's interest rate heats up its currency: demand up, supply down, the currency appreciates.

Exam trap

Both curves can shift from one event

A change in the interest rate differential or the expected future exchange rate shifts both demand and supply, in opposite directions. That's why the exchange rate is so volatile.

Remember
  • The exchange rate changing moves along the curves; other influences shift them
  • Higher interest rate differential: demand up, supply down, currency appreciates
  • Higher expected future exchange rate: demand up, supply down
  • Exports shift demand; imports shift supply
Lesson 4

Arbitrage, speculation and market fundamentals

Good to knowSlides 31-40
  • Arbitrage: buying in one market and selling for a higher price in another related market. It drives the law of one price: if an item is traded in more than one place, it sells for the same price everywhere (once converted into the same currency).
  • Interest rate parity: equal rates of return in different currencies, once the expected change in the exchange rate is included. A country with a higher interest rate is expected to see its currency depreciate, wiping out the gain.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): equal value of money; one dollar buys the same amount everywhere. If prices rise faster in one country, its currency depreciates.
  • Speculation: trading in the hope of profiting from buying low and selling high. Expectations of the future exchange rate are a big part of why the rate is volatile.
  • Real exchange rate: the relative price of domestic and foreign goods: RER = E × (P ÷ P), where E is the nominal rate and P the foreign price level. In the long run the real exchange rate is determined by demand and supply in the goods market, and price levels by money.
Memory trick

"PPP = same Purchasing Power, everywhere"

Under PPP, money has the same purchasing power in every country. Under interest rate parity, assets have the same return in every currency.

Remember
  • Arbitrage enforces the law of one price
  • Interest rate parity: equal returns after expected exchange rate changes
  • PPP: money buys the same amount everywhere
  • Speculation and changing expectations make exchange rates volatile
  • Real exchange rate = E × P ÷ P*
Lesson 5

Exchange rate policy

Must knowSlides 41-45
Policy How it works
Flexible exchange rate Demand and supply decide the rate, with no direct intervention by the central bank (the U.S. dollar)
Fixed exchange rate The central bank pegs the rate at a value it chooses and intervenes in the market to hold it (the Saudi riyal at 3.75 per dollar, the Hong Kong dollar at 7.8)
Crawling peg The rate follows a planned path: the target is changed at a set pace (China has operated something close to it)

Defending a peg. If demand for the currency falls, the central bank buys its own currency with foreign currency: its official reserves fall. If demand rises, it sells its own currency and buys foreign currency: official reserves rise. A central bank can't keep selling reserves forever, so a peg under constant downward pressure eventually breaks.

Try itGroup work, Problem 26: Hong Kong keeps its dollar at 7.8 per U.S. dollar while the U.S. dollar falls

a) A fixed exchange rate (a peg to the U.S. dollar). b) The U.S. dollar was falling, which would push the Hong Kong dollar up. To hold 7.8, the central bank is stopping the Hong Kong dollar from rising: it supplies Hong Kong dollars in exchange for U.S. dollars. c) It's taking in U.S. dollars, so Hong Kong's official reserves increase.

Memory trick

Defending against a fall drains the vault

If a central bank props up a falling currency, it buys its own money with reserves, so the vault drains. Holding down a rising currency fills the vault.

Remember
  • Flexible: market sets the rate; fixed: central bank pegs it; crawling peg: planned path
  • Holding up a weak currency: buy it, official reserves fall
  • Holding down a strong currency: sell it, official reserves rise
  • A peg under steady pressure runs out of reserves
Lesson 6

The balance of payments

Must knowSlides 46-50

A country's balance of payments accounts record its international trading, borrowing and lending. There are three accounts:

Account Records
Current account Exports − imports + net interest income + net transfers
Capital and financial account Foreign investment in the country − the country's investment abroad (+ statistical discrepancy)
Official settlements account Net change in the country's official reserves

The three accounts sum to zero: current + capital and financial + official settlements = 0. An increase in official reserves shows up as a negative official settlements balance.

Step by step

Group work, Problem 10: U.S. 2015 ($ billions): imports 2,764, exports 2,264, foreign investment in U.S. 502, U.S. investment abroad 175, net interest 193, net transfers −127, statistical discrepancy 101

  1. Current account = 2,264 − 2,764 + 193 − 127 = −$434 billion (a deficit).
  2. Capital and financial account = 502 − 175 + 101 (discrepancy) = +$428 billion.
  3. Official settlements = −(−434 + 428) = +$6 billion (official reserves fell by $6 billion).
  4. The U.S. was a net borrower: foreigners invested much more in the U.S. than Americans invested abroad, financing the current account deficit.
Memory trick

"Current = Goods, Capital = IOUs, Official = the Vault"

The current account tracks goods, services, interest and transfers. Capital and financial tracks borrowing and lending. Official settlements tracks the central bank's vault of reserves.

Remember
  • Current account = X − M + net interest + net transfers
  • Capital and financial account = foreign investment in the country − its investment abroad
  • Official settlements = change in official reserves (an increase is recorded as negative)
  • The three accounts sum to zero
Lesson 7

Borrowers, lenders, debtors and creditors

Good to knowSlides 51-58
  • A net borrower borrows more from the rest of the world than it lends in a given year; a net lender does the opposite. These are flows.
  • A debtor nation has, over its history, borrowed more than it has lent; a creditor nation has lent more. These are stocks. The United States became a debtor nation in 1986.
  • In the global loanable funds market, funds flow to where they earn the highest return, so there's one world real interest rate. A country whose saving at that rate is less than its investment borrows from abroad; one whose saving exceeds investment lends.
  • Borrowing isn't a problem if the funds finance investment that earns a return. It's a problem if they finance consumption.
Memory trick

Borrower is a flow, Debtor is a pile

Net borrower: this year's flow. Debtor nation: the pile of debt built up over all past years.

Remember
  • Net borrower/lender: this year's flow
  • Debtor/creditor nation: the accumulated stock
  • One world real interest rate links national loanable funds markets
  • Borrowing to invest is sustainable; borrowing to consume isn't
Lesson 8

The current account and sector balances

Must knowSlides 59-62

From GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) and Y = C + S + T, we get the sector balances identity:

Sector balances
Private sector balance = saving − investment = S − I
Government sector balance = net taxes − government expenditure = T − G
Net exports = private sector balance + government sector balance
X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
Step by step

Group work, Problems 27–28: U.K. (£ billions): C 721, X 277, G 230, net taxes 217, I 181, saving 162

  1. Private sector balance = S − I = 162 − 181 = −£19 billion (a deficit).
  2. Government sector balance = T − G = 217 − 230 = −£13 billion (a deficit).
  3. Net exports = −19 + (−13) = −£32 billion, so imports = 277 + 32 = £309 billion.
  4. Relationship: a government deficit adds to the net exports deficit; with a private deficit too, the U.K. must borrow from abroad.

Where's the exchange rate? In the short run, a deficit or surplus in the current account doesn't depend on the exchange rate; in the long run, the real exchange rate is determined by the real forces of demand and supply and doesn't affect the current account balance either. The current account balance is decided by saving and investment decisions.

Exam trap

A weaker currency doesn't automatically fix a deficit

The current account is driven by S − I and T − G. Cutting a government deficit or raising private saving narrows a current account deficit; a cheaper currency alone doesn't, in the long run.

Remember
  • X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
  • Private balance = S − I; government balance = T − G
  • A government deficit tends to come with a net exports deficit (twin deficits)
  • Saving and investment, not the exchange rate, decide the current account in the long run

Quick reference

Basics

  • Exchange rate = price of a currency
  • Appreciate: buys more foreign currency
  • One up = the other down

Demand and supply

  • Demand: exports effect, slopes down
  • Supply: imports effect, slopes up
  • Interest differential up: D up, S down
  • Expected future rate up: D up, S down

Parity

  • Law of one price
  • Interest rate parity: equal returns
  • PPP: equal purchasing power
  • RER = E × P ÷ P*

Policy

  • Flexible, fixed, crawling peg
  • Defend a falling currency: reserves fall
  • Hold down a rising one: reserves rise

BOP

  • CA = X − M + interest + transfers
  • KA = foreign investment in − investment abroad
  • CA + KA + official settlements = 0

Sector balances

  • X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
  • Net borrower: flow; debtor: stock
