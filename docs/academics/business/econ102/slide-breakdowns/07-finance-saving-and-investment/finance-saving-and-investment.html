The big picture

In one sentenceFinancial markets channel saving into investment: the real interest rate adjusts until the supply of loanable funds (saving) equals the demand (investment), and a government budget deficit raises the real interest rate and crowds out private investment unless households save more (the Ricardo-Barro effect).

Chapter 7 is on Quiz II and Assignment 2. Expect quick calculations (net investment, capital stock, saving and wealth, the real interest rate, present value and NPV), solvency versus liquidity, and many "which way does the curve shift and what happens to the real interest rate?" questions. The group work uses a loanable funds table to find equilibrium, crowding out and the Ricardo-Barro outcome.

Where this fits

Chapter 6 said capital accumulation drives growth. This chapter shows where the funds for that capital come from. Chapter 8 adds money and banks, and Chapter 13 returns to deficits as fiscal policy.

By the end you can

  • Distinguish physical capital from financial capital, and gross from net investment
  • Calculate saving, wealth, the real interest rate, present value and NPV
  • Tell insolvency from illiquidity, and explain why asset prices and interest rates move opposite ways
  • Explain the demand for and supply of loanable funds and find the equilibrium real interest rate
  • Predict the effects of shifts in demand and supply
  • Explain crowding out and the Ricardo-Barro effect

Lesson 1

Capital, investment, wealth and saving

Must knowSlides 4-12
Term Meaning
Physical capital Tools, machines, buildings, vehicles (a Taco Bell store, Hertz's rental cars)
Financial capital The funds firms use to buy physical capital (shares, bonds, loans)
Gross investment Total spending on new capital
Depreciation The fall in the value of capital from wear and tear and obsolescence
Net investment Gross investment − depreciation = the change in the value of capital
Wealth The value of everything a person owns: a stock
Saving Disposable income − consumption: a flow that adds to wealth
Capital and wealth
Net investment = gross investment − depreciation
Capital at end of year = capital at start + net investment
Saving = disposable income − consumption expenditure
Wealth at end = wealth at start + saving (+ capital gains − capital losses)
Step by step

Group work, Problems 1–2: Michael owns $400,000 of capital, buys $500,000 of new servers, and old servers lose $100,000 in value

  1. Gross investment = $500,000.
  2. Depreciation = $100,000.
  3. Net investment = 500,000 − 100,000 = $400,000.
  4. Capital at the end of 2022 = 400,000 + 400,000 = $800,000.
Step by step

Assignment 2, Q2: Tim's Gyms owns $1 million of machines, they lose 30% of their value, and Tim buys $200,000 of new machines

  1. Depreciation = 30% × $1,000,000 = $300,000.
  2. Net investment = 200,000 − 300,000 = −$100,000: the capital stock shrank.
Step by step

Group work, Problem 3: Lori earns $20,000 after tax plus $300 interest, spends $15,300, and starts with $1,000 of assets and $5,000 of savings

  1. Disposable income = 20,000 + 300 = $20,300.
  2. Saving = 20,300 − 15,300 = $5,000.
  3. Wealth at start = 1,000 + 5,000 = $6,000; at end = 6,000 + 5,000 = $11,000.

Financial markets are where firms get financial capital:

  • Loan markets: businesses borrow from banks; households take mortgages.
  • Bond markets: a bond is a promise to make specified payments on specified dates.
  • Stock markets: a stock (share) is a certificate of ownership and a claim to the firm's profits.

Financial institutions (commercial banks, government-sponsored mortgage lenders, pension funds, insurance companies) operate on both sides of these markets: they borrow in one and lend in another. Investment is financed by private saving + government budget surplus + borrowing from the rest of the world.

Exam trap

Shares and salaries aren't physical capital

Assignment 2, Q1: physical capital is II and III only, the restaurant building and the rental cars. Shares of stock are financial capital, and salaries are a payment to labor.

Memory trick

Wealth is a Stock, saving is a Stream

Wealth is a stock (measured at a point in time, like water in a tank). Saving is a stream (a flow per period) that fills the tank.

Remember
  • Physical capital is real equipment; financial capital is the funds that buy it
  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation
  • Saving = disposable income − consumption; saving adds to wealth
  • Investment is financed by private saving, the budget surplus and borrowing from abroad
Lesson 2

Net present value and financial decisions

Must knowSlides 13-15

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future, because today's dollar can earn interest.

Future value, present value and NPV
Future value = present value × (1 + r)ⁿ
Present value = future amount ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
NPV = present value of future returns − cost today
Decision rule: invest if NPV > 0
Step by step

Assignment 2, Q4: present value of $200 in two years at 7%

  1. PV = 200 ÷ (1.07)² = 200 ÷ 1.1449 = $174.69 ≈ $175.
Step by step

Group work, Problems 4–5 and extension (r = 10%)

  1. Tom borrows $2,000 for two years: repays 2,000 × (1.10)² = $2,420.
  2. Joe wants $5,000 in two years: deposits 5,000 ÷ 1.21 = $4,132.23 today.
  3. A project costs $4,000 and pays $5,000 in two years: NPV = 4,132.23 − 4,000 = $132.23 > 0, so undertake it.
Memory trick

Future: multiply. Present: divide.

Going forward in time, multiply by (1 + r) for each year. Coming back to today, divide.

Remember
  • PV = future amount ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
  • NPV = PV of returns − cost
  • Invest when NPV is positive
  • A higher interest rate lowers present values
Lesson 3

Financial risk, asset prices and real interest rates

Must knowSlides 16-19
Insolvent Illiquid
Net worth (assets − liabilities) is negative Has enough assets but can't turn them into cash to pay now
A solvency problem: the firm owes more than it owns A cash problem: it can usually borrow to get through

Interest rates and asset prices move in opposite directions. A bond paying $5 a year has an interest rate of 5% if its price is $100. If the price rises to $200, the same $5 is only a 2.5% return.

Real interest rate
Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate
Try itAssignment 2, Q3: nominal rate 8%, inflation 2%. Real interest rate?

8 − 2 = 6%.

Try itAssignment 2, Q5: a bank's net worth is negative. It is definitely…

Insolvent. Negative net worth is the definition of insolvency; illiquidity is about cash, not net worth.

Memory trick

"Insolvent = In the red"

Insolvent: net worth in the red (negative). Illiquid: ill with no cash right now.

Exam trap

Bond price up, interest rate down

When the price of a bond rises, its interest rate (yield) falls. They always move in opposite directions.

Remember
  • Insolvent: negative net worth; illiquid: can't pay right now
  • Asset prices and interest rates move in opposite directions
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation
  • The real interest rate is the opportunity cost of loanable funds
Lesson 4

The demand for loanable funds

Must knowSlides 20-23

The market for loanable funds is the aggregate of all the individual financial markets. The quantity demanded depends on the real interest rate and expected profit.

  • The demand curve slopes downward: at a lower real interest rate, more projects are profitable, so firms borrow more.
  • Expected profit shifts it: higher expected profit (from technological change or a business cycle expansion) shifts demand rightward.
Try itGroup work, Problems 6–7: First Call does $8m of projects at 7%, $10m at 6% and $12m at 5%. What if expected profit doubles?

Plotted, these points form a downward-sloping demand for loanable funds. If expected profit doubles, more projects are profitable at every interest rate, so demand shifts rightward.

Try itAssignment 2, Q6: technological progress raises expected profit

The demand for loanable funds shifts rightward and the real interest rate rises.

Remember
  • Demand for loanable funds = investment demand
  • Slopes down: a lower real interest rate makes more projects worthwhile
  • Higher expected profit shifts it rightward
Lesson 5

The supply of loanable funds

Must knowSlides 24-27

The quantity supplied depends on the real interest rate, disposable income, expected future income, wealth and default risk.

  • The supply curve slopes upward: a higher real interest rate rewards saving, so people save more.
  • It shifts rightward when disposable income rises, and leftward when wealth or expected future income rises (people feel richer and save less) or default risk rises.
Memory trick

Supply shifters: "D-E-W-D"

Disposable income (up → more saving), Expected future income (up → less), Wealth (up → less), Default risk (up → less). Only disposable income moves supply the same way it moves.

Try itAssignment 2, Q8: household disposable income falls by 0.3%. Effect on the real interest rate?

Saving falls, so the supply of loanable funds decreases (shifts left) and the real interest rate rises.

Try itAssignment 2, Q9: disposable income increases

People increase saving, the supply of loanable funds increases, and the real interest rate falls.

Remember
  • Supply of loanable funds = saving
  • Slopes up: a higher real interest rate encourages saving
  • Higher disposable income increases supply
  • Higher wealth, expected future income or default risk decreases supply
Lesson 6

Equilibrium and changes

Must knowSlides 28-32

The real interest rate adjusts until the quantity of loanable funds demanded equals the quantity supplied.

Real interest rate (%) Investment demanded ($ trillion) Saving supplied ($ trillion)
4 8.5 5.5
5 8.0 6.0
6 7.5 6.5
7 7.0 7.0
8 6.5 7.5
9 6.0 8.0
10 5.5 8.5
  • Above equilibrium (say 8%): saving exceeds investment, a surplus of funds, so the rate falls.
  • Below equilibrium: a shortage, so the rate rises.
Change Real interest rate Quantity of saving and investment
Demand increases Rises Increases
Demand decreases Falls Decreases
Supply increases Falls Increases
Supply decreases Rises Decreases
Step by step

Group work, Problem 8: equilibrium, more saving, more investment

  1. a) Equilibrium: 7%, with investment = saving = $7 trillion.
  2. b) Saving rises by $0.5 trillion at every rate: at 7% saving is 7.5 > 7.0; at 6% saving is 7.0 < 7.5. The new equilibrium is about 6.5% and $7.25 trillion: more supply lowers the rate and raises the quantity.
  3. c) Investment rises by $1 trillion at every rate: at 8%, investment = 7.5 = saving. New equilibrium 8% and $7.5 trillion: more demand raises the rate and the quantity.
Try itGroup work, Problem 23: Vodafone cuts investment because expected profit falls

The demand for loanable funds decreases, the real interest rate falls, and the equilibrium quantity of saving and investment decreases.

Try itAssignment 2, Q7: the real interest rate is 8%, above equilibrium

There is a surplus of loanable funds: more is saved than firms want to borrow, so the rate falls.

Remember
  • Equilibrium where saving supplied = investment demanded
  • Above equilibrium: surplus; below: shortage
  • More demand raises the real interest rate; more supply lowers it
Lesson 7

Government in the loanable funds market

Must knowSlides 33-36
  • A budget surplus adds to the supply of loanable funds: the real interest rate falls, private investment rises, and saving falls back somewhat.
  • A budget deficit adds to the demand for loanable funds: the real interest rate rises, and private investment decreases. The fall in private investment is crowding out.
  • The Ricardo-Barro effect: taxpayers expect future taxes to repay the debt, so they save more today. Private saving rises by the amount of the deficit, the real interest rate doesn't change, and there's no crowding out.
Step by step

Group work, Problem 9: a $1 trillion budget deficit, no Ricardo-Barro effect

  1. Total demand = investment + 1. At 8%: 6.5 + 1 = 7.5 = saving 7.5.
  2. New real interest rate = 8%; private investment = $6.5 trillion.
  3. Crowding out = 7.0 − 6.5 = $0.5 trillion.
Step by step

Group work, Problem 10: households save the full extra $1 trillion (Ricardo-Barro)

  1. Demand and supply both rise by $1 trillion, so the real interest rate stays at 7%.
  2. Private investment stays at $7 trillion: no crowding out.
Try itGroup work, Problem 28: Kuwait runs a budget surplus

The supply of loanable funds increases, the real interest rate falls, private investment rises, and faster capital accumulation boosts long-run economic growth.

Try itAssignment 2, Q10: a larger deficit with no Ricardo-Barro effect

The demand for loanable funds increases and the real interest rate rises.

Memory trick

Deficit → Demand, Surplus → Supply

A deficit adds to demand; a surplus adds to supply. Both start with the same letter.

Exam trap

Ricardo-Barro means no change in the rate

If the question says households save the whole deficit, the real interest rate and private investment don't change. Crowding out happens only without the Ricardo-Barro effect.

Remember
  • Budget surplus: supply of funds up, real interest rate down, investment up
  • Budget deficit: demand for funds up, real interest rate up, private investment down (crowding out)
  • Ricardo-Barro: saving rises by the deficit, so no crowding out

Quick reference

Capital

  • Net investment = gross − depreciation
  • End capital = start + net investment
  • Shares are financial, not physical

Saving and wealth

  • Saving = disposable income − consumption
  • End wealth = start + saving

Present value

  • PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
  • NPV > 0: invest

Risk and rates

  • Insolvent: net worth < 0
  • Illiquid: no cash now
  • Bond price up, rate down
  • Real r ≈ nominal − inflation

Loanable funds

  • Demand: slopes down, expected profit
  • Supply: slopes up, disposable income
  • Above equilibrium: surplus

Government

  • Deficit: demand up, r up, crowding out
  • Surplus: supply up, r down
  • Ricardo-Barro: no crowding out
