The big picture

In one sentenceThe AS-AD model explains real GDP and the price level: long-run aggregate supply is vertical at potential GDP, short-run aggregate supply slopes up because money wages are fixed for a while, aggregate demand slopes down, and shifts in these curves explain growth, inflation and the business cycle.

Chapter 10 is on Major II, and it's the model the rest of the course is built on. The student sheet and the AD/AS shifters study guide make clear what gets tested: LAS vs SAS (shape and why), movement along vs shift, the three reasons AD slopes down, which event shifts which curve and in which direction, and reading short-run and long-run equilibrium, inflationary and recessionary gaps. Learn the four-step method for any "what happens if…" question.

Where this fits

Chapters 6 and 5 gave you potential GDP and the price level. Chapters 7–9 gave you the interest rate, money and the exchange rate, which all shift AD here. Chapter 11 looks inside AD (the multiplier), Chapter 12 uses AS-AD for inflation cycles, and Chapters 13–14 use it for fiscal and monetary policy.

By the end you can

  • Explain LAS and SAS, why one is vertical and the other slopes up
  • Explain what shifts LAS and SAS
  • Explain why the AD curve slopes down (three effects) and what shifts it
  • Find short-run and long-run macroeconomic equilibrium
  • Identify inflationary and recessionary gaps and explain how they close
  • Use AS-AD to explain growth, inflation and the business cycle

Lesson 1

Aggregate supply: LAS and SAS

Must knowSlides 4-9

The quantity of real GDP supplied is the total quantity of goods and services that firms plan to produce in a given period. Aggregate supply is the relationship between the quantity of real GDP supplied and the price level. There are two time frames:

Long-run aggregate supply (LAS) Short-run aggregate supply (SAS)
Shape Vertical at potential GDP Upward sloping
Condition Real GDP = potential GDP Money wage rate, other factor prices and potential GDP held constant
Why Potential GDP doesn't depend on the price level: the money wage rate changes by the same percentage as the price level, so the real wage stays at its full-employment level If the price level rises while the money wage rate stays the same, the real wage falls, production is more profitable, and firms produce more

Where SAS cuts LAS, the money wage rate is at its full-employment level. Above potential GDP, SAS is above LAS; below potential, it's below.

Think of it like this

The sticky wage contract

Workers sign wage contracts for a year. If prices jump in March, their money wage doesn't change until the contract ends, so labor is cheaper in real terms and firms hire more and produce more. That's SAS. Once contracts are renegotiated and wages catch up, firms are back at potential GDP: that's LAS.

Memory trick

"Long run Stands, Short run Slopes"

LAS stands straight up (vertical). SAS slopes up. AD slopes down.

Try itStudent sheet, quick check: why is the SAS curve upward sloping?

Because money wages don't immediately change with the price level. A higher price level with an unchanged money wage lowers the real wage, so firms produce more.

Try itStudent sheet, true or false: a change in the price level shifts the SAS curve

False. A change in the price level, with money wages and potential GDP constant, is a movement along the SAS curve.

Remember
  • LAS is vertical at potential GDP; potential GDP doesn't depend on the price level
  • SAS slopes up because the money wage rate is fixed in the short run
  • A price level change moves along SAS; it doesn't shift it
  • SAS cuts LAS where the money wage rate is at its full-employment level
Lesson 2

Changes in aggregate supply

Must knowSlides 10-13
Change LAS SAS
Potential GDP increases (more labor, capital, technology, education, better institutions) Shifts right Shifts right
Money wage rate rises (or other factor prices, such as oil, rise) No change Shifts left
Productivity rises Right Right
Business taxes, regulations or a natural disaster raise costs No change Shifts left
Memory trick

Capacity moves both; Costs move SAS

A change in the economy's capacity (potential GDP) shifts both LAS and SAS. A change in costs (money wages, raw material prices) shifts only SAS.

Try itStudent sheet: which change shifts BOTH LAS and SAS rightward?

Technological progress: it raises potential GDP. A higher money wage only shifts SAS left; a higher price level is a movement along SAS.

Exam trap

A higher money wage doesn't move LAS

Potential GDP depends on real resources, not on money wages. A rise in the money wage rate shifts SAS left and leaves LAS where it is.

Remember
  • Higher potential GDP shifts LAS and SAS right
  • A higher money wage rate or raw material price shifts SAS left only
  • LAS moves only when productive capacity changes
Lesson 3

Aggregate demand and why it slopes down

Must knowSlides 14-20

The quantity of real GDP demanded is the total amount of final goods and services produced in the country that people, businesses, governments and foreigners plan to buy: Y = C + I + G + X − M. Aggregate demand is the relationship between the quantity of real GDP demanded and the price level.

The AD curve slopes downward because of three effects of a rise in the price level:

Effect Chain
Wealth effect Price level ↑ → real value of wealth ↓ → people save more, spend less → quantity demanded ↓
Intertemporal substitution effect Price level ↑ → real value of money ↓ → interest rate ↑ → less borrowing and spending now → quantity demanded ↓
International substitution effect Price level ↑ → domestic goods dearer relative to foreign goods → exports ↓, imports ↑ → quantity demanded ↓
Memory trick

"WII": Wealth, Intertemporal, International

The AD curve slopes down for three reasons: Wealth, Intertemporal substitution, International substitution. "WII go down."

Exam trap

Price level changes move along AD

A change in the price level is a movement along the AD curve. Only other influences shift it.

Remember
  • AD: quantity of real GDP demanded against the price level
  • Slopes down because of wealth, intertemporal and international substitution effects
  • A change in the price level moves along AD
Lesson 4

Changes in aggregate demand

Must knowSlides 21-26

The curve shifts when anything other than the price level changes buying plans:

Shifter (from the AD study guide) Effect on AD Why
Government expenditure (G) ↑ Right G is a component of aggregate expenditure
Taxes (T) ↑ Left Lower disposable income, so less consumption
Interest rate ↑ Left Less investment and consumption
Quantity of money ↑ Right Interest rate tends to fall, spending rises
Foreign real GDP ↑ Right More demand for exports
Exchange rate ↑ (appreciation) Left Exports dearer, imports cheaper, net exports fall
Expected future income, inflation or profit ↑ (consumer confidence) Right People and firms spend more now
  • Fiscal policy is the government's attempt to influence the economy by changing taxes, transfers and government expenditure.
  • Monetary policy is the Fed's attempt to influence the economy by changing the interest rate and the quantity of money.
Memory trick

"Four steps" from the study guide

  1. Identify the event: spending, production costs, or productive capacity? 2. Pick the curve: spending → AD, costs → SAS, capacity → LAS. 3. Direction: does it raise or lower spending, costs or capacity? 4. Explain the effect on real GDP and the price level.
Exam trap

A stronger currency shifts AD left

An appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, so net exports fall and AD shifts left. Students often guess "right" because a strong currency sounds good.

Remember
  • Fiscal policy (G up, T down) shifts AD right
  • Monetary policy (lower interest rate, more money) shifts AD right
  • Higher foreign income shifts AD right; an appreciation shifts it left
  • Higher expected income, inflation or profit shifts AD right
Lesson 5

Short-run macroeconomic equilibrium

Must knowSlides 27-29

Short-run macroeconomic equilibrium occurs when the quantity of real GDP demanded equals the quantity supplied: the point where AD intersects SAS. Real GDP can be above, below or equal to potential GDP.

If the price level is above equilibrium, firms can't sell all they produce, so they cut output and prices; if it's below, they raise output and prices. These adjustments move the economy along the SAS curve to equilibrium.

Remember
  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SAS
  • Real GDP can be above, below or at potential GDP in the short run
  • Firms adjust output and prices along SAS to reach it
Lesson 6

Long-run equilibrium, growth and inflation

Must knowSlides 30-34

Long-run macroeconomic equilibrium occurs when real GDP equals potential GDP: the economy is on the LAS curve, where AD, SAS and LAS all meet. The money wage rate adjusts to bring the economy there.

  • Economic growth: potential GDP grows, so LAS shifts right.
  • Inflation: the quantity of money grows faster than potential GDP, so AD shifts right faster than LAS.
Memory trick

Growth = LAS right; Inflation = AD outruns LAS

Growth pushes LAS right. Inflation happens when AD runs to the right faster than LAS.

Remember
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD, SAS and LAS meet; real GDP = potential GDP
  • Growth: LAS shifts right over time
  • Inflation: AD grows faster than LAS (money grows faster than potential GDP)
Lesson 7

The business cycle and fluctuations

Must knowSlides 35-39

The business cycle happens because AD and SAS fluctuate while the money wage rate doesn't change fast enough to keep real GDP at potential.

Equilibrium Name Unemployment
Real GDP above potential GDP Inflationary gap Below the natural rate
Real GDP equal to potential GDP Full-employment equilibrium At the natural rate
Real GDP below potential GDP Recessionary gap Above the natural rate

Fluctuations in aggregate demand. Starting at full employment, AD increases:

  1. Short run: AD shifts right along SAS. Real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises: an inflationary gap.
  2. Long run: workers demand higher money wages to restore the real wage, so SAS shifts left. The price level rises further and real GDP returns to potential GDP.

A decrease in AD does the opposite: a recessionary gap opens; money wages slowly fall, SAS shifts right, and real GDP returns to potential at a lower price level (but this can take a long time).

Fluctuations in aggregate supply. A rise in the price of oil shifts SAS left: real GDP falls and the price level rises at the same time. That combination is stagflation.

Step by step

An increase in aggregate demand from full employment

  1. AD shifts right; the economy moves up along SAS.
  2. Real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises: an inflationary gap.
  3. Labor is scarce, so the money wage rate rises and SAS shifts left.
  4. The economy returns to LAS: real GDP back at potential, price level higher.
Memory trick

"Gap closes through wages"

An inflationary gap closes when money wages rise (SAS shifts left). A recessionary gap closes when money wages fall (SAS shifts right). In the long run, AD changes move only the price level.

Exam trap

Stagflation comes from supply, not demand

When real GDP falls and the price level rises, the cause is a leftward shift of SAS (higher oil prices or money wages). A fall in AD lowers both real GDP and the price level.

Remember
  • Inflationary gap: real GDP above potential; recessionary gap: below
  • An AD increase: short run, real GDP and the price level rise; long run, money wages rise and real GDP returns to potential
  • An SAS decrease (e.g. oil prices up): real GDP falls, price level rises (stagflation)
  • In the long run, real GDP returns to potential GDP

Quick reference

Aggregate supply

  • LAS: vertical at potential GDP
  • SAS: slopes up; money wages fixed
  • Price level change: move along SAS

AS shifters

  • Potential GDP up: LAS and SAS right
  • Money wage up: SAS left only
  • Oil price up: SAS left only

Aggregate demand

  • Slopes down: wealth, intertemporal, international
  • Price level change: move along AD

AD shifters

  • G up, T down: right
  • Interest rate down, money up: right
  • Foreign income up: right
  • Appreciation: left

Equilibrium

  • Short run: AD = SAS
  • Long run: AD = SAS = LAS
  • Above potential: inflationary gap
  • Below potential: recessionary gap

Adjustment

  • AD up: P up, Y up, then wages up
  • SAS left: stagflation
  • Long run: Y = potential
