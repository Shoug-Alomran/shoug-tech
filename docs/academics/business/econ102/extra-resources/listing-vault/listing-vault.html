Chapter 1 · What Is Economics?

Economics studies how people, businesses, governments and whole societies choose to use scarce resources, and it does so by thinking in tradeoffs, opportunity costs, margins and incentives.

Definitions

  1. Scarcity: wants exceed resources
  2. Economics: choices under scarcity, incentives, coordination
  3. Micro: individuals, firms, markets
  4. Macro: the whole economy

Factors and incomes

  1. Land: rent
  2. Labor: wages
  3. Capital: interest
  4. Entrepreneurship: profit

Big questions

  1. What, how, for whom?
  2. Self-interest vs social interest
  3. Social interest = efficiency + equity

Way of thinking

  1. Choice = tradeoff
  2. Opportunity cost = next best alternative
  3. Do more if MB > MC
  4. Incentives change choices

Statements

  1. Positive: what is, testable
  2. Normative: should, value judgement
  3. Ceteris paribus: other things the same

Graphs

  1. Positive: upward slope
  2. Negative: downward slope
  3. Slope = Δy ÷ Δx
  4. Third variable changes: curve shifts

Chapter 4 · Measuring the Value of Production: GDP

GDP is the market value of all the final goods and services produced within a country in a given time period; it can be measured by adding up expenditure (C + I + G + X − M) or incomes, and real GDP per person is the standard yardstick for living standards.

Definition

  1. Market value
  2. Final goods and services
  3. Produced within the country
  4. In a given period
  5. Filter: now, here, final?

Not in GDP

  1. Used goods
  2. Intermediate goods
  3. Transfer payments
  4. Shares and bonds
  5. Household production

Identity

  1. Y = C + I + G + (X − M)
  2. Production = expenditure = income
  3. Import: counted in C, subtracted in M

Gross and net

  1. Net investment = gross − depreciation
  2. NDP = GDP − depreciation
  3. Income approach: + indirect taxes − subsidies + depreciation

Real vs nominal

  1. Nominal: current Q × current P
  2. Real: current Q × base-year P
  3. Per person = real GDP ÷ population

Business cycle

  1. Peak → recession → trough → expansion
  2. Recession: 2+ quarters of falling real GDP

Limitations

  1. Household production
  2. Underground economy
  3. Leisure
  4. Environment

Chapter 5 · Monitoring Jobs and Inflation

Unemployment and inflation are the two other big macro indicators: the labor force survey sorts people into employed, unemployed and not in the labor force to give three rates, unemployment is frictional, structural or cyclical, and the CPI measures the price level whose percentage change is the inflation rate.

Labor force

  1. LF = employed + unemployed
  2. Unemployed: looked in past 4 weeks
  3. Not looking: not in LF

Three rates

  1. UR = U ÷ LF × 100
  2. LFPR = LF ÷ WAP × 100
  3. E/P = E ÷ WAP × 100

Types

  1. Frictional: job search
  2. Structural: skills mismatch
  3. Cyclical: recession
  4. Natural = frictional + structural
  5. Full employment: UR = natural rate

Inflation costs

  1. Redistributes income and wealth
  2. Borrowers gain, lenders lose
  3. Diverts resources to forecasting

CPI

  1. Base period = 100
  2. CPI = cost now ÷ cost base × 100
  3. Inflation = %Δ CPI

Bias and alternatives

  1. New goods, quality, commodity, outlet
  2. Chained CPI, PCE
  3. Core: no food and fuel

Chapter 6 · Economic Growth

Economic growth is the sustained expansion of production possibilities: potential GDP grows when the supply of labor grows or, more importantly for living standards, when labor productivity grows through physical capital, human capital and technology.

Growth rates

  1. %Δ real GDP = Δ ÷ last year × 100
  2. Per person ≈ GDP growth − population growth
  3. Rule of 70: doubling = 70 ÷ rate

Growth vs recovery

  1. Growth: PPF shifts out
  2. Recovery: back to the PPF

Potential GDP

  1. LD down, LS up → full-employment hours
  2. Production function → potential GDP
  3. Productivity = real GDP ÷ hours

Population up

  1. LS right, real wage down
  2. Potential GDP up
  3. Productivity down

Productivity up

  1. LD right, real wage up
  2. Potential GDP up
  3. Real GDP per person up

Sources

  1. Physical capital
  2. Human capital
  3. Technology
  4. Needs firms, property rights, markets

Chapter 7 · Finance, Saving, and Investment

Financial markets channel saving into investment: the real interest rate adjusts until the supply of loanable funds (saving) equals the demand (investment), and a government budget deficit raises the real interest rate and crowds out private investment unless households save more (the Ricardo-Barro effect).

Capital

  1. Net investment = gross − depreciation
  2. End capital = start + net investment
  3. Shares are financial, not physical

Saving and wealth

  1. Saving = disposable income − consumption
  2. End wealth = start + saving

Present value

  1. PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
  2. NPV > 0: invest

Risk and rates

  1. Insolvent: net worth < 0
  2. Illiquid: no cash now
  3. Bond price up, rate down
  4. Real r ≈ nominal − inflation

Loanable funds

  1. Demand: slopes down, expected profit
  2. Supply: slopes up, disposable income
  3. Above equilibrium: surplus

Government

  1. Deficit: demand up, r up, crowding out
  2. Surplus: supply up, r down
  3. Ricardo-Barro: no crowding out

Chapter 8 · Money, the Price Level, and Inflation

Money is whatever is generally accepted as a means of payment; banks create it by making loans, the Fed controls it through the monetary base, the money market sets the short-run interest rate, and in the long run the quantity theory says faster money growth means higher inflation.

Money

  1. Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value
  2. M1 = currency outside banks + demand + other liquid deposits
  3. M2 = M1 + small time deposits + money funds
  4. Cheques and credit cards aren't money

Banks

  1. Create liquidity, pool risk
  2. Lower borrowing and monitoring costs
  3. Regulated: deposit insurance, capital, reserves

The Fed

  1. Board: 7 members, 14 years
  2. FOMC: every 6 weeks
  3. MB = notes + coins + bank deposits at Fed
  4. Fed buys securities: reserves up

Money creation

  1. Unplanned = actual − desired reserves
  2. Multiplier = ΔM ÷ ΔMB
  3. More leakage, smaller multiplier

Money market

  1. MD: interest rate moves along
  2. Real GDP, P up: MD right
  3. Innovation: MD left
  4. Target quantity: vertical supply

Quantity theory

  1. MV = PY
  2. π = %ΔM + %ΔV − %ΔY

Chapter 9 · The Exchange Rate and the Balance of Payments

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market; central banks can let it float, fix it or let it crawl, and the balance of payments records how a country pays for its trade and whether it's a net borrower or lender.

Basics

  1. Exchange rate = price of a currency
  2. Appreciate: buys more foreign currency
  3. One up = the other down

Demand and supply

  1. Demand: exports effect, slopes down
  2. Supply: imports effect, slopes up
  3. Interest differential up: D up, S down
  4. Expected future rate up: D up, S down

Parity

  1. Law of one price
  2. Interest rate parity: equal returns
  3. PPP: equal purchasing power
  4. RER = E × P ÷ P*

Policy

  1. Flexible, fixed, crawling peg
  2. Defend a falling currency: reserves fall
  3. Hold down a rising one: reserves rise

BOP

  1. CA = X − M + interest + transfers
  2. KA = foreign investment in − investment abroad
  3. CA + KA + official settlements = 0

Sector balances

  1. X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
  2. Net borrower: flow; debtor: stock

Chapter 10 · Aggregate Supply and Aggregate Demand

The AS-AD model explains real GDP and the price level: long-run aggregate supply is vertical at potential GDP, short-run aggregate supply slopes up because money wages are fixed for a while, aggregate demand slopes down, and shifts in these curves explain growth, inflation and the business cycle.

Aggregate supply

  1. LAS: vertical at potential GDP
  2. SAS: slopes up; money wages fixed
  3. Price level change: move along SAS

AS shifters

  1. Potential GDP up: LAS and SAS right
  2. Money wage up: SAS left only
  3. Oil price up: SAS left only

Aggregate demand

  1. Slopes down: wealth, intertemporal, international
  2. Price level change: move along AD

AD shifters

  1. G up, T down: right
  2. Interest rate down, money up: right
  3. Foreign income up: right
  4. Appreciation: left

Equilibrium

  1. Short run: AD = SAS
  2. Long run: AD = SAS = LAS
  3. Above potential: inflationary gap
  4. Below potential: recessionary gap

Adjustment

  1. AD up: P up, Y up, then wages up
  2. SAS left: stagflation
  3. Long run: Y = potential

Chapter 11 · Expenditure Multipliers

With the price level fixed, real GDP settles where aggregate planned expenditure equals real GDP, and any change in autonomous spending changes real GDP by a multiple of itself, because extra income induces extra consumption: the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) in the simplest model.

Basics

  1. Prices fixed; firms adjust output
  2. AE = C + I + G + X − M
  3. YD = C + S

Propensities

  1. MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYD
  2. MPS = ΔS ÷ ΔYD
  3. MPC + MPS = 1

Spending types

  1. Autonomous: I, G, X, autonomous C
  2. Induced: induced C, imports

Equilibrium

  1. AE = Y (45° line)
  2. AE > Y: inventories fall, output up
  3. AE < Y: inventories rise, output down

Multiplier

  1. ΔY ÷ ΔA
  2. 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS
  3. MPC 0.8 → 5

Smaller multiplier

  1. Income taxes, imports
  2. Rising price level
  3. Long run: zero

Chapter 12 · The Business Cycle, Inflation, and Deflation

Real GDP fluctuates around a growing potential GDP because aggregate demand and supply shift at different speeds; inflation starts from demand (demand-pull) or costs (cost-push) but only continues if the quantity of money keeps growing, deflation comes from demand growing too slowly, and the Phillips curve shows a short-run tradeoff between inflation and unemployment that disappears in the long run.

Cycle

  1. AD faster than potential: inflationary gap
  2. AD slower: recessionary gap
  3. Identify the cause first

Theories

  1. Mainstream: AD swings, sticky wages
  2. RBC: productivity shocks

Demand-pull

  1. AD right: Y up, P up
  2. Wages up: SAS left
  3. Continues with money growth

Cost-push

  1. SAS left: Y down, P up
  2. Stagflation
  3. Spiral if central bank accommodates

Deflation

  1. Deflation: P falls
  2. Disinflation: inflation slows
  3. Cause: slow money growth
  4. Harm: higher real debt

Phillips curve

  1. SRPC: slopes down
  2. LRPC: vertical at natural rate
  3. Expected inflation up: SRPC up

Chapter 13 · Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy uses government spending, taxes and transfer payments to influence the economy: the budget balance is receipts minus outlays, deficits add to debt, automatic stabilizers cushion the cycle without new decisions, and discretionary stimulus shifts aggregate demand by a multiple of the change in spending or taxes.

Budget

  1. Balance = receipts − outlays
  2. Tax revenue = rate × income
  3. Outlays: transfers, G, interest

Deficit and debt

  1. Deficit: this year (flow)
  2. Debt: all past deficits (stock)
  3. Any deficit raises debt

Automatic vs discretionary

  1. Automatic: no new law
  2. Taxes fall, benefits rise in recession
  3. Discretionary: new decision

Balances

  1. Structural: at potential GDP
  2. Cyclical: from the business cycle

Stimulus

  1. Recessionary gap: G up or T down
  2. Inflationary gap: G down or T up
  3. G multiplier > tax multiplier

Limits

  1. Recognition lag
  2. Law-making lag
  3. Impact lag
  4. Tax wedge, Laffer curve

Chapter 14 · Monetary Policy

Monetary policy is the central bank's use of the policy interest rate (and open market operations) to pursue stable prices and maximum sustainable employment: it raises the rate when inflation is too high and lowers it in a recession, and the change works through loan rates, spending and aggregate demand; in a financial crisis it provides emergency liquidity, and macroprudential rules try to prevent the next one.

Objectives

  1. Stable prices (≈ 2% inflation)
  2. Maximum sustainable employment
  3. FOMC targets the federal funds rate

Direction

  1. High inflation: raise rate, AD left
  2. Recession: lower rate, AD right

Tools

  1. Buy securities: reserves up, rates down
  2. Sell securities: reserves down, rates up
  3. Interest on reserves, discount rate

Transmission

  1. Rate → loan rates
  2. → investment and consumption
  3. → AD → Y, jobs, inflation
  4. Long and variable lags

Crisis

  1. Cure: emergency liquidity
  2. Prevention: macroprudential rules
  3. Example: capital requirements
