Chapter-by-chapter course guide

Chapter 1 · What is economics?

Economics studies choices when wants exceed available resources. Scarcity applies to everyone; it is not the same as poverty. Every choice has an opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative forgone.

  • Microeconomics studies individual choices, firms, and markets; macroeconomics studies economy-wide outcomes.
  • Societies answer what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. Efficiency asks whether resources are used well; equity asks how benefits and costs are distributed.
  • Rational choice compares marginal benefit with marginal cost. Choose more while MB exceeds MC; the best stopping point is where MB = MC.
  • Positive statements describe testable relationships. Normative statements express what ought to happen. A model holds other influences constant (ceteris paribus).
  • On a graph, a change in the variable on an axis is a movement along the curve; a changed outside influence shifts the curve. Correlation alone does not establish causation.

Check yourself: What is the opportunity cost of studying for one extra hour? Why is “the government should raise the minimum wage” normative?

Chapter 4 · Measuring production: GDP

GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country during a period. The same production can be counted as output, expenditure, or income.

  • Expenditure identity: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Imports are subtracted because they are already included in C, I, or G but were produced abroad.
  • Count current production, not used goods, intermediate inputs counted again, financial asset trades, transfer payments, or unpaid household work.
  • Nominal GDP values current quantities at current prices; real GDP values them at base-year prices. Per-person real GDP helps compare average material output, but misses distribution, unpaid work, leisure, and environmental costs.
  • Gross includes depreciation; net subtracts it. Net investment = gross investment − depreciation. GDP can be measured from the income side by adding factor income and relevant adjustments.
  • Peak → contraction/recession → trough → expansion. Real GDP growth is a useful cycle indicator, not a complete measure of well-being.

Check yourself: Is a newly produced delivery truck final or intermediate? Why does a rise in nominal GDP not prove that output rose?

Chapter 5 · Jobs and inflation measurement

Labor and price statistics summarize conditions, but each depends on definitions and a measurement method.

  • Labor force = employed + unemployed people actively seeking work. A person who stops looking is not counted as unemployed under the standard measure.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100. Labor-force participation rate = labor force ÷ working-age population × 100. Employment-to-population ratio = employed ÷ working-age population × 100.
  • Frictional unemployment comes from job search; structural from a skills/location mismatch; cyclical from weak aggregate demand. Natural unemployment includes frictional and structural unemployment.
  • CPI tracks the cost of a representative basket. Inflation is the percentage change in the price index, not the index level itself. Quality change, new goods, outlet substitution, and changing spending patterns complicate measurement.
  • Unexpected inflation redistributes purchasing power; borrowers with fixed nominal debt tend to gain and lenders tend to lose. Core inflation excludes food and energy to focus on underlying price movements.

Check yourself: If a discouraged worker stops looking, what happens to the measured unemployment rate and labor-force participation?

Chapter 6 · Economic growth

Long-run growth is an increase in productive capacity and real output per person. A recovery that returns output toward potential is different from a lasting outward shift in capacity.

  • Potential GDP is the output possible when labor and capital are used at sustainable full-employment rates. Labor productivity = real GDP ÷ hours worked.
  • Productivity rises with physical capital, human capital, technology, and institutions that support investment and exchange.
  • Population growth can raise total GDP without raising GDP per person. Approximation: growth in real GDP per person ≈ real GDP growth − population growth.
  • Rule of 70 estimates doubling time: 70 ÷ annual percentage growth rate. Small persistent growth-rate differences compound into large living-standard differences.

Check yourself: If real GDP grows 3% and population grows 1%, approximately how fast does real GDP per person grow? Is returning to potential GDP itself long-run growth?

Chapter 7 · Finance, saving, and investment

Financial markets move saving toward investment projects. Interest rates coordinate the supply of saving and demand for funds, while prices reflect risk and expected returns.

  • Physical capital is equipment, buildings, and inventories; financial assets are claims on income or wealth. Investment adds to physical capital; saving is income not consumed.
  • Present value discounts future dollars: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n. Invest when net present value (present benefits − present costs) is positive.
  • Bond prices and yields move inversely. Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − expected inflation (exact Fisher relation differs slightly).
  • In the loanable-funds market, a higher real rate reduces quantity demanded and increases quantity supplied. Deficit-financed government borrowing can raise demand, rates, and crowd out private investment; the course also considers the Ricardian-Barro argument.
  • Insolvency means liabilities exceed assets; illiquidity means insufficient cash to meet near-term payments. They are related risks, not synonyms.

Check yourself: Why does a bond’s yield fall when its market price rises? What is the difference between saving and investment?

Chapter 8 · Money, the price level, and inflation

Money makes exchange easier. Banks and the central bank influence liquidity, interest rates, and aggregate demand; sustained money growth interacts with output growth to shape the price level.

  • Money serves as medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value. Distinguish money from payment methods and financial assets.
  • Commercial banks accept deposits, make loans, and create deposit money. The monetary base includes currency and bank reserves; reserve leakage and desired reserve holdings limit deposit expansion.
  • The Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy. An open-market purchase adds reserves to the banking system; a sale drains reserves. In the money market, the interest rate moves to balance money demand and supply.
  • Money demand changes with the price level, real output, and payment technology. A movement in the interest rate is a movement along money demand; other influences shift it.
  • Quantity equation: M × V = P × Y. In growth rates, inflation ≈ money growth + velocity growth − real output growth, assuming the definitions are consistent.

Check yourself: When the central bank buys securities, trace the first change in bank reserves and the likely direction of interest rates.

Chapter 9 · Exchange rates and balance of payments

An exchange rate is a relative price between currencies. Trade and asset flows connect currency demand and supply to the current account, capital flows, and a country’s net lending position.

  • Always state the currency quotation: an appreciation means one currency buys more of the other. A change in the quote’s numerator/denominator changes the direction described.
  • Currency demand and supply respond to trade prices, income, interest-rate differentials, and expectations. One event can shift both curves.
  • Purchasing-power parity relates exchange rates to relative price levels; interest-rate parity relates currency returns on comparable assets. They are distinct ideas.
  • Current account includes net exports plus net income and transfers. Capital/financial flows record cross-border asset transactions. The balance-of-payments accounts reconcile overall flows.
  • Sector-balance identity in the course materials: X − M = (S − I) + (T − G). A current-account deficit corresponds to net borrowing from abroad in this accounting framework.

Check yourself: Before deciding whether a currency appreciated, write the quotation in words. What does a current-account deficit imply about national saving relative to investment, all else equal?

Chapter 10 · Aggregate supply and aggregate demand

The AS–AD model explains short-run movements in real GDP and the price level, then shows how wages and productive capacity move output back toward potential in the long run.

  • AD slopes down through wealth, interest-rate, and international-substitution effects. A price-level change moves along AD; fiscal, monetary, foreign-income, and exchange-rate changes can shift it.
  • Short-run aggregate supply (SAS) slopes upward when some input prices are sticky. Long-run aggregate supply (LAS) is vertical at potential GDP.
  • Higher input costs shift SAS left; greater productive capacity shifts SAS and LAS right. A higher wage shifts SAS but does not itself change potential GDP.
  • Short-run equilibrium is AD = SAS. Long-run equilibrium also lies on LAS. Output above/below potential is an inflationary/recessionary gap.
  • A positive AD shock raises output and prices in the short run; subsequent wage adjustment shifts SAS left until output returns to potential. An adverse supply shock can cause stagflation.

Check yourself: For each event, name the curve that shifts and direction: a rise in oil prices; a productivity improvement; a tax cut.

Chapter 11 · Expenditure multipliers

With prices fixed, changes in autonomous spending trigger repeated rounds of income and consumption. The multiplier measures the total equilibrium output response.

  • Planned aggregate expenditure = C + I + G + X − M. Equilibrium occurs when planned expenditure equals output; unplanned inventory changes push firms to adjust production.
  • MPC = change in consumption ÷ change in disposable income; MPS = change in saving ÷ change in disposable income; MPC + MPS = 1 in the simple model.
  • Simple spending multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS. Thus ΔY = multiplier × change in autonomous expenditure.
  • Taxes, imports, saving, and price increases are leakages or dampeners, so real-world multipliers are smaller than the simplest model predicts. In the long run, capacity constrains output.

Check yourself: If MPC = 0.75, find the simple multiplier. If autonomous investment rises by 20, what is the model’s total output change?

Chapter 12 · Business cycle, inflation, and deflation

This chapter uses AS–AD to distinguish fluctuations caused by demand from those caused by supply, and links inflation expectations to wage- and price-setting.

  • Demand-pull inflation begins with AD increasing faster than productive capacity. Cost-push inflation begins with SAS shifting left and can combine rising prices with falling output.
  • Expected inflation affects wage and price decisions. If monetary policy accommodates repeated cost increases, inflation can persist; a one-time price-level increase is not by itself ongoing inflation.
  • Deflation means the price level falls; disinflation means inflation remains positive but slows.
  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down in the basic model; the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural unemployment rate. Higher expected inflation shifts the short-run curve.
  • Mainstream explanations emphasize aggregate-demand fluctuations and sticky prices; real-business-cycle explanations emphasize productivity and other real shocks.

Check yourself: Which shock causes stagflation? How is disinflation different from deflation?

Chapter 13 · Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy changes government spending and taxes. It can stabilize short-run output, but it also affects public debt, incentives, interest rates, and long-run capacity.

  • Budget balance = government receipts − outlays. A deficit is a flow over a period; public debt is the accumulated stock of borrowing.
  • Automatic stabilizers operate without a new vote: tax receipts tend to fall and transfers tend to rise in a downturn. Discretionary policy requires a deliberate change.
  • To close a recessionary gap, expansionary policy raises G or lowers taxes; to reduce an inflationary gap, contractionary policy lowers G or raises taxes.
  • In the simple model, the government-purchases multiplier is larger than the tax multiplier because spending enters aggregate expenditure directly while part of a tax cut is saved.
  • Policy faces recognition, legislative, and implementation lags. Supply-side effects, crowding out, debt costs, and expectations complicate the size and timing of results.

Check yourself: The deficit shrinks this year. Does that guarantee the debt fell? Why can discretionary policy arrive too late?

Chapter 14 · Monetary policy

The central bank influences short-term interest rates and financial conditions to support stable prices and maximum sustainable employment. Its actions reach the economy through aggregate demand and take time.

  • To cool excessive inflation, raise the policy rate: borrowing costs rise, interest-sensitive spending falls, and AD shifts left. To support activity, lower rates: spending and AD tend to rise.
  • Open-market purchases add reserves and tend to lower short-term rates; sales drain reserves and tend to raise rates. The course also covers administered rates and liquidity facilities.
  • Transmission chain: policy rate → market borrowing rates and asset prices → consumption/investment/exchange rate → AD → output, jobs, and inflation.
  • Monetary policy has long and variable lags. Maximum sustainable employment is not zero unemployment.
  • Crisis response can provide emergency liquidity; prevention uses prudential safeguards such as capital requirements. Those are different roles.

Check yourself: Explain why a policy-rate cut shifts AD rather than AS. Is emergency lending a crisis cure or a prevention rule?

The course as one connected story

Output, jobs, and capacity

GDP measures production (Ch. 4). Employment and CPI statistics measure labor slack and prices (Ch. 5). Growth in productivity and resources raises potential output (Ch. 6). The business cycle is the gap between actual output and capacity (Ch. 10–12).

Saving, money, and rates

Saving supplies funds and investment demands them (Ch. 7). Banks create deposit money and the central bank changes reserves and policy rates (Ch. 8, 14). Interest rates connect financial choices to AD and output.

Open economy

Exchange rates affect imports and exports (Ch. 9), which are part of GDP and AD. Cross-border borrowing helps reconcile the current account with saving and investment.

Stabilization and limits

Multiplier logic explains short-run spending effects (Ch. 11). Fiscal policy changes taxes and spending (Ch. 13); monetary policy changes rates and liquidity (Ch. 14). Long-run capacity, inflation expectations, lags, and debt constrain outcomes.

Graph routine: 1) name the model and axes; 2) identify the shock; 3) decide movement or shift; 4) shift the correct curve; 5) state the new short-run price level and output; 6) discuss the long-run adjustment if asked.

Pop Exam A · Foundations and measurement

Suggested time: 12 minutes · 8 questions · 1 point each

1. A student chooses a shift at work instead of attending a concert. Opportunity cost is:
  1. all possible activities missed
  2. the value of the best forgone alternative
  3. the money spent on the concert ticket only
  4. zero if the student is paid
2. Which is a normative statement?
  1. Higher prices reduce quantity demanded, other things equal.
  2. The unemployment rate was 5%.
  3. Government ought to reduce inequality.
  4. Real GDP grew last year.
3. A bakery sells a newly produced loaf to a household. It is counted in GDP as:
  1. an intermediate input
  2. a final good
  3. a transfer
  4. a financial asset
4. Nominal GDP rises 8% while real GDP rises 3%. The best conclusion is:
  1. prices and output both rose, with nominal growth larger
  2. output fell
  3. inflation was exactly 8%
  4. population fell
5. Labor force = 800; unemployed = 40. Unemployment rate is:
  1. 4%
  2. 5%
  3. 8%
  4. 20%
6. A worker loses a job in a recession and actively searches. This is most directly:
  1. frictional unemployment
  2. structural unemployment
  3. cyclical unemployment
  4. not unemployment
7. CPI rises from 120 to 126. Inflation is:
  1. 5%
  2. 6%
  3. 20%
  4. 126%
8. Real GDP grows 4%, population grows 1%. Approximate per-person growth:
  1. 1%
  2. 3%
  3. 4%
  4. 5%
Answer key with reasoning

1 B. Opportunity cost is the single best alternative forgone. 2 C. “Ought” makes a value judgment. 3 B. It is current production sold to the end user. 4 A. Real output increased; the gap between nominal and real growth reflects price changes in the simplified interpretation. 5 B. 40 ÷ 800 × 100 = 5%. 6 C. Recession-related job loss is cyclical. 7 A. (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 5%. 8 B. 4% − 1% ≈ 3%.
