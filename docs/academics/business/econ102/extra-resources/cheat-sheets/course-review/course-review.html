THE COURSE AS ONE STORY

How every chapter connects

Read this first if you're revising the whole course: it's the thread that ties the chapters together.

1

Scarcity and choice (Ch 1)

Everything starts with scarcity: we choose, trade off and respond to incentives. Macroeconomics asks those questions for the whole economy.

2

Measuring the economy (Ch 4–5)

GDP measures output (production = expenditure = income); real GDP strips out prices. The unemployment rate and the CPI measure the other two big indicators.

3

The long run (Ch 6–7)

Potential GDP grows with labor and, above all, productivity. Productivity needs capital, and capital is financed by saving through the loanable funds market.

4

Money and the world (Ch 8–9)

Banks create money; the Fed controls the monetary base. In the long run, money growth sets inflation. Interest rates and expectations set exchange rates, and net exports link us to the world.

5

The short run (Ch 10–12)

AS–AD explains fluctuations: AD and SAS shift, real GDP moves away from potential, and money wages slowly bring it back. The multiplier explains why AD moves so much; inflation is demand-pull or cost-push; the Phillips curve shows the short-run tradeoff.

6

Policy (Ch 13–14)

Fiscal policy (G, T) and monetary policy (the interest rate) shift AD to close gaps. Both have lags; in the long run they can't keep real GDP above potential, only change the price level.

What economics isMust knowSlides 4-6

Every economic question starts from scarcity: our wants are bigger than the resources available to satisfy them. A child wanting a toy and a millionaire wanting more free time both face scarcity. Because we can't have everything, we must choose, and the choices we make depend on the incentives we face.

Economics is the social science that studies the choices that individuals, businesses, governments and entire societies make as they cope with scarcity, the incentives that influence those choices, and the arrangements that coordinate them.

It has two branches:

Branch Studies Example questions
Microeconomics Choices of individuals and businesses, how they interact in markets, and the influence of governments on them Why are people buying more e-books? How many workers should a café hire?
Macroeconomics The performance of the national and global economy Why is unemployment rising? Why did the price level go up this year?
Think of it like this: Zoom level

Microeconomics is the camera zoomed in on one shop, one family or one market. Macroeconomics zooms all the way out to the whole country: total output, the unemployment rate, the inflation rate. ECON102 is the zoomed-out course.

Trap: Scarcity isn't poverty

Scarcity doesn't mean "being poor". It means wants exceed resources, and everyone faces it. A billionaire still has only 24 hours a day and must choose how to spend them.

Group work: which situation best describes scarcity?
Show answer

Available resources are not enough to satisfy all human wants. Watch for distractors such as "people have fewer wants than resources" (the opposite) or "everyone has the same resources" (that's equality, not scarcity).

Pop quiz · Chapter 1

Show answer

Remember
  • Scarcity: wants exceed the resources available
  • Economics studies choices, incentives and the arrangements that coordinate choices
  • Microeconomics: individuals, firms, markets
  • Macroeconomics: the national and global economy
  • An incentive is a reward or a penalty that changes behaviour

What, how and for whom?Must knowSlides 7-13

The first big question: how do choices end up determining what, how and for whom goods and services are produced?

  • What? What we produce changes over time. Rich countries produce mostly services; poorer ones produce more agriculture and manufacturing.
  • How? Goods and services are produced using four factors of production.
  • For whom? Who gets the goods depends on incomes, and each factor earns a different kind of income.
Factor of production What it is Income it earns
Land The "gifts of nature": land, minerals, oil, water, air Rent
Labor The work time and effort people devote to producing goods and services. Its quality is human capital: knowledge and skill from education, training and experience Wages
Capital Tools, machines, buildings and other constructions used to produce goods and services Interest
Entrepreneurship The human resource that organizes land, labor and capital Profit
Memory trick: L-L-C-E earns R-W-I-P

Land Rent, Labor Wages, Capital Interest, Entrepreneurship Profit. Say it as "Land Rents, Labor Works, Capital Is interested, Entrepreneurs Profit."

Trap: Capital is not money

In economics, capital means physical tools and buildings, not money or shares. Money and shares are financial capital. Capital earns interest, never profit.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q2: which pair doesn't match? Labor–wages, Land–rent, Entrepreneurship–profit, Capital–profit
Show answer

Capital and profit don't match. Capital earns interest; profit is the income of entrepreneurship.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q6: how does a successful online series change what, how and for whom?
Show answer

What: more online series (and streaming services) get produced. How: production uses cameras, editing software, streaming servers and skilled digital workers. For whom: the actors, writers, producers and streaming firms earn higher incomes, so they get a bigger share of goods and services.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Group work: firms produce more computers and fewer televisions. Which question are they answering?
Show answer

"What" is produced. Changing the mix of output is always the what question.

Remember
  • Three questions: what, how, for whom
  • Land earns rent, labor earns wages, capital earns interest, entrepreneurship earns profit
  • Human capital is the quality of labor: education, training, experience
  • Capital means physical tools and buildings, not money

Self-interest and the social interestGood to knowSlides 14-23

The second big question: when do choices made in the pursuit of self-interest also promote the social interest?

  • Self-interest: a choice that is best for you.
  • Social interest: a choice that is best for society as a whole. Social interest has two parts: efficiency and equity (fairness).
  • Resource use is efficient if it isn't possible to make someone better off without making someone else worse off.

The slides use four topics to show self-interest and the social interest sometimes clash:

Topic Self-interest Social-interest question
Globalization Firms cut costs by producing where wages are low Is it fair to workers who lose their jobs at home?
Information-age monopolies Tech firms like Google and Apple earn big profits Are their prices and power in society's interest?
Climate change We burn fossil fuels for cheap energy and transport Carbon emissions harm everyone; who should cut them?
The Covid pandemic People want to work, travel and meet Social contact spreads the virus; lockdowns protect others but cost jobs
Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q3: explain self-interest and social interest in each headline
Show answer

a) A coffee chain opens 120 new stores in the Gulf. Self-interest: the chain expects more profit, and customers get more convenient coffee. Social interest: the new jobs and choice are good for society, but the stores may push small local cafés out.

b) Packaged foods must display clear nutrition labels. Self-interest: firms would rather not pay to print labels, and might prefer to hide high sugar or fat. Social interest: labels help people choose healthier food and cut health-care costs, so the rule protects society.

Remember
  • Self-interest: best for you. Social interest: best for society
  • Social interest is judged by efficiency and equity
  • Efficient: you can't make anyone better off without making someone worse off
  • Globalization, tech monopolies, climate change and Covid show the two can clash

The economic way of thinkingMust knowSlides 24-31

Six ideas define how economists think about choices:

  1. A choice is a tradeoff. Getting more of one thing means getting less of something else.
  2. People make rational choices by comparing benefits and costs.
  3. Benefit is what you gain, measured by what you are willing to give up to get it.
  4. Cost is what you must give up: the opportunity cost, the highest-valued alternative forgone.
  5. Most choices are "how much" choices made at the margin.
  6. Choices respond to incentives.

The rational choice rule

If marginal benefit > marginal cost → do more of itIf marginal benefit < marginal cost → do less of it

Marginal benefit is the benefit from pursuing an incremental (one more unit) increase in an activity. Marginal cost is the opportunity cost of that increase. "At the margin" means you don't decide "study or not study"; you decide "one more hour of studying or one more hour of something else".

Slide 28: the opportunity cost of attending class

Going to class costs you the one best thing you'd otherwise do with that hour, for example an hour of sleep. It's not the sum of sleep, going to the gym and watching a series: you could only have done one of them.

Trap: Opportunity cost is ONE alternative, not all of them

The group-work True/False: "If a student gives up several activities to attend class, all of them together are the opportunity cost." That's false. The opportunity cost is only the highest-valued single alternative.

Memory trick: "The NEXT best thing"

Opportunity cost = the next best alternative. Not every alternative, not the worst, just the single next best one you gave up.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q4: movies instead of studying; grade 54% instead of the usual 76%
Show answer

a) Yes, you faced a tradeoff: you had one evening, and spending it at the movies meant not spending it studying.

b) The opportunity cost of the evening at the movies was the 22 percentage points of grade you gave up (76% − 54%), plus whatever else that study time would have given you.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q1: a streaming company makes its basic app free in unlimited quantities. Do incentives change?
Show answer

Yes. A zero price removes the money cost of using the app, so people have an incentive to use it more and to drop paid rivals. Rival firms also face a new incentive: cut their prices or offer something better. Using it still costs time, though, so it isn't completely free.

Remember
  • Six ideas: tradeoff, rational choice, benefit, opportunity cost, the margin, incentives
  • Opportunity cost = the highest-valued alternative given up
  • Do more if marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost
  • A change in marginal cost or marginal benefit changes incentives and choices

Economics as a social science and a policy toolMust knowSlides 32-35

As social scientists, economists try to discover how the economic world works. They distinguish two kinds of statement:

Positive statement Normative statement
About what is About what ought to be
Can be tested by checking it against the facts Can't be tested; it depends on values
May be right or wrong Neither right nor wrong, it's an opinion
"Our planet is warming because of carbon emissions." "We should cut our use of carbon-based fuels."

Unscrambling cause and effect. Economists build an economic model: a description of some part of the economy that includes only the features needed for the purpose at hand. They test a model by comparing its predictions with the facts. This is hard because many things change at once, so economists use:

  • Ceteris paribus ("other things remaining the same") reasoning, to isolate one cause.
  • Natural experiments, statistical investigations and economic experiments.

As policy advisers, economists use positive economics to find the best way to reach a normative goal. Positive economics describes the consequences of alternative policies; normative economics chooses between them.

Memory trick: "Should" signals normative

Look for should, ought, must, better, fair, too high: the statement is normative. Cause-and-effect claims ("if X rises, Y falls") are positive even when they're wrong.

Trap: Positive doesn't mean "true"

The group-work True/False says "a statement must be factually correct before economists call it positive." False. A positive statement only has to be testable. "The moon is made of cheese" is a (false) positive statement.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Assignment 1, Q5: positive or normative?
Show answer

a) "The country should reduce its imports of food." Normative, because of "should": it's a value judgement. b) "The country imports wheat and rice." Positive: you can check it against trade data. c) "If the price of smartphones rises, the quantity sold will fall." Positive: a testable cause-and-effect claim.

Remember
  • Positive: what is, testable. Normative: what ought to be, a value judgement
  • An economic model keeps only the features needed for the purpose
  • Ceteris paribus isolates one cause by holding everything else fixed
  • Policy advice combines positive analysis with normative goals

Appendix: graphing dataMust knowSlides 44-53

A graph represents a quantity as a distance on a line. A two-variable graph has a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis that meet at the origin (zero). The value of the variable on the vertical axis is measured along the y-axis.

A scatter diagram plots the value of one variable against the value of another for a number of observations. It shows whether two variables appear to be related, but it can't by itself prove that one causes the other.

Trap: Correlation isn't causation

A scatter diagram showing that two variables move together doesn't prove that one causes the other. A third variable may drive both, or the link may be a coincidence.

Pop quiz · Chapter 1
Group work: what is the main purpose of a scatter diagram?
Show answer

To show whether two variables appear to be related. It doesn't determine the cause, add the variables up, or show only changes over time (that's a time-series graph, in the Chapter 4 appendix).

Remember
  • x-axis horizontal, y-axis vertical, origin at zero
  • Scatter diagram: one variable plotted against another to see whether they're related
  • A pattern on a scatter diagram is not proof of cause and effect

Appendix: slope and more than two variablesMust knowSlides 63-74

The slope of a relationship is the change in the value of the variable on the y-axis divided by the change in the value of the variable on the x-axis.

Slope

slope = Δy ÷ Δx = rise ÷ run
  • A straight line has a constant slope. Upward sloping means a positive slope; downward sloping means a negative slope.
  • A curved line's slope varies. Measure it at a point by drawing the tangent at that point, or across an arc using the straight line between two points (an average slope).
Group work, Q4: Point A (2 hours, SAR 100) and Point B (6 hours, SAR 300)
Rise: Δy = 300 − 100 = SAR 200.
Run: Δx = 6 − 2 = 4 hours.
Slope = 200 ÷ 4 = 50.
Meaning: each extra hour worked adds SAR 50 to total earnings (the hourly wage).

More than two variables. Ice-cream consumption depends on both the price and the temperature. To draw it on a two-dimensional graph we hold one variable constant, ceteris paribus: plot price against quantity for a given temperature. When the temperature changes, the whole curve shifts.

Memory trick: Rise over Run, and the Run goes Right

Slope = rise ÷ run: go along the x-axis (run) first, then up the y-axis (rise). If the line rises as you run right, the slope is positive.

Trap: Slope and steepness

A steeper line has a larger slope in absolute value, but don't read slope off a graph with unequal scales. Always calculate Δy ÷ Δx from the numbers.

Remember
  • Slope = Δy ÷ Δx
  • Straight line: constant slope; curve: slope varies
  • Slope of a curve at a point = slope of its tangent; across an arc = average slope
  • Graph a third variable by holding it constant; changing it shifts the curve

The big picture

GDP is the market value of all the final goods and services produced within a country in a given time period; it can be measured by adding up expenditure (C + I + G + X − M) or incomes, and real GDP per person is the standard yardstick for living standards.

Learn the three-question filter for "is it in GDP?".

Part of the definition What it means Quick check
Market value Unlike things (apples, haircuts, cars) are added up using their market prices What did it sell for?

An intermediate good is bought by one firm from another and used up as a component of a final good. The flour a bakery buys, or the tyres a car maker fits, are already included in the price of the bread and the car. Counting them again is double counting.

Memory trick: The 3-question filter: Now, Here, Final

Is it produced now (this period)? Produced here (inside the country)? A final good? Three yeses means it's in GDP. Any no means it's out.

The circular flow: production = expenditure = incomeMust knowSlides 8-21

The circular flow shows the transactions among the four economic sectors in two kinds of market:

  • Factor markets: households sell the services of labor, land, capital and entrepreneurship; firms buy them. The money flow back to households is aggregate income, Y (wages, rent, interest, profit).
  • Goods markets: firms sell goods and services; households, firms, governments and the rest of the world buy them.
Flow From → to What it is
Y Firms → households (factor markets) Aggregate income: wages, rent, interest, profit
C Households → firms (goods markets) Consumption expenditure
I Firms → firms Investment: new capital and additions to inventories
G Governments → firms Government expenditure on goods and services
X − M Rest of the world → firms Net exports

Firms pay out everything they receive as incomes (profit is whatever is left over), so the red expenditure flows add up to the blue income flow:

The circular-flow identity

Y = C + I + G + (X − M)aggregate production = aggregate expenditure = aggregate income
Think of it like this: Every riyal spent is a riyal earned

When you pay SAR 20 for a coffee, the café's SAR 20 of revenue is paid out as wages, rent, interest and the owner's profit. Spending and income are two sides of the same transactions, so totalled across the economy they must be equal.

Memory trick: Households: Sell factors, Buy goods

Households Sell in Factor markets and Buy in Goods markets. Firms do the reverse. If you remember one side, the other is its mirror.

A household rents land to a business: factor market, the rent goes to the household. The government buys cybersecurity services: goods market, G.

Show answer

Because every riyal spent on final goods and services is received by firms and paid out as wages, rent, interest or profit. Total expenditure on final output therefore equals total income, and both equal the value of production.

Remember

  • Goods markets: firms sell to households (C), firms (I), governments (G) and foreigners (X − M)
  • Y = C + I + G + (X − M)
  • Aggregate production = aggregate expenditure = aggregate income

Why "domestic" and why "gross"?Must knowSlides 22-24

Domestic means production within the country. Gross national income (GNI) instead measures the income earned by the country's residents wherever they produce, so it differs from GDP by net income received from abroad.

Gross means before deducting depreciation.

  • Depreciation is the decrease in the value of a firm's capital that results from wear and tear and obsolescence.
  • Gross investment is the total amount spent on new capital. Net investment is the change in the value of capital.

Net investment and net domestic product

Net investment = gross investment − depreciationNet domestic product = GDP − depreciation
Gross versus net investment

A firm starts the year with SAR 30 million of capital, spends SAR 10 million on new machines, and SAR 4 million of its capital wears out. Gross investment = 10; net investment = 10 − 4 = SAR 6 million, and capital at the end of the year is 30 + 6 = SAR 36 million.

Trap: Depreciation isn't added again

GDP already uses gross investment. When a GDP data table lists depreciation separately, don't add it in the expenditure approach. (It's only subtracted if you want net domestic product.)

Remember
  • Domestic: produced within the country; GNI counts residents' income wherever earned
  • Gross: before subtracting depreciation
  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation

Measuring GDP: the expenditure approachMust knowSlides 25-28

The expenditure approach measures GDP as the sum of consumption expenditure, investment, government expenditure on goods and services, and net exports.

Expenditure approach

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
Component Includes Doesn't include
C Household purchases of goods and services, including new consumer durables New houses (those are I)
I New machines, buildings, new houses, additions to inventories Shares and bonds (financial assets)
G Government purchases: ambulances, roads, teachers' and soldiers' services Transfer payments (pensions, cash benefits)
X − M Exports minus imports

In the United States, consumption is by far the largest component, roughly two thirds of GDP.

Trap: Imports are subtracted, not ignored

An imported car bought by a household is first counted in C, then subtracted in M, so it adds nothing to GDP. That's why you mark both C and M for an import.

Trap: Transfer payments aren't G

A pension or cash benefit buys no good or service. It's excluded from G and from GDP.

Measuring GDP: the income approachGood to knowSlides 29-33

The income approach adds up the incomes that firms pay households for the factors of production they hire.

  • Compensation of employees: wages plus benefits (the payment for labor).
  • Net interest, rental income, corporate profits and proprietors' income (the payments for capital, land and entrepreneurship).

Their sum is net domestic income at factor cost. Two adjustments turn it into GDP:

From incomes to GDP

GDP = net domestic income at factor cost + indirect taxes − subsidies + depreciation
  1. Indirect taxes less subsidies: sales taxes make market prices higher than factor cost, so add them; subsidies make prices lower, so subtract them.
  2. Depreciation: incomes are net of depreciation, so add it back to get gross product.

Any remaining gap between the two approaches is the statistical discrepancy.

Memory trick: Two adjustments: "Taxes and Wear"

Factor incomes miss taxes (indirect taxes − subsidies) and wear (depreciation). Add both back.

Remember
  • Income approach: wages + interest + rent + profits
  • Add indirect taxes less subsidies, and add depreciation, to reach GDP
  • The two approaches differ only by the statistical discrepancy

Nominal GDP and real GDPMust knowSlides 34-37

Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rise, even if no more is produced. Real GDP strips out price changes.

Measure Quantities Prices Changes when
Nominal GDP Current year Current-year prices Production or prices change
Real GDP Current year Base-year prices Only production changes

In the base year, real GDP equals nominal GDP.

Assignment 1, Q9: Desert Republic, base year 2024 (coffee 600 → 700 units at $5 → $6; dates 900 → 1,000 boxes at $2 → $3)
Nominal GDP 2024 = (600 × $5) + (900 × $2) = 3,000 + 1,800 = $4,800.
Nominal GDP 2025 = (700 × $6) + (1,000 × $3) = 4,200 + 3,000 = $7,200.
Real GDP 2025 at 2024 prices = (700 × $5) + (1,000 × $2) = 3,500 + 2,000 = $5,500.
Nominal GDP rose 50%, but real GDP rose only 14.6%; the rest was higher prices.
Memory trick: Real = "Rewind the prices"

For real GDP, keep this year's quantities and rewind the prices to the base year. Nominal uses "now" prices.

Trap: Nominal up doesn't mean real up

The group-work MCQ: nominal GDP rose; what can you conclude? Only that real GDP may have increased, decreased or stayed the same. Higher prices alone could explain the rise.

Remember
  • Nominal GDP: current quantities × current prices
  • Real GDP: current quantities × base-year prices
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP in the base year
  • A rise in nominal GDP can come from higher prices alone

Uses of real GDP: living standards, growth and the business cycleMust knowSlides 38-47

Real GDP per person (real GDP ÷ population) measures the standard of living, the value of goods and services the average person can enjoy.

Real GDP per person

real GDP per person = real GDP ÷ population
Assignment 1, Q10: real GDP per person, 2022–2025
2022: $900 bn ÷ 30 m = $30,000.
2023: $945 bn ÷ 31 m = $30,484: up, so the standard of living increased.
2024: $984 bn ÷ 32 m = $30,750: up again, increased.
2025: $990 bn ÷ 33 m = $30,000: down, the standard of living decreased, because population grew faster (3.1%) than real GDP (0.6%).

Long-term trend. Potential GDP is the value of real GDP when all the economy's labor, capital, land and entrepreneurial ability are fully employed. U.S. potential GDP per person grew fast in the 1960s, then slowed: the productivity growth slowdown. The output lost because of the slowdown is the Lucas wedge, the gap between the actual path and the path real GDP per person would have followed if the 1960s growth rate had continued.

The business cycle is a periodic but irregular up-and-down movement of total production and other measures of economic activity. Its four parts:

Phase What happens
Expansion Real GDP rises, from a trough to a peak
Peak The highest point, where an expansion ends
Recession Real GDP decreases for at least two successive quarters (peak to trough)
Trough The lowest point, where a recession ends
Memory trick: "PRETty" cycle

Peak → Recession → Trough → Expansion → back to Peak. A recession always runs down from a peak; an expansion always runs up from a trough.

Show answer

Real GDP is falling, moving from a peak down to a trough (officially, for at least two successive quarters). An expansion is the rising stretch from a trough up to the next peak.

Remember
  • Real GDP per person = real GDP ÷ population, the standard measure of living standards
  • Potential GDP: real GDP at full employment of all resources
  • Lucas wedge: output lost through the productivity growth slowdown
  • Business cycle: expansion, peak, recession (falling for two quarters or more), trough

Comparing countries and the limits of GDPMust knowSlides 48-52

Comparing countries raises two problems: the GDP of each country must be converted into the same currency, and the same goods must be valued at the same prices. Using market exchange rates can mislead, because prices of many goods are much lower in poorer countries. Purchasing power parity (PPP) prices value every country's output at the same prices. At market exchange rates China's GDP per person looked tiny next to the U.S.; at PPP prices it's much larger.

Limitations. Real GDP leaves out goods and services that matter for well-being:

Left out of GDP Example
Household production Cooking, cleaning, caring for children at home
Underground economic activity Cash jobs hidden to avoid taxes, illegal trade
Leisure time Extra free time makes us better off but adds nothing to GDP
Environmental quality Pollution may rise as output grows
Health and life expectancy, political freedom and social justice Not measured at all
Memory trick: "HULE": what GDP misses

Household production, Underground economy, Leisure, Environment. GDP measures output, not happiness.

Not necessarily. Real GDP per person is an average: it doesn't show how income is distributed, so rising inequality can leave many people no better off. It also ignores environmental quality, so higher pollution reduces well-being in a way GDP doesn't record.

Use: it lets you compare the standard of living over time or across countries, because it measures the average value of goods and services per person. Limitation: it omits household production, the underground economy, leisure and environmental quality, and says nothing about how income is shared.

Chained-dollar real GDPJust skimSlides 53-59

The Bureau of Economic Analysis doesn't use one fixed base year. It values production in the prices of adjacent years, calculates two growth rates (one at each year's prices), takes their average, and then chains these averages back to a base year (currently 2012). This avoids the bias of using prices that get more out of date each year.

Remember
  • Value both years' output at each year's prices
  • Average the two growth rates
  • Link (chain) the averages back to the base year

Appendix: time-series graphsJust skimSlides 60-67

A time-series graph puts time on the x-axis and the variable on the y-axis. It shows the variable's level, its direction of change, its speed of change and its trend. On a ratio (logarithmic) scale, equal distances show equal percentage changes, so a steady growth rate looks like a straight line, and a steeper line means faster growth.

Remember
  • Time-series graph: time on the x-axis
  • Shows level, direction, speed and trend
  • Ratio scale: equal percentage changes look equal; the slope shows the growth rate

Why unemployment matters and who is unemployedMust knowSlides 4-9

Unemployment is a problem for two reasons:

  • Lost incomes and production: an unemployed worker loses income, and the economy loses the output that worker could have produced.
  • Lost human capital: prolonged unemployment destroys skills and harms health and self-esteem.

Every month the Current Population Survey sorts the population:

To be counted as unemployed, a person must be in one of three categories:

  1. Without work, but has made specific efforts to find a job within the previous four weeks
  2. Waiting to be called back to a job from which they were laid off
  3. Waiting to start a new job within 30 days
Trap: Not looking = not in the labor force

Someone without a job who isn't looking (a retiree, a full-time student, a discouraged worker) is not in the labor force. They're not unemployed. Only people actively searching (or waiting to be recalled or to start) count as unemployed.

The three labor market indicatorsMust knowSlides 10-15

The three rates

Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100Labor force participation rate = labor force ÷ working-age population × 100Employment-to-population ratio = employed ÷ working-age population × 100
Memory trick: "U over L, L over W, E over W"

Unemployment rate: U ÷ Labor force. Labor force participation: L ÷ Working-age pop. Employment-to-pop: E ÷ W. Only the unemployment rate divides by the labor force; the other two divide by the working-age population.

Group work, Problem 1: labor force 159 m, employment 148 m, working-age population 261 m
Unemployed = 159 − 148 = 11 million.
Unemployment rate = 11 ÷ 159 × 100 = 6.9%.
Labor force participation rate = 159 ÷ 261 × 100 = 60.9%.
Employment-to-population ratio = 148 ÷ 261 × 100 = 56.7%.
Group work, Problem 2: Sandy Island (July: 10,000 employed, 1,000 unemployed, 5,000 not in the labor force)
July: labor force = 11,000; working-age population = 16,000. Unemployment rate = 1,000 ÷ 11,000 = 9.1%. E/P ratio = 10,000 ÷ 16,000 = 62.5%.
August flows: 80 lost jobs and stopped looking (E −80, out of the labor force); 20 quit and retired (E −20, out); 150 unemployed were hired (U −150, E +150); 50 quit the labor force (U −50); 40 entered to look for work (U +40).

Employed = 10,000 − 80 − 20 + 150 = 10,050.

Broader measures of unemploymentGood to knowSlides 16-21

The official (U-3) rate misses some underused labor:

  • Marginally attached workers: people who currently are neither working nor looking but have looked in the past 12 months. A discouraged worker is a marginally attached worker who has stopped looking because of repeated failure.
  • Part-time workers who want full-time jobs (part time for economic reasons).

The BLS publishes six measures, U-1 to U-6. U-3 is the official rate. U-4 adds discouraged workers, U-5 adds all marginally attached workers, and U-6, the broadest, also adds part-time workers who want full-time work.

Memory trick: Higher U-number, bigger net

U-1 is the narrowest and U-6 the widest net. Each step up catches more under-used workers.

Remember
  • Discouraged workers aren't counted as unemployed in the official rate
  • So the official rate understates under-used labor
  • U-6 adds marginally attached workers and involuntary part-timers

Frictional, structural and cyclical unemploymentMust knowSlides 22-28

Type Cause Example
Frictional Normal labor turnover: people entering the labor market and searching for jobs, firms searching for workers A new graduate looking for her first job
Structural Changes in technology or international competition change the skills or locations jobs need Bank tellers replaced by apps; factory jobs moved abroad
Cyclical The higher-than-normal unemployment of a business cycle recession Workers laid off because sales fell in a slowdown
  • Natural unemployment = frictional + structural. It's the unemployment that arises when there's no cyclical unemployment.
  • Full employment is the situation in which the unemployment rate equals the natural unemployment rate. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • The natural rate changes over time with the age distribution of the population, the scale of structural change, the real wage rate and unemployment benefits.
Memory trick: "FSC": Fresh, Skills, Cycle

Frictional: fresh job search. Structural: skills or place mismatch. Cyclical: the business cycle. Natural = F + S (everything except the cycle).

Trap: Structural lasts longer than frictional

Frictional unemployment is short and healthy (matching people to jobs). Structural unemployment usually lasts longer, because workers must retrain or move.

Unemployment and real GDP over the cycleGood to knowSlides 29-30

Potential GDP is the real GDP produced at full employment. The output gap is real GDP minus potential GDP, as a percentage of potential GDP.

  • When real GDP is below potential (a negative output gap, a recession), unemployment is above the natural rate: cyclical unemployment is positive.
  • When real GDP is above potential, unemployment is below the natural rate.
Memory trick: Gap and joblessness move opposite ways

Output gap down (negative), unemployment up. They mirror each other through the cycle.

Remember
  • Output gap = (real GDP − potential GDP) ÷ potential GDP
  • Negative gap: unemployment above the natural rate
  • Positive gap: unemployment below the natural rate

Why inflation and deflation are problemsMust knowSlides 31-33

The price level is the average level of prices. Inflation is a rising price level; deflation a falling one. Low, steady and anticipated inflation or deflation isn't a big problem. Unpredictable inflation is, because it:

  1. Redistributes income: workers' wages lag behind prices (or run ahead of them).
  2. Redistributes wealth: unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders, because loans are repaid in money worth less.
  3. Lowers real GDP and consumption: hard-to-predict inflation makes planning harder.
  4. Diverts resources from production: people spend time and effort forecasting inflation and protecting themselves from it instead of producing. In hyperinflation (50% a month or more) people stop using money altogether.
Memory trick: Unexpected inflation: Borrowers Benefit, Lenders Lose

Borrowers benefit and lenders lose, because the money paid back buys less than the money lent.

The borrower gains and you (the lender) lose: you're repaid SAR 1,050, but it buys less than SAR 1,000 did a year ago, so your real return is about −3%.

  • It redistributes income and wealth (borrowers gain, lenders lose)

The Consumer Price Index and the inflation rateMust knowSlides 34-44

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the average of the prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed basket of consumer goods and services. It's defined to equal 100 in the reference base period; a CPI of 271.7 means prices are 171.7% higher than in the base period.

Constructing the CPI takes three steps: select the CPI basket (from the Consumer Expenditure Survey), do a monthly price survey, and calculate the CPI.

CPI and the inflation rate

Cost of basket = Σ (base-period quantity × price)CPI = cost of basket at current prices ÷ cost of basket at base-period prices × 100Inflation rate = (CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year × 100
Group work, Problems 8–9: Coral Island buys juice and cloth. 2020: $60 on juice at $2 a bottle, $30 on cloth at $5 a yard. 2021: juice $4, cloth $6
CPI basket: juice = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 bottles; cloth = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 yards.
Share of budget on juice in 2020 = 60 ÷ 90 × 100 = 66.7%.
Cost of the basket in 2021 = (30 × $4) + (6 × $6) = 120 + 36 = $156.
CPI 2021 = 156 ÷ 90 × 100 = 173.3 (2020 = 100).
Inflation rate in 2021 = (173.3 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 73.3%.
Group work, Problem 10: CPI June 2019 = 256.1, June 2020 = 257.8, June 2021 = 271.7
Year to June 2020: (257.8 − 256.1) ÷ 256.1 × 100 = 0.66%.
Year to June 2021: (271.7 − 257.8) ÷ 257.8 × 100 = 5.39%.
The inflation rate increased sharply in 2021.
Trap: The basket quantities stay fixed

The CPI keeps the base-period quantities and changes only the prices. Don't recalculate quantities with the new prices.

Trap: CPI level vs inflation rate

A CPI that is still rising but more slowly means a falling inflation rate, not deflation. Deflation means the CPI itself falls.

Remember
  • CPI = 100 in the base period
  • CPI = cost of the fixed basket now ÷ cost in the base period × 100
  • Inflation rate = percentage change in the CPI
  • The basket's quantities are fixed at base-period amounts

Is the CPI biased? Alternative price indexesGood to knowSlides 45-53

The CPI may overstate the true inflation rate, because of four biases:

Bias Why it overstates inflation
New goods bias New goods (a smartphone replacing a landline) are pricier but better, and enter the basket late
Quality change bias Part of a price rise pays for better quality, not higher prices
Commodity substitution bias When a good's relative price rises, people switch to cheaper substitutes, but the fixed basket doesn't
Outlet substitution bias People shift to cheaper stores and online sellers

The bias was estimated at about 1.1 percentage points a year (the Boskin Commission, 1996). It distorts private contracts and raises government outlays, since pensions and benefits are linked to the CPI.

Alternative measures:

  • Chained CPI: uses current and previous-period quantities, which reduces substitution bias.
  • Personal consumption expenditure (PCE) price index: uses data from the national accounts on all consumption spending.
  • Core inflation: excludes the volatile prices of food and fuel, to show the underlying trend. The Fed watches core PCE inflation.
Memory trick: "New Quality Substitutes Outlets" (NQSO)

New goods, Quality change, commodity Substitution, Outlet substitution: the four reasons the CPI overstates inflation.

Remember
  • The CPI overstates inflation: new goods, quality, commodity and outlet substitution biases
  • Chained CPI reduces substitution bias

  • Core inflation excludes food and fuel

How potential GDP is determinedMust knowSlides 15-21

Potential GDP depends on two things:

  1. The aggregate production function: real GDP rises as labor hours rise, holding capital and technology constant, with diminishing returns (each extra hour adds less).
  2. The aggregate labor market: the demand for labor slopes down (firms hire more hours at a lower real wage, because of diminishing marginal product), and the supply of labor slopes up (people supply more hours at a higher real wage).

Labor market equilibrium determines the real wage rate and the full-employment quantity of labor. Plug that quantity into the production function and you get potential GDP.

Group work, Problems 15–16: find the equilibrium and potential GDP
Read the labor table: at a real wage of $40 an hour, labor supplied = labor demanded = 35 hours. Above $40 there's a surplus of labor; below $40, a shortage.
Read the production function at 35 hours: real GDP = $2,625 (potential GDP).
Labor productivity = real GDP ÷ hours = 2,625 ÷ 35 = $75 per hour.
Memory trick: Two diagrams, one answer

Labor market first (where LD meets LS → hours), then production function (hours → potential GDP). "Find the hours, then read the output."

Remember
  • Production function: more labor → more real GDP, with diminishing returns
  • Labor demand slopes down, labor supply slopes up
  • Equilibrium gives the full-employment quantity of labor
  • Potential GDP = output on the production function at that quantity of labor
  • Labor productivity = real GDP ÷ labor hours

More people add to total output but not to output per person. Only higher productivity raises the real wage and the standard of living.

Why labor productivity growsMust knowSlides 30-33

Preconditions. The fundamental precondition is an incentive system, created by three institutions:

  • Firms, which organize production
  • Property rights, which protect ownership of resources and what they produce
  • Markets, which let people trade

With those in place, labor productivity grows through:

Source How it works
Physical capital growth More and better machines per worker (saving and investment)
Human capital growth Education, training and on-the-job experience; the most fundamental source
Technological advances Discovering and applying new technologies; usually needs new capital to put to work
Memory trick: "Machines, Minds, Methods"

Productivity grows from machines (physical capital), minds (human capital) and methods (technology). Underneath them: firms, property rights and markets.

Growth theories and policiesJust skimSlides 34-49

Theory Core idea Prediction
Classical Growth raises real GDP per person above subsistence, causing a population explosion that drives it back down (Malthus) Growth is temporary; living standards return to subsistence
Neoclassical Growth comes from technological change; diminishing returns to capital mean growth stops if technology stops (Solow) Growth rates should converge across countries
New growth Real GDP per person grows because people choose to innovate in pursuit of profit; knowledge is not subject to diminishing returns (Romer) Growth can persist indefinitely

The evidence: growth is linked to saving and investment, education, openness to trade and stable, rule-of-law institutions.

Five policies for faster growth: create incentive mechanisms (property rights, markets), encourage saving, encourage research and development, improve the quality of education, and encourage international trade.

Memory trick: Classical = Crowded, Neoclassical = Needs tech, New = Never stops

Classical: population growth crowds out gains. Neoclassical: growth needs outside technology. New growth: innovation is a choice, so growth never has to stop.

Remember
  • Classical: population growth returns living standards to subsistence
  • Neoclassical: technology drives growth; diminishing returns to capital
  • New growth: profit-seeking innovation keeps growth going
  • Policies: incentives, saving, R&D, education, trade

The demand for loanable fundsMust knowSlides 20-23

The supply of loanable fundsMust knowSlides 24-27

The quantity supplied depends on the real interest rate, disposable income, expected future income, wealth and default risk.

  • The supply curve slopes upward: a higher real interest rate rewards saving, so people save more.
  • It shifts rightward when disposable income rises, and leftward when wealth or expected future income rises (people feel richer and save less) or default risk rises.
Memory trick: Supply shifters: "D-E-W-D"

Disposable income (up → more saving), Expected future income (up → less), Wealth (up → less), Default risk (up → less). Only disposable income moves supply the same way it moves.

Government in the loanable funds marketMust knowSlides 33-36

  • A budget surplus adds to the supply of loanable funds: the real interest rate falls, private investment rises, and saving falls back somewhat.
  • A budget deficit adds to the demand for loanable funds: the real interest rate rises, and private investment decreases. The fall in private investment is crowding out.
  • The Ricardo-Barro effect: taxpayers expect future taxes to repay the debt, so they save more today. Private saving rises by the amount of the deficit, the real interest rate doesn't change, and there's no crowding out.

Depository institutionsMust knowSlides 13-20

A depository institution takes deposits from households and firms and makes loans. The main ones are commercial banks, thrift institutions and money market funds.

A bank's goal is to maximize its owners' wealth by lending at a higher rate than it pays depositors, but it must balance profit against the risk of loans going bad and of running out of cash. It puts depositors' funds into reserves (cash in the vault and deposits at the Fed), liquid assets (Treasury bills, overnight loans to other banks), securities and loans.

Depository institutions provide four benefits:

  1. Create liquidity: they borrow short (deposits) and lend long (loans).
  2. Pool risk: spreading loans across many borrowers lowers the risk to each depositor.
  3. Lower the cost of borrowing: one loan from a bank instead of many small loans from individuals.
  4. Lower the cost of monitoring borrowers: banks specialize in checking credit risk.

They're regulated through deposit insurance (the FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000) and balance sheet rules: minimum capital requirements, limits on risky lending, and reserve requirements.

Memory trick: "LPCM": Lend Pools Cut Monitoring

Liquidity, Pool risk, Cut borrowing costs, Monitor borrowers. Banks pool and spread risk; they never concentrate it.

Show answer

I and II. Banks pool (spread) risk; they don't concentrate it.

Remember
  • Banks lend at higher rates than they pay, balancing profit and risk
  • Assets: reserves, liquid assets, securities, loans
  • Benefits: create liquidity, pool risk, lower borrowing and monitoring costs
  • Regulation: deposit insurance, capital and reserve requirements

How banks create moneyMust knowSlides 35-41

Banks create deposits (money) when they make loans. The loan is spent, and the seller deposits the money in another bank, which can lend part of it again.

  • Desired reserves = desired reserve ratio × deposits.
  • Unplanned (excess) reserves = actual reserves − desired reserves. Banks lend out unplanned reserves.
  • The currency drain: people hold part of their money as currency, so not all of each loan comes back to banks as deposits.

The supply of money and money market equilibriumMust knowSlides 48-61

The Fed is the most powerful player, so the supply of money depends on what it targets:

Memory trick: Target the quantity → Vertical; target the rate → Horizontal

Fix the quantity and the curve stands up (vertical). Fix the rate and the curve lies flat (horizontal).

Equilibrium. The interest rate adjusts until the quantity of money demanded equals the quantity supplied. If the rate is too high, people hold less money than exists, so they buy bonds; bond prices rise and the interest rate falls.

Short run. An increase in the quantity of money lowers the nominal interest rate; a decrease raises it.

Long run. The loanable funds market fixes the real interest rate, and real GDP equals potential GDP. An increase in the quantity of money then just raises the price level in the same proportion: in the long run, money affects only prices.

The quantity theory of moneyMust knowSlides 62-64

The quantity theory of money says that, in the long run, an increase in the quantity of money brings an equal percentage increase in the price level (other things the same). It starts from the equation of exchange:

Equation of exchange and the quantity theory

M × V = P × YV = P × Y ÷ MInflation rate = money growth rate + velocity growth rate − real GDP growth rate

If velocity is constant and real GDP is at potential, faster money growth means equally faster inflation.

The foreign exchange marketMust knowSlides 4-9

To buy goods, services or assets produced in another country, we need that country's currency. We get it in the foreign exchange market, where one currency is traded for another.

The exchange rate is the price at which one currency exchanges for another, for example 3.75 riyals per U.S. dollar.

  • Appreciation: a currency's value rises in terms of another currency. You get more of the other currency for one unit.
  • Depreciation: a currency's value falls. You get less of the other currency.

Demand and supply in the foreign exchange marketMust knowSlides 10-21

Demand for U.S. dollars comes from foreigners who want to buy U.S. exports and U.S. assets. It depends on the exchange rate, world demand for U.S. exports, interest rates in the U.S. and abroad, and the expected future exchange rate.

The demand curve slopes downward because of two effects:

  • Exports effect: a lower exchange rate makes U.S. exports cheaper for foreigners, so they buy more, and need more dollars.
  • Expected profit effect: for a given expected future exchange rate, a lower rate today means a bigger expected profit from buying dollars now.

Supply of U.S. dollars comes from Americans who want to buy imports and foreign assets. The supply curve slopes upward because of:

  • Imports effect: a higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper for Americans, so they buy more and sell more dollars to get foreign currency.
  • Expected profit effect: a higher rate today means a bigger expected profit from selling dollars now and buying foreign currency.

Equilibrium: the exchange rate where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Above it there's a surplus of dollars and the rate falls; below it, a shortage and the rate rises.

Memory trick: "Exports demand, Imports supply"

Foreigners buying our exports create demand for our currency. We buying imports create supply of our currency.

Remember
  • Demand for dollars: foreigners buying U.S. exports and assets; slopes down
  • Supply of dollars: Americans buying imports and foreign assets; slopes up
  • Exports effect and imports effect, plus expected profit effects, explain the slopes
  • Surplus above equilibrium pushes the rate down; shortage below pushes it up

Arbitrage, speculation and market fundamentalsGood to knowSlides 31-40

  • Arbitrage: buying in one market and selling for a higher price in another related market. It drives the law of one price: if an item is traded in more than one place, it sells for the same price everywhere (once converted into the same currency).
  • Interest rate parity: equal rates of return in different currencies, once the expected change in the exchange rate is included. A country with a higher interest rate is expected to see its currency depreciate, wiping out the gain.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): equal value of money; one dollar buys the same amount everywhere. If prices rise faster in one country, its currency depreciates.
  • Speculation: trading in the hope of profiting from buying low and selling high. Expectations of the future exchange rate are a big part of why the rate is volatile.
  • Real exchange rate: the relative price of domestic and foreign goods: RER = E × (P ÷ P), where E is the nominal rate and P the foreign price level. In the long run the real exchange rate is determined by demand and supply in the goods market, and price levels by money.
Memory trick: "PPP = same Purchasing Power, everywhere"

Under PPP, money has the same purchasing power in every country. Under interest rate parity, assets have the same return in every currency.

Remember
  • Arbitrage enforces the law of one price
  • Interest rate parity: equal returns after expected exchange rate changes
  • PPP: money buys the same amount everywhere
  • Speculation and changing expectations make exchange rates volatile
  • Real exchange rate = E × P ÷ P*

Exchange rate policyMust knowSlides 41-45

Policy How it works
Flexible exchange rate Demand and supply decide the rate, with no direct intervention by the central bank (the U.S. dollar)
Fixed exchange rate The central bank pegs the rate at a value it chooses and intervenes in the market to hold it (the Saudi riyal at 3.75 per dollar, the Hong Kong dollar at 7.8)
Crawling peg The rate follows a planned path: the target is changed at a set pace (China has operated something close to it)

Defending a peg. If demand for the currency falls, the central bank buys its own currency with foreign currency: its official reserves fall. If demand rises, it sells its own currency and buys foreign currency: official reserves rise. A central bank can't keep selling reserves forever, so a peg under constant downward pressure eventually breaks.

If a central bank props up a falling currency, it buys its own money with reserves, so the vault drains. Holding down a rising currency fills the vault.

  • Holding up a weak currency: buy it, official reserves fall
  • Holding down a strong currency: sell it, official reserves rise

Capital and financial account Foreign investment in the country − the country's investment abroad (+ statistical discrepancy)

The three accounts sum to zero: current + capital and financial + official settlements = 0. An increase in official reserves shows up as a negative official settlements balance.

The U.S. was a net borrower: foreigners invested much more in the U.S. than Americans invested abroad, financing the current account deficit.
Memory trick: "Current = Goods, Capital = IOUs, Official = the Vault"

The current account tracks goods, services, interest and transfers. Capital and financial tracks borrowing and lending. Official settlements tracks the central bank's vault of reserves.

  • Capital and financial account = foreign investment in the country − its investment abroad
  • Official settlements = change in official reserves (an increase is recorded as negative)

Borrowers, lenders, debtors and creditorsGood to knowSlides 51-58

  • A net borrower borrows more from the rest of the world than it lends in a given year; a net lender does the opposite. These are flows.
  • A debtor nation has, over its history, borrowed more than it has lent; a creditor nation has lent more. These are stocks. The United States became a debtor nation in 1986.
  • In the global loanable funds market, funds flow to where they earn the highest return, so there's one world real interest rate. A country whose saving at that rate is less than its investment borrows from abroad; one whose saving exceeds investment lends.
  • Borrowing isn't a problem if the funds finance investment that earns a return. It's a problem if they finance consumption.
Memory trick: Borrower is a flow, Debtor is a pile

Net borrower: this year's flow. Debtor nation: the pile of debt built up over all past years.

Remember
  • Net borrower/lender: this year's flow
  • Debtor/creditor nation: the accumulated stock
  • One world real interest rate links national loanable funds markets
  • Borrowing to invest is sustainable; borrowing to consume isn't

Where's the exchange rate? In the short run, a deficit or surplus in the current account doesn't depend on the exchange rate; in the long run, the real exchange rate is determined by the real forces of demand and supply and doesn't affect the current account balance either. The current account balance is decided by saving and investment decisions.

Trap: A weaker currency doesn't automatically fix a deficit

The current account is driven by S − I and T − G. Cutting a government deficit or raising private saving narrows a current account deficit; a cheaper currency alone doesn't, in the long run.

Remember
  • X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
  • Private balance = S − I; government balance = T − G
  • A government deficit tends to come with a net exports deficit (twin deficits)
  • Saving and investment, not the exchange rate, decide the current account in the long run

1

Aggregate supply: LAS and SASMust knowSlides 4-9

The quantity of real GDP supplied is the total quantity of goods and services that firms plan to produce in a given period. Aggregate supply is the relationship between the quantity of real GDP supplied and the price level. There are two time frames:

Long-run aggregate supply (LAS) Short-run aggregate supply (SAS)
Shape Vertical at potential GDP Upward sloping
Condition Real GDP = potential GDP Money wage rate, other factor prices and potential GDP held constant
Why Potential GDP doesn't depend on the price level: the money wage rate changes by the same percentage as the price level, so the real wage stays at its full-employment level If the price level rises while the money wage rate stays the same, the real wage falls, production is more profitable, and firms produce more

Where SAS cuts LAS, the money wage rate is at its full-employment level. Above potential GDP, SAS is above LAS; below potential, it's below.

Think of it like this: The sticky wage contract

Workers sign wage contracts for a year. If prices jump in March, their money wage doesn't change until the contract ends, so labor is cheaper in real terms and firms hire more and produce more. That's SAS. Once contracts are renegotiated and wages catch up, firms are back at potential GDP: that's LAS.

Memory trick: "Long run Stands, Short run Slopes"

LAS stands straight up (vertical). SAS slopes up. AD slopes down.

Pop quiz · Chapter 10
Student sheet, quick check: why is the SAS curve upward sloping?
Show answer

Because money wages don't immediately change with the price level. A higher price level with an unchanged money wage lowers the real wage, so firms produce more.

Pop quiz · Chapter 10

Show answer

False. A change in the price level, with money wages and potential GDP constant, is a movement along the SAS curve.

Remember
  • LAS is vertical at potential GDP; potential GDP doesn't depend on the price level
  • SAS slopes up because the money wage rate is fixed in the short run
  • A price level change moves along SAS; it doesn't shift it
  • SAS cuts LAS where the money wage rate is at its full-employment level

Aggregate demand and why it slopes downMust knowSlides 14-20

The quantity of real GDP demanded is the total amount of final goods and services produced in the country that people, businesses, governments and foreigners plan to buy: Y = C + I + G + X − M. Aggregate demand is the relationship between the quantity of real GDP demanded and the price level.

The AD curve slopes downward because of three effects of a rise in the price level:

Effect Chain
Wealth effect Price level ↑ → real value of wealth ↓ → people save more, spend less → quantity demanded ↓
Intertemporal substitution effect Price level ↑ → real value of money ↓ → interest rate ↑ → less borrowing and spending now → quantity demanded ↓
International substitution effect Price level ↑ → domestic goods dearer relative to foreign goods → exports ↓, imports ↑ → quantity demanded ↓
Memory trick: "WII": Wealth, Intertemporal, International

The AD curve slopes down for three reasons: Wealth, Intertemporal substitution, International substitution. "WII go down."

Trap: Price level changes move along AD

A change in the price level is a movement along the AD curve. Only other influences shift it.

Remember
  • AD: quantity of real GDP demanded against the price level
  • Slopes down because of wealth, intertemporal and international substitution effects
  • A change in the price level moves along AD

  • Monetary policy is the Fed's attempt to influence the economy by changing the interest rate and the quantity of money.

Short-run macroeconomic equilibriumMust knowSlides 27-29

Short-run macroeconomic equilibrium occurs when the quantity of real GDP demanded equals the quantity supplied: the point where AD intersects SAS. Real GDP can be above, below or equal to potential GDP.

If the price level is above equilibrium, firms can't sell all they produce, so they cut output and prices; if it's below, they raise output and prices. These adjustments move the economy along the SAS curve to equilibrium.

Remember
  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SAS
  • Real GDP can be above, below or at potential GDP in the short run
  • Firms adjust output and prices along SAS to reach it
6

Long-run equilibrium, growth and inflationMust knowSlides 30-34

Long-run macroeconomic equilibrium occurs when real GDP equals potential GDP: the economy is on the LAS curve, where AD, SAS and LAS all meet. The money wage rate adjusts to bring the economy there.

  • Economic growth: potential GDP grows, so LAS shifts right.
  • Inflation: the quantity of money grows faster than potential GDP, so AD shifts right faster than LAS.
Memory trick: Growth = LAS right; Inflation = AD outruns LAS

Growth pushes LAS right. Inflation happens when AD runs to the right faster than LAS.

Remember
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD, SAS and LAS meet; real GDP = potential GDP
  • Growth: LAS shifts right over time
  • Inflation: AD grows faster than LAS (money grows faster than potential GDP)

The business cycle and fluctuationsMust knowSlides 35-39

The business cycle happens because AD and SAS fluctuate while the money wage rate doesn't change fast enough to keep real GDP at potential.

Equilibrium Name Unemployment
Real GDP above potential GDP Inflationary gap Below the natural rate
Real GDP equal to potential GDP Full-employment equilibrium At the natural rate
Real GDP below potential GDP Recessionary gap Above the natural rate

Fluctuations in aggregate demand. Starting at full employment, AD increases:

  1. Short run: AD shifts right along SAS. Real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises: an inflationary gap.
  2. Long run: workers demand higher money wages to restore the real wage, so SAS shifts left. The price level rises further and real GDP returns to potential GDP.

A decrease in AD does the opposite: a recessionary gap opens; money wages slowly fall, SAS shifts right, and real GDP returns to potential at a lower price level (but this can take a long time).

Fluctuations in aggregate supply. A rise in the price of oil shifts SAS left: real GDP falls and the price level rises at the same time. That combination is stagflation.

An increase in aggregate demand from full employment
AD shifts right; the economy moves up along SAS.
Real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises: an inflationary gap.
Labor is scarce, so the money wage rate rises and SAS shifts left.
The economy returns to LAS: real GDP back at potential, price level higher.
Memory trick: "Gap closes through wages"

An inflationary gap closes when money wages rise (SAS shifts left). A recessionary gap closes when money wages fall (SAS shifts right). In the long run, AD changes move only the price level.

Trap: Stagflation comes from supply, not demand

When real GDP falls and the price level rises, the cause is a leftward shift of SAS (higher oil prices or money wages). A fall in AD lowers both real GDP and the price level.

Remember
  • Inflationary gap: real GDP above potential; recessionary gap: below
  • An AD increase: short run, real GDP and the price level rise; long run, money wages rise and real GDP returns to potential
  • An SAS decrease (e.g. oil prices up): real GDP falls, price level rises (stagflation)
  • In the long run, real GDP returns to potential GDP

MPC, MPS, autonomous and induced spendingMust knowStudent sheet, Class 1

Marginal propensities

MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYDMPS = ΔS ÷ ΔYDMPC + MPS = 1

The MPC is the fraction of a change in disposable income that's spent on consumption, not the share of total income consumed.

Group work, Problem 1: income rises from $400 bn to $500 bn, consumption from $420 bn to $500 bn
ΔYD = 100 and ΔC = 80, so MPC = 80 ÷ 100 = 0.8.
Saving at $400 bn = 400 − 420 = −20; saving at $500 bn = 500 − 500 = 0. ΔS = +$20 billion.
MPS = 20 ÷ 100 = 0.2 (check: 0.8 + 0.2 = 1).
Autonomous expenditure Induced expenditure
Doesn't change when real GDP changes Changes when real GDP changes
Investment, government expenditure, exports, and autonomous consumption Induced consumption, imports

Why isn't all consumption induced? Even with zero income, people must eat: they consume out of past savings or by borrowing. That base level of consumption, set by wealth, expected future income and interest rates, is autonomous consumption. Only the part that rises with income is induced.

Memory trick: "Every extra riyal is either spent or saved"

MPC + MPS = 1: each extra riyal of disposable income is either spent or saved, nothing else. If MPC = 0.75, then MPS = 0.25.

Trap: MPC uses changes, not levels

MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYD. In Problem 1, consumption is $500 bn out of $500 bn of income, but the MPC is 0.8, not 1.

Remember
  • MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYD; MPS = ΔS ÷ ΔYD; MPC + MPS = 1
  • Autonomous spending doesn't depend on real GDP; induced spending does
  • Consumption has an autonomous part (spending even at zero income) and an induced part

Equilibrium expenditureMust knowStudent sheet, Class 1

Equilibrium expenditure is the level of aggregate expenditure at which planned AE equals real GDP: the point where the AE curve crosses the 45° line.

If inventories fall (shelves empty), people are buying more than firms make: AE > Y, so firms make more. Full shelves mean AE < Y and firms make less.

The multiplierMust knowStudent sheet, Class 2

A rise in autonomous expenditure (investment, say) raises production and income. Higher income induces more consumption, which raises income again, and so on. The total change in real GDP is bigger than the first change in spending.

The multiplier

Multiplier = ΔY ÷ ΔA (change in real GDP ÷ change in autonomous expenditure)Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − slope of AE curve)Simple model (no taxes, no imports): multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPSΔY = multiplier × ΔA
Group work, Problems 6–7: MPC = 0.80, real GDP $150 bn, investment rises by $5 bn
Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.80) = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.
ΔY = 5 × $5 bn = $25 billion.
New real GDP = 150 + 25 = $175 billion.
Why more than $5 bn: the $5 bn of investment becomes $5 bn of income; households spend 80% of it ($4 bn), which becomes someone else's income, and they spend 80% of that ($3.2 bn), and so on.
Pop quiz · Chapter 11
Student sheet: a $75 bn increase in autonomous expenditure raises equilibrium expenditure by $150 bn. The multiplier?
Show answer

150 ÷ 75 = 2. (A multiplier is a pure number, never a dollar amount.)

Pop quiz · Chapter 11
Student sheet: investment rises by $300 and equilibrium expenditure by $600. The multiplier?
Show answer

Memory trick: "One over the leak"

Multiplier = 1 ÷ leak. In the simple model the only leak is saving, so 1 ÷ MPS. MPS 0.2 → multiplier 5; MPS 0.25 → multiplier 4; MPS 0.5 → multiplier 2.

Trap: A bigger MPC means a bigger multiplier

A higher MPC means more of each round is re-spent, so the multiplier is larger. A higher MPS (or a higher marginal propensity to import) makes it smaller.

Remember
  • Multiplier = ΔY ÷ ΔA, always greater than 1 with fixed prices
  • Simple multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS
  • ΔY = multiplier × change in autonomous expenditure
  • A higher MPC gives a larger multiplier
5

Why the real-world multiplier is smallerMust knowStudent sheet, Class 2

Saving, income taxes and imports are leakages: each takes part of every extra riyal of income out of the spending stream. Taxes and imports make the AE curve flatter (a smaller slope than the MPC), so the multiplier is smaller.

The multiplier and the price level. The fixed-price model predicts the full multiplier effect. But when higher aggregate demand raises the price level (the SAS curve slopes up), part of the increase in spending shows up as higher prices, so the final increase in real GDP is smaller than the fixed-price prediction. In the long run, at full employment, the multiplier is zero: real GDP returns to potential and only the price level rises.

Memory trick: "STIM": the leaks

Saving, Taxes, Imports reduce the Multiplier.

Remember
  • Saving, taxes and imports are leakages that shrink the multiplier
  • Taxes and imports make the AE curve flatter
  • A rising price level makes the real GDP increase smaller than the fixed-price multiplier predicts
  • In the long run at full employment, the multiplier is zero

The business cycle in the AS-AD modelMust knowSlides 4-15

Economic growth, inflation and the business cycle are all parts of one process: potential GDP grows (LAS shifts right), and aggregate demand grows too, usually faster because the quantity of money grows. What happens depends on how fast AD grows compared with potential GDP:

AD grows… Result
At the same pace as potential GDP The economy stays at full employment; steady growth with steady inflation
More slowly than potential GDP Real GDP grows slowly, a recessionary gap opens, inflation slows
Faster than potential GDP Real GDP grows fast, an inflationary gap opens, inflation speeds up

Three shocks drive fluctuations (from the short slides):

Shock Curve Real GDP Price level
AD increases AD right Up Up
AD decreases AD left Down Down
Adverse supply shock SAS left Down Up
Memory trick: "Identify the cause first"

The short slides' rule: name the cause (spending → AD; costs → SAS; capacity → LAS), then predict real GDP and the price level. Never start from the answer.

2

Mainstream and real business cycle theoriesJust skimSlides 16-24

  • Mainstream approach: potential GDP grows at a steady rate, while aggregate demand grows at a fluctuating rate. Money wages are sticky, so swings in AD (from expectations, fiscal and monetary policy, and the world economy) cause swings in real GDP.
  • Real business cycle (RBC) theory: fluctuations come from changes in productivity growth (technology shocks). The impulse is a productivity change; the mechanism is that people decide when to work (intertemporal substitution): a lower real interest rate makes it worth working less now and more later. In RBC theory, money doesn't cause the cycle.
  • Criticisms of RBC: money wages are in fact sticky, intertemporal substitution is too weak to explain big swings in employment, and productivity shocks are hard to observe independently of output. Defenders say RBC explains the facts about growth and cycles with one theory, and that its micro foundations are consistent.
Memory trick: Mainstream = Spending, RBC = Technology

Mainstream: swings in spending (AD) cause the cycle. RBC: swings in technology (productivity) cause it.

Remember
  • Mainstream: fluctuating AD with sticky money wages
  • RBC: productivity shocks and people's choice of when to work
  • RBC says money doesn't drive the cycle; critics point to sticky wages
3

Demand-pull inflationMust knowSlides 25-31

Demand-pull inflation starts because aggregate demand increases (more money, more government spending, higher exports).

The demand-pull process
Trigger: AD shifts right. Real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises: an inflationary gap.
Wage response: labor is scarce, so the money wage rate rises and SAS shifts left. Real GDP returns to potential and the price level rises again.
Continuing: if the central bank keeps increasing the quantity of money, AD keeps shifting right, wages keep chasing prices, and the price level keeps rising: an inflation spiral.
Trap: A one-time price rise isn't inflation

A single increase in AD gives a one-time rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs AD to keep increasing, and in the long run that only happens if the quantity of money keeps growing.

Pop quiz · Chapter 12
Predicted question: the government raises spending at full employment. Short run and long run?
Show answer

Short run: AD shifts right, real GDP rises above potential and the price level rises. Long run: money wages rise, SAS shifts left, real GDP returns to potential and the price level is higher still. It's a one-time rise unless AD keeps increasing.

Remember
  • Demand-pull starts with AD shifting right: real GDP and the price level rise
  • Money wages then rise, shifting SAS left
  • Inflation continues only if money growth keeps shifting AD right

Cost-push inflationMust knowSlides 32-37

Cost-push inflation starts with an increase in costs: a rise in money wage rates or in the money prices of raw materials such as oil.

The cost-push process
Trigger: costs rise, so SAS shifts left. Real GDP falls and the price level rises: stagflation.
AD response: unemployment rises, so the central bank may increase the quantity of money to restore full employment. AD shifts right; real GDP returns to potential, but the price level rises further.
Continuing: oil producers (or unions) see their real income fall and push costs up again, SAS shifts left again, the central bank responds again, and a cost-push inflation spiral develops.
Demand-pull Cost-push
Initial shift AD right SAS left
Real GDP at first Increases Decreases
Price level Increases Increases
Keeps going if… AD keeps growing (money growth) Costs keep rising and the central bank accommodates with money growth
Memory trick: Demand Pulls output Up; Cost Pushes output Down

Both raise prices. Demand-pull pulls real GDP up; cost-push pushes real GDP down (stagflation).

Trap: Stagflation = cost-push

If real GDP falls while prices rise, the cause is on the supply side (SAS left). A demand shock moves real GDP and the price level in the same direction.

Expected inflationGood to knowSlides 38-41

If inflation is expected, the money wage rate rises in line with the expected rise in the price level. AD and SAS shift together, so the price level rises while real GDP stays at potential GDP. To expect inflation correctly, people must forecast it, using all relevant data: the best forecast is a rational expectation.

When the inflation forecast is wrong, real GDP moves away from potential: inflation higher than expected opens an inflationary gap; lower than expected, a recessionary gap.

Remember
  • Expected inflation: AD and SAS shift together, real GDP stays at potential
  • Unexpected inflation moves real GDP away from potential
  • A rational expectation uses all available information

Deflation and disinflationMust knowSlides 42-45

Deflation Disinflation
The price level falls The inflation rate falls, but prices may still rise
The inflation rate is negative The inflation rate is positive but smaller

Causes. The price level falls persistently if aggregate demand grows more slowly than potential GDP, usually because the quantity of money grows too slowly (or shrinks). Japan is the textbook case: in the 1990s and 2000s real GDP grew slowly, the money supply grew slowly, and prices fell.

Consequences. Unanticipated deflation redistributes income and wealth, lowers real GDP and diverts resources, like unpredictable inflation. In particular:

Borrowers repay debts with money that's worth more, so the real burden of debt rises and spending falls further. Anticipated deflation also pushes interest rates down toward zero, limiting monetary policy.

Ending deflation. Support demand with monetary easing (faster money growth) or fiscal stimulus, so AD strengthens and steady price pressure returns. The ultimate cure is to raise the money growth rate.

Memory trick: Deflation = Down prices; Disinflation = Decelerating inflation

Deflation: prices go down. Disinflation: inflation slows but prices still go up.

Trap: A falling inflation rate isn't deflation

Inflation falling from 6% to 2% is disinflation: prices are still rising, just more slowly. Deflation needs a negative inflation rate.

The Phillips curveMust knowSlides 46-53

A Phillips curve shows the relationship between the inflation rate and the unemployment rate.

Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC) Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)
Shape Downward sloping Vertical at the natural unemployment rate
Holds constant Expected inflation and the natural unemployment rate Actual inflation = expected inflation
Meaning Higher inflation goes with lower unemployment (a tradeoff) No permanent tradeoff
  • The SRPC intersects the LRPC at the expected inflation rate.
  • A rise in expected inflation shifts the SRPC upward by the same amount; the LRPC doesn't move.
  • A rise in the natural unemployment rate shifts both curves rightward.

Moving along the SRPC corresponds to moving along the SAS curve: a surprise rise in AD raises inflation and lowers unemployment in the short run.

Memory trick: "Short run Slopes, Long run Lines up"

SRPC slopes down (a tradeoff). LRPC lines up straight and vertical at the natural rate (no tradeoff). Same idea as SAS and LAS.

Trap: Expectations shift the SRPC, not the LRPC

Higher expected inflation shifts the SRPC up. The LRPC moves only when the natural unemployment rate changes.

Pop quiz · Chapter 12

Remember
  • SRPC slopes down: inflation and unemployment trade off in the short run
  • LRPC is vertical at the natural rate: no long-run tradeoff
  • Higher expected inflation shifts the SRPC up
  • A higher natural rate shifts both curves right

The budget

Government debt is the total amount the government has borrowed: the accumulation of all past deficits minus past surpluses. A deficit is a flow (this year); the debt is a stock (all years). Since the mid-2000s the U.S. has run large deficits, and debt has grown well above 100% of GDP.

Short slides: tax rate 10%, income $800 bn, outlays $100 bn
Tax revenue = 0.10 × 800 = $80 billion.

Receipts = outlays − deficit = 3.4 − 1.7 = $1.7 trillion.
The deficit is financed by borrowing, so government debt increases by $1.7 trillion.
To stop the debt growing, policymakers must cut outlays or raise receipts until the budget is balanced (or in surplus).
Memory trick: Deficit is the Drip, debt is the Bucket

Each year's deficit is a drip; the debt is the bucket that fills up. A smaller deficit still adds to the debt; only a surplus empties the bucket.

Trap: A falling deficit doesn't mean falling debt

If the deficit shrinks from $1.7 trillion to $1 trillion, the debt still rises by $1 trillion. The debt falls only with a surplus.

Remember

  • Deficit: a flow; debt: the stock of past borrowing
  • Any deficit adds to the debt; only surpluses reduce it

Supply-side effects of fiscal policyJust skimSlides 17-26

Taxes affect potential GDP, not just AD:

  • An income tax drives a tax wedge between the before-tax real wage firms pay and the after-tax real wage workers receive. Labor supply falls, so employment and potential GDP fall.
  • Taxes on expenditure (sales taxes) add to the wedge.
  • Taxes on interest income lower the after-tax real interest rate, so saving, investment and growth fall.
  • The Laffer curve shows that raising the tax rate beyond some point (T*) lowers tax revenue, because the tax base shrinks.
Remember
  • An income tax creates a tax wedge, lowering employment and potential GDP
  • Taxing interest income reduces saving, investment and growth
  • Laffer curve: above some rate, higher tax rates bring in less revenue
4

Generational effectsJust skimSlides 27-35

Generational accounting measures the lifetime tax burden and benefits of each generation using present values. The Social Security time bomb: as the baby-boom generation retires, benefits promised far exceed the present value of future taxes. The gap between the two is the fiscal imbalance; the part that falls on future generations is the generational imbalance. Persistent borrowing also adds to international debt as foreigners buy government bonds.

Remember
  • Generational accounting compares each generation's taxes and benefits in present value
  • Promised Social Security benefits exceed future taxes: a fiscal imbalance
  • Future generations bear the generational imbalance

Automatic stabilizers and the structural deficitMust knowSlides 36-45

A fiscal stimulus is the use of fiscal policy to increase production and employment. It can be automatic or discretionary:

Automatic stabilizers Discretionary policy
No new law or decision needed Needs a new government decision (an act of Congress)

Structural and cyclical balances. Because receipts fall and outlays rise in a recession, the budget moves toward deficit even with no policy change.

Structural balance = the balance if real GDP were at potential GDPCyclical balance = actual balance − structural balance

Discretionary fiscal stimulus and multipliersMust knowSlides 46-50

Situation Fiscal policy Effect on AD
Recessionary gap Expansionary: increase G or cut taxes AD right
Inflationary gap Contractionary: cut G or raise taxes AD left

The size of the effect depends on the multipliers (Chapter 11):

  • Government expenditure multiplier: the effect of a change in G on equilibrium real GDP. It is greater than 1, because the extra G creates income that induces more consumption.
  • Tax multiplier: the effect of a change in taxes. It's negative (a tax cut raises spending) and smaller in absolute size than the G multiplier, because part of a tax cut is saved, not spent.
  • Balanced budget multiplier: equal increases in G and taxes still raise real GDP, because the G multiplier is bigger than the tax multiplier.
Memory trick: G beats T

A riyal of G is spent in full in the first round; a riyal of tax cut is partly saved. So the G multiplier beats the tax multiplier, and a balanced increase in both still raises real GDP.

Trap: Stimulus in the short run, prices in the long run

Fiscal stimulus raises real GDP and the price level in the short run. If it pushes real GDP above potential, money wages rise and the long-run effect is mostly on prices.

Remember
  • Recessionary gap: increase G or cut taxes (AD right)
  • Inflationary gap: cut G or raise taxes (AD left)
  • G multiplier > tax multiplier in size; the tax multiplier is negative
  • Balanced budget multiplier is positive
7

Limits: supply effects, magnitude and time lagsGood to knowSlides 51-53

  • Aggregate supply effects: tax cuts also lower the tax wedge, so they can raise potential GDP as well as AD.
  • Magnitude: economists disagree about the size of the multipliers, so it's hard to know how big a stimulus is needed.
  • Time lags make discretionary policy slow and risky:
  • Recognition lag: the time it takes to work out that action is needed.
  • Law-making lag: the time Congress takes to pass the budget.
  • Impact lag: the time from passing a law to its effect on real GDP.

By the time a stimulus takes effect, the economy may already be recovering, so the policy can make fluctuations worse.

Memory trick: Three lags: "Realize, Ratify, React"

Realize the problem (recognition lag), ratify the law (law-making lag), wait for the economy to react (impact lag).

Remember
  • Tax cuts can raise potential GDP through supply-side effects
  • Multiplier size is uncertain
  • Recognition, law-making and impact lags slow discretionary policy

What monetary policy is and what it aims forMust knowSlides 1-3

Monetary policy uses central bank decisions to influence interest rates and economic activity. In the U.S. the Fed has a dual mandate, set by law:

  1. Price stability: low and stable inflation (in practice, about 2% a year on the PCE price index).
  2. Maximum sustainable employment: not zero unemployment, but unemployment close to its natural rate.

Why price stability matters. Low, stable inflation lets people plan, sign long-term contracts and save without worrying that money will lose value. Unpredictable inflation redistributes income and wealth and diverts resources into forecasting it (Chapter 5). Price stability is also the foundation for sustained growth and employment in the long run.

Who decides. The FOMC sets the target for the federal funds rate, the interest rate banks charge each other on overnight loans of reserves, and then uses its tools to hit it.

Memory trick: "Two jobs: Prices and People"

The central bank's two goals: stable prices (low inflation) and people at work (maximum sustainable employment).

"Maximum sustainable employment" means unemployment at about the natural rate. Pushing it lower would create an inflationary gap and rising inflation.

The transmission mechanismMust knowSlides 7-9

How a policy rate change reaches the economy, step by step:

When the central bank lowers the rate:

  1. Short-term rates fall, then longer-term rates (loans, mortgages) fall.
  2. Borrowing is cheaper: business investment and consumption (especially of durables and houses) rise.
  3. Lower U.S. rates also make the dollar depreciate, so net exports rise.
  4. AD shifts right: real GDP and employment rise in the short run, and inflation rises.

When it raises the rate, everything runs the other way: investment and consumption slow, the currency appreciates, AD shifts left, and inflationary pressure eases.

Group work, Q3: the central bank lowers interest rates
Borrowing costs fall, so more investment projects become profitable: business investment increases.
Investment and consumption rise, and the currency tends to depreciate, raising net exports.
AD shifts right, raising real GDP and employment in the short run (and putting upward pressure on prices).

The transmission has long and variable lags: a rate change can take a year or two to have its full effect on output and inflation, so the central bank has to act on forecasts.

Memory trick: "R → L → C/I → AD → Y,P"

Rate, Loan rates, Consumption and Investment, AD, then real GDP (Y) and the price level (P). Five links, always in that order.

Trap: Lower rates raise AD, not AS

A rate cut works through spending, so it shifts AD. It doesn't directly shift SAS or LAS.

Remember
  • Policy rate → loan rates → investment and consumption → AD → real GDP, jobs, inflation
  • Lower rates: investment and consumption rise, AD shifts right
  • Higher rates: spending slows, AD shifts left
  • The effects arrive with long and variable lags

May cut the policy rate sharply, even to near zero, and buy assets on a large scale (quantitative easing) Higher capital requirements, liquidity requirements, limits on risky lending and leverage, stress tests

In the 2008 financial crisis, falling house prices left banks insolvent or illiquid; the Fed lent to banks and other institutions, cut the federal funds rate to almost zero and bought large amounts of securities.

Pop quiz · Chapter 14
Group work, Q4: what is macroprudential regulation? Give one example
Show answer

Rules that limit risk across the financial system as a whole, not just at one bank. Example: higher capital requirements, so banks have more of their own funds to absorb losses before they become insolvent. (Others: liquidity requirements, stress tests, limits on risky lending.)

Memory trick: "Cure = Cash, Prevent = Prudence"

In a crisis, the cure is cash (emergency liquidity). To prevent one, use prudence (macroprudential rules like capital requirements).

Trap: Emergency lending is a cure, not prevention

Emergency liquidity responds after a crisis starts. Capital and liquidity rules work before, to stop one happening.

Remember
  • Cure: emergency liquidity, lender of last resort, deep rate cuts
  • Prevention: macroprudential regulation of the whole system
  • Example: higher capital requirements so banks can absorb losses

The government runs a larger deficit (no Ricardo-Barro effect) while the Fed sells securities. What happens to the real interest rate and private investment?
Show answer

The deficit raises the demand for loanable funds and the open market sale tightens money: both push interest rates up, so private investment falls (crowding out).
