CHAPTER 7 · FINANCE, SAVING, AND INVESTMENT

Finance, Saving, and Investment

SlidesBreakdownMind mapChapter quizStudent sheetGroup workChapter mapAssignment 2

Financial markets channel saving into investment: the real interest rate adjusts until the supply of loanable funds (saving) equals the demand (investment), and a government budget deficit raises the real interest rate and crowds out private investment unless households save more (the Ricardo-Barro effect).

★

The big picture

Financial markets channel saving into investment: the real interest rate adjusts until the supply of loanable funds (saving) equals the demand (investment), and a government budget deficit raises the real interest rate and crowds out private investment unless households save more (the Ricardo-Barro effect).

Chapter 7 is on Quiz II and Assignment 2. Expect quick calculations (net investment, capital stock, saving and wealth, the real interest rate, present value and NPV), solvency versus liquidity, and many "which way does the curve shift and what happens to the real interest rate?" questions. The group work uses a loanable funds table to find equilibrium, crowding out and the Ricardo-Barro outcome.

Where this fits

Chapter 6 said capital accumulation drives growth. This chapter shows where the funds for that capital come from. Chapter 8 adds money and banks, and Chapter 13 returns to deficits as fiscal policy.

By the end you can
  • Distinguish physical capital from financial capital, and gross from net investment
  • Calculate saving, wealth, the real interest rate, present value and NPV
  • Tell insolvency from illiquidity, and explain why asset prices and interest rates move opposite ways
  • Explain the demand for and supply of loanable funds and find the equilibrium real interest rate
  • Predict the effects of shifts in demand and supply
  • Explain crowding out and the Ricardo-Barro effect

Capital, investment, wealth and savingMust knowSlides 4-12

Term Meaning
Physical capital Tools, machines, buildings, vehicles (a Taco Bell store, Hertz's rental cars)
Financial capital The funds firms use to buy physical capital (shares, bonds, loans)
Gross investment Total spending on new capital
Depreciation The fall in the value of capital from wear and tear and obsolescence
Net investment Gross investment − depreciation = the change in the value of capital
Wealth The value of everything a person owns: a stock
Saving Disposable income − consumption: a flow that adds to wealth

Capital and wealth

Net investment = gross investment − depreciationCapital at end of year = capital at start + net investmentSaving = disposable income − consumption expenditureWealth at end = wealth at start + saving (+ capital gains − capital losses)
Group work, Problems 1–2: Michael owns $400,000 of capital, buys $500,000 of new servers, and old servers lose $100,000 in value
Gross investment = $500,000.
Depreciation = $100,000.
Net investment = 500,000 − 100,000 = $400,000.
Capital at the end of 2022 = 400,000 + 400,000 = $800,000.
Assignment 2, Q2: Tim's Gyms owns $1 million of machines, they lose 30% of their value, and Tim buys $200,000 of new machines
Depreciation = 30% × $1,000,000 = $300,000.
Net investment = 200,000 − 300,000 = −$100,000: the capital stock shrank.
Group work, Problem 3: Lori earns $20,000 after tax plus $300 interest, spends $15,300, and starts with $1,000 of assets and $5,000 of savings
Disposable income = 20,000 + 300 = $20,300.
Saving = 20,300 − 15,300 = $5,000.
Wealth at start = 1,000 + 5,000 = $6,000; at end = 6,000 + 5,000 = $11,000.

Financial markets are where firms get financial capital:

  • Loan markets: businesses borrow from banks; households take mortgages.
  • Bond markets: a bond is a promise to make specified payments on specified dates.
  • Stock markets: a stock (share) is a certificate of ownership and a claim to the firm's profits.

Financial institutions (commercial banks, government-sponsored mortgage lenders, pension funds, insurance companies) operate on both sides of these markets: they borrow in one and lend in another. Investment is financed by private saving + government budget surplus + borrowing from the rest of the world.

Trap: Shares and salaries aren't physical capital

Assignment 2, Q1: physical capital is II and III only, the restaurant building and the rental cars. Shares of stock are financial capital, and salaries are a payment to labor.

Memory trick: Wealth is a Stock, saving is a Stream

Wealth is a stock (measured at a point in time, like water in a tank). Saving is a stream (a flow per period) that fills the tank.

Remember
  • Physical capital is real equipment; financial capital is the funds that buy it
  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation
  • Saving = disposable income − consumption; saving adds to wealth
  • Investment is financed by private saving, the budget surplus and borrowing from abroad

Net present value and financial decisionsMust knowSlides 13-15

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future, because today's dollar can earn interest.

Future value, present value and NPV

Future value = present value × (1 + r)ⁿPresent value = future amount ÷ (1 + r)ⁿNPV = present value of future returns − cost todayDecision rule: invest if NPV > 0
Assignment 2, Q4: present value of $200 in two years at 7%
PV = 200 ÷ (1.07)² = 200 ÷ 1.1449 = $174.69 ≈ $175.
Group work, Problems 4–5 and extension (r = 10%)
Tom borrows $2,000 for two years: repays 2,000 × (1.10)² = $2,420.
Joe wants $5,000 in two years: deposits 5,000 ÷ 1.21 = $4,132.23 today.
A project costs $4,000 and pays $5,000 in two years: NPV = 4,132.23 − 4,000 = $132.23 > 0, so undertake it.
Memory trick: Future: multiply. Present: divide.

Going forward in time, multiply by (1 + r) for each year. Coming back to today, divide.

Remember
  • PV = future amount ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
  • NPV = PV of returns − cost
  • Invest when NPV is positive
  • A higher interest rate lowers present values

Financial risk, asset prices and real interest ratesMust knowSlides 16-19

Insolvent Illiquid
Net worth (assets − liabilities) is negative Has enough assets but can't turn them into cash to pay now
A solvency problem: the firm owes more than it owns A cash problem: it can usually borrow to get through

Interest rates and asset prices move in opposite directions. A bond paying $5 a year has an interest rate of 5% if its price is $100. If the price rises to $200, the same $5 is only a 2.5% return.

Real interest rate

Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate

Memory trick: "Insolvent = In the red"

Insolvent: net worth in the red (negative). Illiquid: ill with no cash right now.

Trap: Bond price up, interest rate down

When the price of a bond rises, its interest rate (yield) falls. They always move in opposite directions.

Remember
  • Insolvent: negative net worth; illiquid: can't pay right now
  • Asset prices and interest rates move in opposite directions
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation
  • The real interest rate is the opportunity cost of loanable funds

The demand for loanable fundsMust knowSlides 20-23

The market for loanable funds is the aggregate of all the individual financial markets. The quantity demanded depends on the real interest rate and expected profit.

  • The demand curve slopes downward: at a lower real interest rate, more projects are profitable, so firms borrow more.
  • Expected profit shifts it: higher expected profit (from technological change or a business cycle expansion) shifts demand rightward.
Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Group work, Problems 6–7: First Call does $8m of projects at 7%, $10m at 6% and $12m at 5%. What if expected profit doubles?
Show answer

Plotted, these points form a downward-sloping demand for loanable funds. If expected profit doubles, more projects are profitable at every interest rate, so demand shifts rightward.

Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Assignment 2, Q6: technological progress raises expected profit
Show answer

The demand for loanable funds shifts rightward and the real interest rate rises.

Remember
  • Demand for loanable funds = investment demand
  • Slopes down: a lower real interest rate makes more projects worthwhile
  • Higher expected profit shifts it rightward
5

The supply of loanable fundsMust knowSlides 24-27

The quantity supplied depends on the real interest rate, disposable income, expected future income, wealth and default risk.

  • The supply curve slopes upward: a higher real interest rate rewards saving, so people save more.
  • It shifts rightward when disposable income rises, and leftward when wealth or expected future income rises (people feel richer and save less) or default risk rises.
Memory trick: Supply shifters: "D-E-W-D"

Disposable income (up → more saving), Expected future income (up → less), Wealth (up → less), Default risk (up → less). Only disposable income moves supply the same way it moves.

Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Assignment 2, Q8: household disposable income falls by 0.3%. Effect on the real interest rate?
Show answer

Saving falls, so the supply of loanable funds decreases (shifts left) and the real interest rate rises.

Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Assignment 2, Q9: disposable income increases
Show answer

People increase saving, the supply of loanable funds increases, and the real interest rate falls.

Remember
  • Supply of loanable funds = saving
  • Slopes up: a higher real interest rate encourages saving
  • Higher disposable income increases supply
  • Higher wealth, expected future income or default risk decreases supply

Group work, Problem 8: equilibrium, more saving, more investment
a) Equilibrium: 7%, with investment = saving = $7 trillion.
b) Saving rises by $0.5 trillion at every rate: at 7% saving is 7.5 > 7.0; at 6% saving is 7.0 < 7.5. The new equilibrium is about 6.5% and $7.25 trillion: more supply lowers the rate and raises the quantity.

Government in the loanable funds marketMust knowSlides 33-36

  • A budget surplus adds to the supply of loanable funds: the real interest rate falls, private investment rises, and saving falls back somewhat.
  • A budget deficit adds to the demand for loanable funds: the real interest rate rises, and private investment decreases. The fall in private investment is crowding out.
  • The Ricardo-Barro effect: taxpayers expect future taxes to repay the debt, so they save more today. Private saving rises by the amount of the deficit, the real interest rate doesn't change, and there's no crowding out.
Group work, Problem 9: a $1 trillion budget deficit, no Ricardo-Barro effect
Total demand = investment + 1. At 8%: 6.5 + 1 = 7.5 = saving 7.5.
New real interest rate = 8%; private investment = $6.5 trillion.
Crowding out = 7.0 − 6.5 = $0.5 trillion.
Group work, Problem 10: households save the full extra $1 trillion (Ricardo-Barro)
Demand and supply both rise by $1 trillion, so the real interest rate stays at 7%.
Private investment stays at $7 trillion: no crowding out.
Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Group work, Problem 28: Kuwait runs a budget surplus
Show answer

The supply of loanable funds increases, the real interest rate falls, private investment rises, and faster capital accumulation boosts long-run economic growth.

Pop quiz · Chapter 7
Assignment 2, Q10: a larger deficit with no Ricardo-Barro effect
Show answer

The demand for loanable funds increases and the real interest rate rises.

Memory trick: Deficit → Demand, Surplus → Supply

A deficit adds to demand; a surplus adds to supply. Both start with the same letter.

Trap: Ricardo-Barro means no change in the rate

If the question says households save the whole deficit, the real interest rate and private investment don't change. Crowding out happens only without the Ricardo-Barro effect.

Remember
  • Budget surplus: supply of funds up, real interest rate down, investment up
  • Budget deficit: demand for funds up, real interest rate up, private investment down (crowding out)
  • Ricardo-Barro: saving rises by the deficit, so no crowding out

What money isMust knowSlides 4-12

Money is any commodity or token that is generally acceptable as a means of payment, a method of settling a debt. Money has three functions:

Function Meaning Example
Medium of exchange Generally accepted in exchange for goods and services; avoids barter's double coincidence of wants Paying cash for groceries
Unit of account An agreed measure for stating prices Every price tag is in riyals
Store of value Can be held and exchanged later for goods and services Keeping $200 at home for an emergency
Memory trick: "MUS": Money Must Serve

Medium of exchange, Unit of account, Store of value. "Money MUSt serve three ways."

Money today consists of currency (notes and coins held outside banks) and deposits at banks and other depository institutions.

Official measures of money

M1 = currency outside banks + demand deposits + other liquid deposits (including savings deposits)M2 = M1 + small time deposits + retail money market funds
Group work, Problem 2: currency $1,824 bn, demand deposits $2,126 bn, other liquid deposits $12,234 bn, small time deposits $466 bn, retail money funds $1,142 bn
M1 = 1,824 + 2,126 + 12,234 = $16,184 billion.
M2 = 16,184 + 466 + 1,142 = $17,792 billion.
Assignment 2, Q12: currency outside banks $200 m, currency in banks $250 m, individuals' demand deposits $300 m, businesses' demand deposits $600 m, other liquid deposits $100 m
M1 = 200 + 300 + 600 + 100 = $1,200 million.
The $250 million held by banks isn't counted: it's bank reserves, not currency held by the public.
Trap: Cheques, debit cards and credit cards aren't money

Deposits are money. A cheque or debit card is only an instruction to move money between deposits. A credit card is an ID card that lets you borrow: the loan has to be repaid with money.

Depository institutionsMust knowSlides 13-20

A depository institution takes deposits from households and firms and makes loans. The main ones are commercial banks, thrift institutions and money market funds.

A bank's goal is to maximize its owners' wealth by lending at a higher rate than it pays depositors, but it must balance profit against the risk of loans going bad and of running out of cash. It puts depositors' funds into reserves (cash in the vault and deposits at the Fed), liquid assets (Treasury bills, overnight loans to other banks), securities and loans.

Depository institutions provide four benefits:

  1. Create liquidity: they borrow short (deposits) and lend long (loans).
  2. Pool risk: spreading loans across many borrowers lowers the risk to each depositor.
  3. Lower the cost of borrowing: one loan from a bank instead of many small loans from individuals.
  4. Lower the cost of monitoring borrowers: banks specialize in checking credit risk.

They're regulated through deposit insurance (the FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000) and balance sheet rules: minimum capital requirements, limits on risky lending, and reserve requirements.

Memory trick: "LPCM": Lend Pools Cut Monitoring

Liquidity, Pool risk, Cut borrowing costs, Monitor borrowers. Banks pool and spread risk; they never concentrate it.

Pop quiz · Chapter 8
Assignment 2, Q13: which do depository institutions do? I. long-term loans from short-term deposits, II. gather funds from many depositors, III. concentrate risk
Show answer

I and II. Banks pool (spread) risk; they don't concentrate it.

Remember
  • Banks lend at higher rates than they pay, balancing profit and risk
  • Assets: reserves, liquid assets, securities, loans
  • Benefits: create liquidity, pool risk, lower borrowing and monitoring costs
  • Regulation: deposit insurance, capital and reserve requirements

The Federal Reserve SystemMust knowSlides 21-29

The Federal Reserve System (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. Its key elements:

Element What it is
Board of Governors Seven members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms
12 regional Federal Reserve Banks One in each Federal Reserve district; the New York Fed carries out open market operations
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) The Board plus the president of the New York Fed and four rotating district presidents. Meets about every six weeks to set monetary policy

In practice, the chair of the Board of Governors has the most influence.

The Fed's balance sheet. Its assets are mainly U.S. government securities and loans to banks. Its liabilities are Federal Reserve notes and banks' deposits at the Fed. Those liabilities, plus coins, make up the monetary base.

Monetary base

Monetary base = Federal Reserve notes + coins + banks' deposits at the Fed= currency held by the public + bank reserves

The Fed's policy tools: open market operations, last resort loans (the discount window), the interest on reserves rate and (formerly) required reserve ratios.

Memory trick: FOMC meets "Every Six"

The FOMC sets policy and meets roughly every six weeks. The Board has 7 members on 14-year terms (double 7). There are 12 district banks.

Remember
  • The Fed is the U.S. central bank
  • Board of Governors: 7 members, 14-year terms
  • FOMC sets monetary policy and meets about every six weeks
  • Monetary base = notes + coins + banks' deposits at the Fed

Open market operations and the other toolsMust knowSlides 30-34

An open market operation is the purchase or sale of government securities by the Fed from or to a commercial bank or the public.

Operation Fed's balance sheet Bank's balance sheet Bank reserves and monetary base
Open market purchase Securities +, banks' deposits (reserves) + Securities −, reserves + Increase
Open market sale Securities −, banks' deposits (reserves) − Securities +, reserves − Decrease
Memory trick: Fed Buys → Base Builds

When the Fed buys securities, the monetary base builds up. When it sells, the base shrinks.

How banks create moneyMust knowSlides 35-41

Banks create deposits (money) when they make loans. The loan is spent, and the seller deposits the money in another bank, which can lend part of it again.

  • Desired reserves = desired reserve ratio × deposits.
  • Unplanned (excess) reserves = actual reserves − desired reserves. Banks lend out unplanned reserves.
  • The currency drain: people hold part of their money as currency, so not all of each loan comes back to banks as deposits.

Reserves and the money multiplier

Desired reserves = desired reserve ratio × depositsUnplanned reserves = actual reserves − desired reservesMoney multiplier = change in quantity of money ÷ change in monetary base = (1 + C/D) ÷ (R/D + C/D)
Assignment 2, Q16: deposits $1,500,000, desired reserve ratio 12%, reserves $200,000
Desired reserves = 12% × 1,500,000 = $180,000.
Unplanned reserves = 200,000 − 180,000 = $20,000.

Show answer

10 ÷ 4 = 2.5.

Group work, Problem 5: Nocoin, deposits $300 bn, reserves $15 bn (two-thirds at the central bank), $30 bn of notes held by the public
Monetary base = currency held by the public + bank reserves = 30 + 15 = $45 billion.
Quantity of money = currency + deposits = 30 + 300 = $330 billion.
Desired reserve ratio = 15 ÷ 300 = 5%. Currency drain ratio = 30 ÷ 300 = 10%.
Group work, Problem 20: reserve ratio 10%, currency drain 50% of deposits, monetary base +$1,200 bn

Memory trick: Bigger leaks, smaller multiplier

Reserves (R/D) and currency drain (C/D) are leaks from the lending chain. The bigger the leaks, the smaller the money multiplier.

Remember
  • Banks create money by lending their unplanned reserves
  • Unplanned reserves = actual − desired reserves
  • Money multiplier = ΔM ÷ ΔMB
  • A larger reserve ratio or currency drain makes the multiplier smaller

The demand for moneyMust knowSlides 42-47

The quantity of money people plan to hold depends on four things:

Influence Effect Graph
Nominal interest rate The opportunity cost of holding money; higher rate → hold less Movement along MD
Price level Higher prices → need more money (nominal demand rises in proportion) Shifts MD right
Real GDP More spending → hold more money Shifts MD right
Financial innovation ATMs, cards and online banking let people hold less money Shifts MD left
Trap: Interest rate moves along; the rest shift

A change in the nominal interest rate is a movement along the demand for money curve. Changes in real GDP, the price level or financial innovation shift it.

The supply of money and money market equilibriumMust knowSlides 48-61

The Fed is the most powerful player, so the supply of money depends on what it targets:

Fed targets… Supply of money curve What happens when money demand changes
The monetary base Upward sloping (banks lend more at higher rates) Both the rate and the quantity change
The nominal interest rate Horizontal The quantity of money changes; the rate doesn't
The quantity of money Vertical The rate changes; the quantity doesn't
Memory trick: Target the quantity → Vertical; target the rate → Horizontal

Fix the quantity and the curve stands up (vertical). Fix the rate and the curve lies flat (horizontal).

Equilibrium. The interest rate adjusts until the quantity of money demanded equals the quantity supplied. If the rate is too high, people hold less money than exists, so they buy bonds; bond prices rise and the interest rate falls.

Short run. An increase in the quantity of money lowers the nominal interest rate; a decrease raises it.

Long run. The loanable funds market fixes the real interest rate, and real GDP equals potential GDP. An increase in the quantity of money then just raises the price level in the same proportion: in the long run, money affects only prices.

The quantity theory of moneyMust knowSlides 62-64

The quantity theory of money says that, in the long run, an increase in the quantity of money brings an equal percentage increase in the price level (other things the same). It starts from the equation of exchange:

Equation of exchange and the quantity theory

M × V = P × YV = P × Y ÷ MInflation rate = money growth rate + velocity growth rate − real GDP growth rate

If velocity is constant and real GDP is at potential, faster money growth means equally faster inflation.

Pop quiz · Chapter 8
Assignment 2, Q20: money grows 3%, velocity is constant, real GDP grows 2%. Inflation?
Show answer

3 + 0 − 2 = 1%.

Group work, Problem 8: year 1 real GDP = 350 million, P = 2.2, M = 50 million; in year 2 M is halved
Velocity in year 1 = P × Y ÷ M = 2.2 × 350 ÷ 50 = 15.4.
Year 2: M = 25 million; velocity stays 15.4; real GDP stays at full employment, 350 million.
P = M × V ÷ Y = 25 × 15.4 ÷ 350 = 1.1 (GDP deflator 110): halving money halves the price level.
Memory trick: "MV = PY": "My Vote = Party Yes"

Money × Velocity = Price level × real Y (GDP). In growth rates: inflation = money growth + velocity growth − real GDP growth.

Remember
  • MV = PY; V = PY ÷ M
  • Inflation = money growth + velocity growth − real GDP growth
  • In the long run, more money raises the price level in the same proportion
