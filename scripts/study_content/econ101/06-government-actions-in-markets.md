## Price ceilings and floors | Slides 4-12 | must
| | Rent ceiling (price ceiling) | Minimum wage (price floor) |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | Illegal to charge **more** | Illegal to pay **less** |
| Has an effect when set | **Below** equilibrium | **Above** equilibrium |
| Creates | **Shortage** | **Surplus** of labour: **unemployment** |
| Side effects | More search activity, a black market | More job search |
| Fair? | No, on both views | No, on both views |

::mnemonic Ceilings bind Below, floors bind Above
A ceiling only matters if it's set **below** equilibrium. A floor only matters if it's set **above**. Otherwise it changes nothing.
::
::steps Slide 6: rent ceiling. Demand P = 5,000 − 50Q; supply P = 1,000 + 50Q
Equilibrium: 5,000 − 50Q = 1,000 + 50Q, so Q = **40** thousand apartments at **3,000 SAR**.
Ceiling of 2,500. Quantity demanded: (5,000 − 2,500) ÷ 50 = **50**.
Quantity supplied: (2,500 − 1,000) ÷ 50 = **30**.
Shortage = 50 − 30 = **20 thousand** apartments.
Someone would pay 5,000 − 50(30) = **3,500** for the 30,000th apartment: 2,500 in rent plus 1,000 in search costs, or an illicit rent of 3,500.
::
::steps Slide 10: minimum wage. Demand W = 44 − 0.6L; supply W = 8 + 0.3L
Equilibrium: 44 − 0.6L = 8 + 0.3L, so L = **40** thousand hours at **20 SAR**.
Minimum wage of 26. Demanded: (44 − 26) ÷ 0.6 = **30**. Supplied: (26 − 8) ÷ 0.3 = **60**.
Unemployment = 60 − 30 = **30 thousand hours** a week.
Someone would supply the last hour hired for just 8 + 0.3(30) = **17 SAR**.
::
::tip Deadweight loss in both cases
Less is traded than the efficient quantity: the triangle between demand and supply from the restricted quantity to the equilibrium quantity is lost. For the rent ceiling: ½ × (40 − 30) × (3,500 − 2,500) = 5,000 (thousand SAR), or 5 million SAR a month.
::
::key
- A rent ceiling below equilibrium causes a shortage, search activity and an illicit market
- A minimum wage above equilibrium causes unemployment
- Both cause inefficient underproduction and deadweight loss
- Both are unfair on both fairness views
::

## Taxes | Slides 13-23 | must
A tax drives a **wedge** between what buyers pay and what sellers receive. **It doesn't matter whether the law taxes buyers or sellers: the outcome is the same.**

::steps Slide 14: an 8 SAR tax on energy drinks. Demand P = 30 − 0.06Q; supply P = 6 + 0.02Q
No tax: 30 − 0.06Q = 6 + 0.02Q, so Q = **300** thousand packs at **12 SAR**.
With the tax, buyers' price − sellers' price = 8: (30 − 0.06Q) − (6 + 0.02Q) = 8.
24 − 0.08Q = 8, so Q = **200**.
Buyers pay 30 − 0.06(200) = **18 SAR** (up 6). Sellers receive **10 SAR** (down 2). Buyers bear 6 of the 8.
Tax revenue = 8 × 200 = **1,600** thousand SAR. Deadweight loss = ½ × 8 × (300 − 200) = **400** thousand SAR.
::
::formula The wedge rule
The taxed quantity is where the vertical gap between demand and supply equals the tax
::

Who pays depends on **elasticity**:

| Case (2 SAR tax) | Buyers pay | Sellers receive | Quantity | Tax paid by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfectly inelastic demand | 12 | 10 | 50 | Buyers |
| Perfectly elastic demand | 10 | 8 | 30 | Sellers |
| Perfectly inelastic supply | 10 | 8 | 50 | Sellers |
| Perfectly elastic supply | 12 | 10 | 30 | Buyers |

::mnemonic The less flexible side pays
Whoever **can't escape** pays most. More inelastic demand means buyers pay more. More inelastic supply means sellers pay more.
::
::class Taxes in practice
Taxes usually fall on goods with inelastic demand (tobacco, fuel) or inelastic supply (labour). Two fairness principles: the **benefits principle** (pay for what you use) and the **ability-to-pay principle** (richer people pay more). The handwriting adds that it's easy for the rich, so they're taxed more.
::
::trap No deadweight loss in one case
If demand or supply is **perfectly inelastic**, the quantity doesn't change, so there's no deadweight loss.
::
::key
- A tax is a wedge between buyers' price and sellers' price
- The result is the same whether buyers or sellers are taxed
- The more inelastic side pays more of the tax
- A tax creates deadweight loss unless demand or supply is perfectly inelastic
- Fairness: benefits principle and ability-to-pay principle
::

## Quotas and subsidies | Slides 24-27 | must
Same tomato market: demand P = 54 − Q, supply P = 14 + Q. No intervention: Q = 20 million crates, P = 34.

| | Production quota of 15 | Subsidy of 10 per crate |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity | Falls to **15** | Rises to **25** |
| Market price | Rises to 54 − 15 = **39** | Falls to **29** (new supply P = 4 + Q) |
| Marginal cost | Falls to 14 + 15 = **29** | Rises to 14 + 25 = **39** |
| Result | **Underproduction**: MSB 39 > MSC 29 | **Overproduction**: MSC 39 > MSB 29 |
| Deadweight loss | ½ × 5 × 10 = **25 million** | ½ × 5 × 10 = **25 million** |
| Extra | **Incentive to cheat**: P > MC | Government pays 10 × 25 = **250 million** |

::analogy Same problem, opposite directions
A quota stops the market short of the efficient point; a subsidy pushes it past. Both leave the market off the efficient point, and in this example both waste 25 million.
::
::key
- A quota raises price, lowers marginal cost and causes underproduction
- A quota gives farmers an incentive to cheat
- A subsidy lowers price, raises marginal cost and causes overproduction
- Both create deadweight loss
::

## Markets for illegal goods | Slides 28-32 | know
Free market: demand P = 100 − Q, supply P = 20 + Q → **40** thousand units at **60**. The **cost of breaking the law (CBL)** is 20 per unit.

| Penalty on | Curve shift | Quantity | Market price | Buyer pays | Seller gets |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sellers | S + CBL: P = 40 + Q | 30 | 70 | 70 | 50 |
| Buyers | D − CBL: P = 80 − Q | 30 | 50 | 70 (50 + 20) | 50 |
| Both | Both shift | **20** | 60 | 80 | 40 |

**Legalize and tax:** a 40 SAR tax gives the same 20 thousand units, with buyers paying 80 and sellers receiving 40, and raises **800 thousand SAR** a week in revenue.

::key
- Penalties on sellers shift supply left and raise the market price
- Penalties on buyers shift demand left and lower the market price
- Penalties on both cut quantity the most
- A high enough tax can achieve the same quantity as prohibition
::
@cheat
### Ceilings and floors
- Ceiling binds below equilibrium: shortage
- Floor binds above equilibrium: surplus
- Both: deadweight loss, unfair
### Tax method
- Set (buyers' price − sellers' price) = tax
- Solve for Q, then both prices
- DWL = ½ × tax × ΔQ
### Incidence
- Inelastic demand: buyers pay
- Inelastic supply: sellers pay
- Perfectly inelastic: no DWL
### Quotas and subsidies
- Quota: P up, MC down, underproduction
- Subsidy: P down, MC up, overproduction
### Illegal goods
- Seller penalty: S left, P up
- Buyer penalty: D left, P down
