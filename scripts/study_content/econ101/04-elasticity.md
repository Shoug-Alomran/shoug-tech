## Price elasticity of demand | Slides 4-26 | must
Elasticity is a **units-free** measure of how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price, other things remaining the same.

::formula Price elasticity of demand
ε = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price)
% change = Δ ÷ average value × 100
::

Using the **average** of old and new (the midpoint) means you get the same answer whether the price goes up or down.

::steps Slide 9: pizza price falls from 20.50 to 19.50; quantity rises from 9 to 11
ΔQ = 11 − 9 = 2. Average Q = (9 + 11) / 2 = 10. %ΔQ = 2 / 10 × 100 = **20%**.
ΔP = 1. Average P = (20.50 + 19.50) / 2 = 20. %ΔP = 1 / 20 × 100 = **5%**.
ε = 20 / 5 = **4**.
4 is above 1, so demand is **elastic**.
::
::class How to write the answer
The handwriting: report the elasticity as a positive number (ignore the minus sign), show every step, never round in the middle, and then interpret: "a 1% rise in price lowers quantity demanded by 4%".
::

| ε | Type | Meaning | Curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity doesn't change at all | Vertical |
| Between 0 and 1 | **Inelastic** | Quantity changes by a smaller % than price | Steep |
| 1 | **Unit elastic** | Same % change | |
| Above 1 | **Elastic** | Quantity changes by a larger % than price | Flatter |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic | Any price rise sends quantity to zero | Horizontal |

::example Slide 25: unit elastic
Price falls from 15 to 10 and quantity rises from 20 to 30. Average P = 12.50 and average Q = 25. %ΔQ = 10/25 = 40%, %ΔP = 5/12.50 = 40%. ε = **1**.
::
::trap Slope isn't elasticity
Along a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant, but **elasticity changes**: elastic at high prices, unit elastic at the midpoint, inelastic at low prices.
::
::mnemonic Insulin is Inelastic
The handwriting's example: a diabetic buys insulin whatever the price. **I**nsulin, **i**nelastic. Luxuries with plenty of substitutes are elastic.
::
::key
- Elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price
- Use average values as the base
- Report it as a positive number
- Above 1 elastic, below 1 inelastic, equal to 1 unit elastic
- Elasticity changes along a straight-line demand curve
::

## The total revenue test | Slides 27-33 | must
Total revenue = price × quantity. When price rises, quantity falls, and which effect wins depends on elasticity.

| Demand | Price rises → total revenue | Price falls → total revenue |
|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | **Falls** | **Rises** |
| **Inelastic** | **Rises** | **Falls** |
| Unit elastic | No change | No change |

::mnemonic Inelastic: In the same direction
With **in**elastic demand, total revenue moves **in** the same direction as price. With elastic demand it moves the opposite way.
::
::example Why airlines and cafés care
A café raises the price of coffee by 10% and sells 25% fewer cups: demand is elastic, so revenue falls. A pharmacy raises the price of essential medicine by 10% and sells 2% less: inelastic, so revenue rises.
::
::key
- Elastic: price and total revenue move in opposite directions
- Inelastic: price and total revenue move in the same direction
- Unit elastic: total revenue doesn't change
::

## What makes demand elastic? | Slides 34-40 | must
| Factor | More elastic when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Closeness of substitutes** | Close substitutes exist | One brand of bottled water |
| **Proportion of income spent** | It takes a large share of income | Cars versus salt |
| **Time since the price change** | More time has passed | Petrol: little change at first, more later |

::class Time frames
The handwriting: immediately after a price change demand can be almost perfectly inelastic, because people need time to adjust. The longer the window, the more elastic.
::
::key
- Close substitutes make demand more elastic
- A larger share of income makes demand more elastic
- More time to adjust makes demand more elastic
- Necessities tend to be inelastic, luxuries elastic
::

## Income, cross and supply elasticity | Slides 41-55 | must
| Elasticity | Formula | Sign tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | %ΔQd ÷ %Δincome | Positive: normal good. Above 1: income elastic. Negative: **inferior** good |
| **Cross** | %ΔQd of good A ÷ %ΔP of good B | Positive: **substitutes**. Negative: **complements** |
| **Supply** | %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP | Always positive; larger with more time and easy substitution of resources |

::example Cross elasticity: Pepsi and Coke
Pepsi's price rises 10% and Coke sales rise 5%. Cross elasticity = 5 / 10 = **+0.5**: substitutes. If burgers rose 10% and fries sales fell 4%, the cross elasticity is −0.4: complements.
::
::mnemonic Substitutes Share a Sign
Price of one up, demand for the other up: both **up**, a positive sign, **s**ubstitutes. **C**omplements **c**ontradict: one up, the other down.
::
::tip Supply elasticity and time
**Momentary** supply is often perfectly inelastic, **short-run** supply is inelastic, and **long-run** supply is the most elastic, since firms can change plant size.
::
::key
- Income elasticity positive means normal, negative means inferior
- Cross elasticity positive means substitutes, negative means complements
- Supply elasticity grows with time and resource flexibility
::

### Formula
- ε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP

### Ranges
- 0: perfectly inelastic (vertical)
- Below 1: inelastic
- 1: unit elastic
- Above 1: elastic
- Infinite: perfectly elastic (horizontal)
### Total revenue
- Elastic: P up, TR down
- Inelastic: P up, TR up
- Unit: TR unchanged
### Determinants
- Close substitutes
- Share of income
- Time to adjust
### Other elasticities
- Income +: normal, −: inferior
- Cross +: substitutes, −: complements
- Supply rises with time
