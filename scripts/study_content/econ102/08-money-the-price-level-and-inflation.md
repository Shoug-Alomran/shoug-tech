## What money is | Slides 4-12 | must

**Money** is any commodity or token that is generally acceptable as a **means of payment**, a method of settling a debt. Money has three functions:

| Function               | Meaning                                                                                                | Example                               |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **Medium of exchange** | Generally accepted in exchange for goods and services; avoids barter's **double coincidence of wants** | Paying cash for groceries             |
| **Unit of account**    | An agreed measure for stating prices                                                                   | Every price tag is in riyals          |
| **Store of value**     | Can be held and exchanged later for goods and services                                                 | Keeping $200 at home for an emergency |

::mnemonic "MUS": Money Must Serve
**M**edium of exchange, **U**nit of account, **S**tore of value. "Money **MUS**t serve three ways."
::

**Money today** consists of **currency** (notes and coins held outside banks) and **deposits** at banks and other depository institutions.

::formula Official measures of money
M1 = currency outside banks + demand deposits + other liquid deposits (including savings deposits)
M2 = M1 + small time deposits + retail money market funds
::

::steps Group work, Problem 2: currency $1,824 bn, demand deposits $2,126 bn, other liquid deposits $12,234 bn, small time deposits $466 bn, retail money funds $1,142 bn
M1 = 1,824 + 2,126 + 12,234 = **$16,184 billion**.
M2 = 16,184 + 466 + 1,142 = **$17,792 billion**.
::
::steps Assignment 2, Q12: currency outside banks $200 m, currency in banks $250 m, individuals' demand deposits $300 m, businesses' demand deposits $600 m, other liquid deposits $100 m
M1 = 200 + 300 + 600 + 100 = **$1,200 million**.
The **$250 million held by banks isn't counted**: it's bank reserves, not currency held by the public.
::
::trap Cheques, debit cards and credit cards aren't money
**Deposits** are money. A cheque or debit card is only an instruction to move money between deposits. A **credit card** is an ID card that lets you **borrow**: the loan has to be repaid with money.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q11: which is money as a store of value?
**Keeping $200 on hand for an emergency.** Paying cash or by cheque uses money as a medium of exchange; a credit card isn't money at all.
::
::key

- Money: generally accepted means of payment
- Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value
- M1 = currency outside banks + demand deposits + other liquid deposits
- M2 = M1 + small time deposits + retail money funds
- Currency inside banks isn't part of M1; cheques and credit cards aren't money
::

## Depository institutions | Slides 13-20 | must

A **depository institution** takes deposits from households and firms and makes loans. The main ones are **commercial banks**, thrift institutions and money market funds.

A bank's goal is to maximize its owners' wealth by lending at a higher rate than it pays depositors, but it must balance **profit against the risk** of loans going bad and of running out of cash. It puts depositors' funds into **reserves** (cash in the vault and deposits at the Fed), **liquid assets** (Treasury bills, overnight loans to other banks), **securities** and **loans**.

Depository institutions provide four benefits:

1. **Create liquidity**: they borrow short (deposits) and lend long (loans).
2. **Pool risk**: spreading loans across many borrowers lowers the risk to each depositor.
3. **Lower the cost of borrowing**: one loan from a bank instead of many small loans from individuals.
4. **Lower the cost of monitoring borrowers**: banks specialize in checking credit risk.

They're regulated through **deposit insurance** (the FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000) and **balance sheet rules**: minimum **capital** requirements, limits on risky lending, and **reserve** requirements.

::mnemonic "LPCM": Lend Pools Cut Monitoring
**L**iquidity, **P**ool risk, **C**ut borrowing costs, **M**onitor borrowers. Banks **pool** and **spread** risk; they never **concentrate** it.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q13: which do depository institutions do? I. long-term loans from short-term deposits, II. gather funds from many depositors, III. concentrate risk
**I and II.** Banks **pool** (spread) risk; they don't concentrate it.
::
::key

- Banks lend at higher rates than they pay, balancing profit and risk
- Assets: reserves, liquid assets, securities, loans
- Benefits: create liquidity, pool risk, lower borrowing and monitoring costs
- Regulation: deposit insurance, capital and reserve requirements
::

## The Federal Reserve System | Slides 21-29 | must

The **Federal Reserve System (the Fed)** is the central bank of the United States. Its key elements:

| Element                                  | What it is                                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Board of Governors**                   | Seven members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms                                                         |
| **12 regional Federal Reserve Banks**    | One in each Federal Reserve district; the New York Fed carries out open market operations                                                      |
| **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)** | The Board plus the president of the New York Fed and four rotating district presidents. **Meets about every six weeks** to set monetary policy |

In practice, the **chair of the Board of Governors** has the most influence.

**The Fed's balance sheet.** Its assets are mainly U.S. government securities and loans to banks. Its liabilities are **Federal Reserve notes** and **banks' deposits** at the Fed. Those liabilities, plus coins, make up the **monetary base**.

::formula Monetary base
Monetary base = Federal Reserve notes + coins + banks' deposits at the Fed
= currency held by the public + bank reserves
::

The Fed's **policy tools**: **open market operations**, **last resort loans** (the discount window), the **interest on reserves rate** and (formerly) **required reserve ratios**.

::mnemonic FOMC meets "Every Six"
The **FOMC** sets policy and meets roughly **every six weeks**. The Board has **7** members on **14**-year terms (double 7). There are **12** district banks.
::
::key

- The Fed is the U.S. central bank
- Board of Governors: 7 members, 14-year terms
- FOMC sets monetary policy and meets about every six weeks
- Monetary base = notes + coins + banks' deposits at the Fed
::

## Open market operations and the other tools | Slides 30-34 | must

An **open market operation** is the purchase or sale of government securities by the Fed from or to a commercial bank or the public.

| Operation                | Fed's balance sheet                        | Bank's balance sheet     | Bank reserves and monetary base |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| **Open market purchase** | Securities +, banks' deposits (reserves) + | Securities −, reserves + | **Increase**                    |
| **Open market sale**     | Securities −, banks' deposits (reserves) − | Securities +, reserves − | **Decrease**                    |

::mnemonic Fed Buys → Base Builds
When the Fed **b**uys securities, the monetary **b**ase **b**uilds up. When it sells, the base shrinks.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q15: the Fed buys securities and pays the bank with a cheque. After it clears?
Reserves have **increased** by the amount of the cheque, because the Fed pays by increasing the bank's deposits at the Fed.
::
::steps Group work, Problem 4: the FOMC sells $20 million of securities to a bank
Fed: assets (securities) **−$20 m**; liabilities (banks' deposits at the Fed) **−$20 m**.
Bank: securities **+$20 m**; reserves (deposits at the Fed) **−$20 m**.
The monetary base and bank reserves both **decrease by $20 million**.
::

- **Discount window**: the Fed lends reserves to banks at the **discount rate**, as a lender of last resort.
- **Interest on reserves**: a higher rate paid on banks' reserves makes banks hold more reserves and lend less.

::key

- Open market purchase: reserves and the monetary base increase
- Open market sale: reserves and the monetary base decrease
- Discount window: last-resort loans at the discount rate
- A higher interest on reserves rate discourages lending
::

## How banks create money | Slides 35-41 | must

Banks create deposits (money) when they **make loans**. The loan is spent, and the seller deposits the money in another bank, which can lend part of it again.

- **Desired reserves** = desired reserve ratio × deposits.
- **Unplanned (excess) reserves** = actual reserves − desired reserves. Banks lend out unplanned reserves.
- The **currency drain**: people hold part of their money as currency, so not all of each loan comes back to banks as deposits.

::formula Reserves and the money multiplier
Desired reserves = desired reserve ratio × deposits
Unplanned reserves = actual reserves − desired reserves
Money multiplier = change in quantity of money ÷ change in monetary base = (1 + C/D) ÷ (R/D + C/D)
::

::steps Assignment 2, Q16: deposits $1,500,000, desired reserve ratio 12%, reserves $200,000
Desired reserves = 12% × 1,500,000 = $180,000.
Unplanned reserves = 200,000 − 180,000 = **$20,000**.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q17: the monetary base rises by $4 billion and the quantity of money by $10 billion. Money multiplier?
10 ÷ 4 = **2.5**.
::
::steps Group work, Problem 5: Nocoin, deposits $300 bn, reserves $15 bn (two-thirds at the central bank), $30 bn of notes held by the public
Monetary base = currency held by the public + bank reserves = 30 + 15 = **$45 billion**.
Quantity of money = currency + deposits = 30 + 300 = **$330 billion**.
Desired reserve ratio = 15 ÷ 300 = **5%**. Currency drain ratio = 30 ÷ 300 = **10%**.
::
::steps Group work, Problem 20: reserve ratio 10%, currency drain 50% of deposits, monetary base +$1,200 bn
**a)** Banks have $1,200 bn of unplanned reserves, so first-round lending = **$1,200 bn**.
**b–c)** The loan is held as deposits D plus currency 0.5D: 1.5D = 1,200, so **$800 bn returns as deposits** and **$400 bn is held as currency**.
**d)** The new $800 bn of deposits needs only $80 bn of reserves, leaving $720 bn of unplanned reserves, so banks **lend again** in a second round.
::
::mnemonic Bigger leaks, smaller multiplier
Reserves (R/D) and currency drain (C/D) are **leaks** from the lending chain. The bigger the leaks, the **smaller** the money multiplier.
::
::key

- Banks create money by lending their unplanned reserves
- Unplanned reserves = actual − desired reserves
- Money multiplier = ΔM ÷ ΔMB
- A larger reserve ratio or currency drain makes the multiplier smaller
::

## The demand for money | Slides 42-47 | must

The quantity of money people plan to hold depends on four things:

| Influence                 | Effect                                                                   | Graph                 |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| **Nominal interest rate** | The opportunity cost of holding money; higher rate → hold **less**       | **Movement along** MD |
| **Price level**           | Higher prices → need **more** money (nominal demand rises in proportion) | Shifts MD right       |
| **Real GDP**              | More spending → hold **more** money                                      | Shifts MD right       |
| **Financial innovation**  | ATMs, cards and online banking let people hold **less** money            | Shifts MD **left**    |

::trap Interest rate moves along; the rest shift
A change in the **nominal interest rate** is a **movement along** the demand for money curve. Changes in real GDP, the price level or financial innovation **shift** it.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q18: ATMs and other financial innovation are introduced
The demand for money curve shifts **leftward** (to MD0): people need to hold less money.
::
::key

- Demand for money slopes down against the nominal interest rate
- Higher real GDP or price level shifts it right
- Financial innovation shifts it left
- A change in the interest rate is a movement along the curve
::

## The supply of money and money market equilibrium | Slides 48-61 | must

The Fed is the most powerful player, so the supply of money depends on what it **targets**:

| Fed targets…                  | Supply of money curve                            | What happens when money demand changes          |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **The monetary base**         | Upward sloping (banks lend more at higher rates) | Both the rate and the quantity change           |
| **The nominal interest rate** | **Horizontal**                                   | The quantity of money changes; the rate doesn't |
| **The quantity of money**     | **Vertical**                                     | The rate changes; the quantity doesn't          |

::mnemonic Target the quantity → Vertical; target the rate → Horizontal
Fix the **quantity** and the curve **stands up** (vertical). Fix the **rate** and the curve **lies flat** (horizontal).
::

**Equilibrium.** The interest rate adjusts until the quantity of money demanded equals the quantity supplied. If the rate is too high, people hold less money than exists, so they buy **bonds**; bond prices rise and the interest rate **falls**.

**Short run.** An increase in the quantity of money **lowers** the nominal interest rate; a decrease raises it.

**Long run.** The loanable funds market fixes the real interest rate, and real GDP equals potential GDP. An increase in the quantity of money then just raises the **price level** in the same proportion: in the long run, money affects only prices.

::check Group work, Problem 21: what happens to the nominal interest rate in the short run?
**a) Real GDP increases:** money demand rises, so the rate **rises**. **b) The money supply increases:** the rate **falls**. **c) The price level rises:** money demand rises, so the rate **rises**.
::
::check Assignment 2, Q19: the supply of money curve when the Fed targets the quantity of money
The **vertical** curve.
::
::key

- Targeting the base: upward-sloping supply; the rate: horizontal; the quantity: vertical
- Equilibrium where money demanded = money supplied
- Short run: more money lowers the interest rate
- Long run: more money raises only the price level
::

## The quantity theory of money | Slides 62-64 | must

The **quantity theory of money** says that, in the long run, an increase in the quantity of money brings an **equal percentage increase in the price level** (other things the same). It starts from the **equation of exchange**:

::formula Equation of exchange and the quantity theory
M × V = P × Y
V = P × Y ÷ M
Inflation rate = money growth rate + velocity growth rate − real GDP growth rate
::

If velocity is constant and real GDP is at potential, faster money growth means equally faster inflation.

::check Assignment 2, Q20: money grows 3%, velocity is constant, real GDP grows 2%. Inflation?
3 + 0 − 2 = **1%**.
::
::steps Group work, Problem 8: year 1 real GDP = 350 million, P = 2.2, M = 50 million; in year 2 M is halved
Velocity in year 1 = P × Y ÷ M = 2.2 × 350 ÷ 50 = **15.4**.
Year 2: M = **25 million**; velocity stays **15.4**; real GDP stays at full employment, **350 million**.
P = M × V ÷ Y = 25 × 15.4 ÷ 350 = **1.1** (GDP deflator 110): halving money halves the price level.
::
::mnemonic "MV = PY": "My Vote = Party Yes"
**M**oney × **V**elocity = **P**rice level × real **Y** (GDP). In growth rates: **inflation = money growth + velocity growth − real GDP growth**.
::
::key

- MV = PY; V = PY ÷ M
- Inflation = money growth + velocity growth − real GDP growth
- In the long run, more money raises the price level in the same proportion
::
